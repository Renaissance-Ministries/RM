# 260809 - The Great Commission - Part 3

# Reply to Leonard: Three Questions and the Many Voices


**Christos Fellowship — Renaissance Ministries**


*In response to Leonard’s letter of clarification on the essay of 3 August 2026*


---


Leonard,


Before I answer anything, I want to acknowledge what your letter is. You have gone to the text. You are asking for exegesis — what the words mean, what the grammar carries, what the scope of a warning is. You are arguing from Scripture and asking me to do the same. That is exactly the shared ground I hoped we would stand on together, and it is the ground on which brothers can actually reason rather than merely trade allegiances. So I receive these questions gladly, and I will try to answer each one on its own terms: granting what is true in your reading before testing it, and conceding plainly where you have caught me overreaching — which, on your third question, you have.


I’ll take them in order.


---


## I. Question 1: “Baptizing in the Name” — Water, or Immersion in the Divine Nature?


Your reading is not frivolous, and I want to grant what is true in it before testing it. The phrase “in the name” (*eis to onoma*) does carry the weight you sense in it. In Hebrew thought, the Name is not a label; it is the revealed character, authority, and presence of God. And baptism is certainly *into* something more than water — Paul says we are “baptized into Jesus Christ” and “into his death” (Romans 6:3), and that “by one Spirit are we all baptized into one body” (1 Corinthians 12:13). So the spiritual reality you’re pointing at — immersion into the nature, essence, and character of God — is genuinely present in the doctrine of baptism. The question is whether that reality *replaces* the water or is *signified by* it. I believe the text settles this in four steps.


**First, the word itself.** You’re right that *baptizo* means immerse — but in first-century usage it was the ordinary word for a physical act done with liquid: washing, dipping, plunging. When Jesus spoke the command, His hearers had one overwhelming referent for the word: John’s baptism, in the Jordan, in water, which Jesus Himself had received (Matthew 3:13-16). A command using that word, given to those men, without qualification, would be heard as the act they knew. If Jesus intended a purely metaphorical immersion, He chose a word whose plain meaning to His audience was wet — and He never corrected their understanding. That silence matters, because we can check what they understood. Which brings me to the second step.


**Second, the apostles’ execution is the controlling interpretation** — the same test the essay applied to the Commission’s scope applies to its content. Watch what the commissioned men actually did. At Pentecost, Peter commands, “Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ” — and three thousand “were baptized” that same day (Acts 2:38-41): a discrete, countable, same-day event, not a gradual immersion in divine character. The Ethiopian eunuch sees water and says, “See, here is water; what doth hinder me to be baptized?… and they went down both into the water” (Acts 8:36-38). Peter at Cornelius’s house asks, “Can any man forbid *water*, that these should not be baptized?” (Acts 10:47) — and note the force of that verse for your reading: the Spirit had *already fallen* on them; the immersion in God’s nature had already begun — and Peter’s response was that water was therefore *required*, not rendered unnecessary. If any moment in Acts invited the conclusion “the spiritual baptism has occurred, so the water is superfluous,” it was that one, and Peter drew the opposite conclusion. The men who heard the command with their own ears baptized in water, every recorded time. Either they misunderstood their Lord unanimously and immediately, or water is what He meant.


**Third, the grammar of the phrase.** “Baptizing them *in the name of*” — the Name is the authority and ownership into which the person is baptized, not the medium in which the immersing is done. We use the same construction ourselves: to act “in the name of the king” is to act under his authority, not to be submerged in royalty. The verse gives the medium question no answer because the word *baptizo* had already answered it; what the verse adds is whose act it is and to whom the baptized now belongs — Father, Son, and Holy Ghost. Your instinct that the Name is the substance of the matter is right; but the text’s own structure makes the Name the *authority over* the act, not a substitute for the act.


**Fourth** — and here is where I’d gently turn the question around — notice what kind of interpretive move the metaphorical reading requires, because it is the same move this whole conversation has been about. It requires us to say that the plain meaning of the words, as understood and practiced by the apostles who received them, and by the whole church in every branch and century since — Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and for that matter LDS — conceals a different meaning, invisible for two thousand years, now surfaced. That is not exegesis drawing meaning *out* of the text; it is a reading imported *over* the unanimous witness of the text’s first hearers. And the fruit of the reading, if followed, is the suspension of a dominical command — the second such suspension we have examined in two weeks, the first being “Go ye.” I don’t say this to score a point; I say it because the pattern deserves your attention: twice now, the interpretation on offer relieves the believer of a plain instruction.


Scripture holds both realities together without tension — the sign and the thing signified: “baptism doth also now save us (not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God)” (1 Peter 3:21). Peter affirms in one verse that the water is not the power *and* that the baptism is commanded. The historic faith has never confused the sign with the substance; it has simply declined to discard the sign its Lord commanded.


So the answer to your question is: it is not either/or. The command is water; the meaning is the Name; and we are not authorized to keep the meaning and drop the command.

## II. Question 2: The Four Verses and the Telephone Game


You cite four passages as evidence that the word was corrupted in transmission — that what we hold now stands at the end of a chain like the children’s game, where the phrase that comes out is nothing like the phrase that went in. I want to take each verse seriously and individually, because I believe that examined one at a time, each describes something quite different from what your argument needs it to describe. Each one predicts the arrival of *men* — teachers, claimants, spirits — alongside a word that remains the standard. Let me walk through them.


**Acts 20:29-30 — “fierce wolves will come in among you… men speaking twisted things, to draw away the disciples after them.”**


Notice the mechanism Paul names: the wolves draw disciples *after themselves*. The danger described is not that the text will be altered but that allegiance will be transferred — from the apostolic word to a new teacher’s person. That is a precise description of what a later prophet-figure does: he gathers disciples after himself. Notice also what the warning presupposes: “twisted things” can only be identified as twisted against a straight standard, so the verse assumes the true teaching remains available for comparison. And notice the remedy Paul gives two verses later, in the same farewell: “And now, brethren, I commend you to God, and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up” (Acts 20:32). Facing the wolves, Paul does not say “await a restoration”; he entrusts the flock to the word. The word is the safeguard in this passage, not the casualty.


**2 Corinthians 11:13-15 — “false apostles… disguising themselves as apostles of Christ… Satan disguises himself as an angel of light.”**


This verse describes counterfeit *claimants to apostolic authority* — and the disguise Paul specifies is light. Men will arrive presenting themselves as bearers of illumination beyond what the church possesses. Read plainly, this verse does not support the corruption of Scripture; it warns against exactly the phenomenon of a man offering “more light, more knowledge, more understanding.” And Paul tells us in the same chapter how the counterfeit operates: not by editing documents, but by preaching — “another Jesus, whom we have not preached… another spirit… another gospel” (2 Corinthians 11:4). The corruption enters through new proclamation, not textual decay. I ask you gently: on this verse’s own terms, which is the more natural candidate for the angel-of-light pattern — the manuscript tradition, checked across thousands of copies and continents, or a message arriving through a single man’s private, uncheckable encounters? And let me be equally honest in the other direction: this verse cannot be waved at Denver as a self-certifying accusation, either. What it establishes is that the *category* exists, and it gives the test — the content of the gospel preached, measured against the one already delivered. That is the test this whole exchange is applying, and I submit my own arguments to it as well.


**Galatians 2:4 — “false brothers secretly brought in… that they might bring us into slavery.”**


The context is the Judaizers who infiltrated to add requirements to the gospel of liberty. Two things follow. First, the verse describes infiltration of the *community*, not alteration of the *canon* — and the very next verse records the outcome: “to whom we gave place by subjection, no, not for an hour; that the truth of the gospel might continue with you” (Galatians 2:5). The infiltration was detected and repelled; the truth *continued* — and continued in writing, in the very letter we are now quoting. The verse you cite as evidence of corruption is itself the record of corruption being defeated. Second, this is the same epistle that erects the strongest possible barrier against later revelation: “though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed” (Galatians 1:8). Paul anticipated the angelic-messenger claim by name. A message delivered by an angel, carrying a gospel different from the one already preached, is the specific scenario Galatians rules out in advance.


**1 Timothy 4:1-2 — “in later times some will depart from the faith by devoting themselves to deceitful spirits and teachings of demons.”**


Look at the verb and its object: some will *depart from* the faith. Departure presupposes that “the faith” remains standing, fixed, and identifiable — you cannot depart from something that has dissolved. This matches the pattern everywhere in the pastoral epistles: “Hold fast the form of sound words, which thou hast heard of me” (2 Timothy 1:13), and Jude’s “the faith which was once delivered unto the saints” (Jude 3) — delivered once, as a completed deposit. And look at the *mechanism* of the departure: not scribal error, but “deceitful spirits” — people giving heed to spirit communications and the teachings they carry. Of your four verses, this one is the least serviceable to the corruption argument and the most pointed against it: the predicted failure mode of the last days is *listening to spirits bearing new doctrine*. That is not a description of manuscript transmission. It is a description of ongoing private revelation.


**The pattern across all four, and the telephone game.**


Put the four together and the pattern is uniform: wolves among the flock, false apostles disguised as light, false brothers infiltrating, spirits teaching new doctrine. Every threat is *personal and revelatory*; none is *textual*. Scripture predicts the arrival of counterfeit messengers in abundance — and nowhere predicts its own destruction. Indeed the opposite: “The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever” (Isaiah 40:8); “Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away” (Matthew 24:35). The corruption reading requires these warnings to have survived intact inside a text that the same warnings supposedly failed to protect. The verses’ own preservation is evidence against the thesis they are cited for.


And this is where the telephone-game analogy breaks down as a matter of plain history. The children’s game corrupts the message because it is *serial and oral*: one whisper, one chain, no written record, no checking. The New Testament’s transmission is the opposite on every count: written down within the apostolic generation, then copied in *parallel* across thousands of independent manuscript lines, in multiple languages, across separated regions. An error introduced in Alexandria does not propagate to Antioch; comparing the lines exposes it. We possess several thousand Greek manuscripts, plus early translations, plus quotations in the church fathers extensive enough to reconstruct nearly the whole New Testament independently — and the variants that exist are overwhelmingly spelling and word-order trivia, with no doctrine of the faith resting on any disputed reading. A telephone game with thousands of players writing everything down and checking each other across continents is no longer a telephone game.


And here is the irony I would ask you to sit with: the one transmission the telephone-game analogy *does* accurately describe is a message passing through a single man’s private, uncheckable encounter — one link in the chain, no parallel witnesses, no manuscript trail, no possibility of comparison. The analogy you raised against the Bible is the strongest argument against the alternative.

## III. On the Many Voices and the One


Inside your second question, you raised a point that deserves its own section rather than a passing reply, because it is one of the deepest structural questions in this whole conversation: what difference does it make that the Bible came through many authors over four millennia, while the latter-day revelations — Muhammad’s, Joseph Smith’s, Denver Snuffer’s — each came through one man?


Here is the argument, stated carefully, because I want to claim only what it can bear.


**The design of the canon is polyphonic.** The Bible contains the voices of prophets, historians, psalmists, wise men, lawgivers, apostles, and the Lord Himself — authors separated by centuries, cultures, languages, and stations in life, writing independently, not in collaboration. This is not an accident of history that God worked around; it is God’s own description of His method: “God, who at sundry times and *in divers manners* spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets” (Hebrews 1:1) — many portions, many ways, by design. The unity of the result is therefore evidential: when independent witnesses who could not have coordinated converge on one testimony, the convergence points beyond the witnesses to a single Author behind them. One composer, many instruments. And the plurality does real protective work: no single author’s personality, era, or limitation becomes the lens through which everything must be read. Each voice is checked by every other voice. The narrowness that hardens into dogma when one perspective rules is structurally resisted when forty perspectives must agree.


**God wrote this requirement into His own law.** “At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established” (Deuteronomy 19:15). Jesus reaffirmed it (Matthew 18:16); Paul invoked it (2 Corinthians 13:1). No matter — not a property line, not a capital charge — may be established on one man’s unsupported word. A single-channel revelation claiming to establish the weightiest matters of all, on the testimony of one witness, fails the evidentiary standard God Himself decreed for every lesser matter.


**And here is the crown of the argument: Jesus submitted to it.** “If I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true. There is another that beareth witness of me” (John 5:31-32) — and He proceeds to stack the witnesses: John the Baptist, the works the Father gave Him, the Father Himself, and the Scriptures. The incarnate Son of God declined to rest His claim on His own uncorroborated testimony. Consider also the form in which His words reach us: He wrote nothing. God gave us four independent gospels rather than one authorized autobiography — the multi-witness structure applied even to the words of Christ Himself. If the Lord would not come to us as a single unsupported voice, the man who does is claiming a privilege the Son declined.


**Now, the reply you gave when I raised this before.** You said, in substance: the Book of Mormon is also multivocal — it presents itself as the record of many prophets over a thousand years, Nephi and Alma and Mormon and Moroni, many voices. I want to answer this directly, because it misses where the check actually operates. The protective power of many voices does not lie in the *cast of characters inside the book*; it lies in the *independence of the channels that delivered it*. Isaiah could not edit Moses; Paul never met Ezekiel; John did not review Jeremiah’s scroll. Forty-odd authors, separated by up to fifteen centuries, delivered their portions through separate hands into separate communities — and the convergence of channels that *could not coordinate* is what makes the unity evidence of a single divine Author. But every voice in the Book of Mormon — every prophet, every sermon, every history — reached the world through one throat, in one dictation, in one span of months. A novel can contain a hundred characters and remain the voice of one novelist. When the entire chorus passes through a single channel, the chorus can no longer check the channel; the many voices are not independent witnesses but a single witness’s report of many voices. The multivocal *content* is delivered by a monovocal *source*, and it is the source, not the cast, that the two-witness standard measures. The same holds for any revelator: a single channel, however sincere, has no external check — and that is precisely the condition Deuteronomy 19:15 exists to forbid.


**The Paul question — because you should ask it, and I will not pretend it away.** Is not Paul exactly this: a single man, claiming a private encounter with the risen Christ on the Damascus road, whose personal revelation became a third of the New Testament? If single-source revelation is inherently suspect, why is Paul in the canon? The answer is that Paul was never received as a single source. He submitted his gospel to the Jerusalem apostles — “lest by any means I should run, or had run, in vain” (Galatians 2:2) — and was examined by living eyewitnesses of the ministry and resurrection. His signs were wrought publicly: “Truly the signs of an apostle were wrought among you in all patience, in signs, and wonders, and mighty deeds” (2 Corinthians 12:12). His message was measured against the deposit already delivered, under the very standard he pronounced against himself: “though *we*, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel…” (Galatians 1:8) — the “we” includes Paul. The canon never actually admitted a lone voice; it admitted new voices only after the existing chorus tested them. So the question for any modern claimant is not “did you see the Lord?” — Paul’s own principle is that even a true messenger must submit to the delivered standard — but “to what existing body of apostolic eyewitnesses did you submit your gospel, and against what prior deposit was it verified?” Paul had Jerusalem to answer to. Denver has an audience. The difference is not sincerity; it is structure.


**On liberty.** There is a further consequence, and it touches the freedom we have in Christ. A completed, polyphonic canon gives fixed principles — and then entrusts their application to the believer indwelt by the Spirit: “where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty” (2 Corinthians 3:17). The canon cannot issue you a new directive next Tuesday. A living revelator can — indeed, that is his function. He is a standing possibility of new binding instruction, a perpetual subjection to whatever comes next through the channel. This is the degrees-of-freedom point: the single living channel narrows the space in which conscience operates, because at any moment the oracle may speak and the matter is closed. The closed canon, by contrast, is finished speaking new law — and in that finality, paradoxically, lies the liberty: the principles are fixed, the applications are free, and no man stands between you and them.


**One honest limit, so the argument claims no more than it can bear.** The multivocal canon makes single-perspective dogma structurally *resisted*, not impossible. Churches have built rigid systems on the polyphonic Bible too — and notice what that rigidity is in every such case: the product of *men* imposing a single interpretive voice over the many voices of the text. Which is to say, ecclesiastical dogmatism is the same error as monovocal revelation, arrived at from the other side — one voice ruling the chorus, whether the voice belongs to a magisterium or a revelator. The remedy for both is the same: back to the many voices, each checking each, with no single throat between God and the reader. The claim I make is therefore comparative and structural, and at that strength it holds without exception: the polyphonic canon contains an internal check that the single living channel, by its very architecture, cannot have.

## IV. Question 3: Does Revelation 22:18-19 Close the Whole Canon?


Here I owe you a concession before an argument, because you have caught a real overreach — one that is common in our camp and deserves to be corrected honestly.


**You are right about the verse’s immediate referent.** “The words of the book of this prophecy” means, in the first instance, the Book of Revelation. John wrote those words on a scroll circulating alone, decades before any bound New Testament existed; he cannot have meant a table of contents not yet assembled. The warning follows a pattern from Deuteronomy — “Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish ought from it” (Deuteronomy 4:2; likewise 12:32) — a formula protecting the integrity of a specific covenant document against tampering. So when someone waves Revelation 22:18-19 as if the verse itself declares the canon closed, they are claiming more than the verse says, and you are right to object. I withdraw the verse from that service.


But notice what follows from your own observation — three things, and they run against the conclusion you may want to draw.


**First,** if the warning protects the Book of Revelation specifically, it still protects the Book of Revelation — and by the Deuteronomy pattern, it models how God regards His completed deposits generally. The formula appears at the canon’s opening (Deuteronomy) and at its close (Revelation), bracketing the whole with the same principle: when God has spoken, the words are not to be supplemented or trimmed. That the verse doesn’t *enact* canon-closure doesn’t mean the principle it instantiates is silent about new books; it means the argument for closure must rest on other ground. Which it does.


**Second, the actual ground.** The case that the faith is complete does not hang on Revelation 22; it hangs on texts whose scope is explicit. Jude 3: “earnestly contend for the faith which was *once delivered* unto the saints” — the Greek is *hapax*, once for all, the same word Hebrews uses for the finality of Christ’s sacrifice. Hebrews 1:1-2: “God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath *in these last days spoken unto us by his Son*” — the epistle’s whole argument is that the Son is God’s final and superior word, after which no further mediating revelation is needed, “for there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). And Galatians 1:8, which I keep returning to because it was written precisely for this scenario: “though we, *or an angel from heaven*, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed.” Paul did not say “unless the angel’s message is lengthy, sincere, and delivered on plates.” He anticipated the angelic-messenger claim by name and closed the door in advance. The test he gives is not the messenger’s impressiveness but the message’s agreement with what was *already delivered* — which means the standard is fixed, and anything claiming to stand beside it must first submit to it.


**Third** — and this is the part I’d most like you to weigh — apply the verse *as you have correctly read it*. Its immediate scope is the Book of Revelation: no additions to *this prophecy*, no subtractions from *this prophecy*. Now ask whether the revelations under discussion leave Revelation’s own content standing. The Book of Revelation presents itself as the capstone prophecy of the end of the age, given because “the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy” (Revelation 19:10). A new prophetic system that resituates the last days around a new gathering, a new covenant people, and a new authoritative voice does not merely add a separate book on a separate shelf; it *re-frames the prophecy John sealed*. On your own narrow reading, that is the thing the verse forbids. So the concession costs my argument nothing and yours something: read broadly, the verse warns against adding to Scripture; read narrowly — your way — it warns against exactly the kind of eschatological supplementation a latter-day revelation performs on John’s book.


So my answer to your question is: you are right that the verse’s first meaning is local, and I will not use it as a canon-closure proof text again. The canon-closure case stands on Jude 3, Hebrews 1, 1 Timothy 2:5, and Galatians 1 — texts whose scope is the faith itself, not one scroll. And the verse you have correctly narrowed still stands guard over the one book whose territory every new prophecy must trespass to establish itself.
