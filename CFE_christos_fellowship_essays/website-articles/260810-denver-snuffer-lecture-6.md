# An Academic Assessment of Denver Snuffer’s 500th-Anniversary Lecture 6


## Historical Accuracy and Biblical Consistency


**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 10, 2026**


[**Source:**](https://restorationarchives.com/blog/index.php/2020/06/06/6th-address-to-christians/) Denver Snuffer Jr., sixth lecture of the eight-lecture series delivered on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of the Protestant Reformation. The transcript provided is the lecture body, without introduction or question period. The series and its supporting materials are published at https://www.christianreformation500years.info and at https://www.learnofchrist.org. Readers are encouraged to hear or read the lecture in full. **Method:** As in the assessments of Lectures 1–5, this essay is an academic evaluation only. Each substantive claim is extracted and assessed on two axes: (1) **historical accuracy** against the documented record and (2) **biblical consistency** against the text of Scripture (King James Version), with the subsidiary check introduced in the Lecture 5 assessment retained — where the lecture makes claims about a text (biblical, Book of Mormon, or documentary), does the text say what the lecture reports? Claims are paraphrased; quotation is minimal; no judgment is offered on rhetorical importance or persuasive weight. Claims already assessed in earlier essays are cross-referenced rather than re-argued.


---


## I. The Emmaus Road and the Alleged Lacuna


### 1. The Emmaus retelling


**Claims as delivered.** After appearing to the women at the tomb, the risen Lord walked the better part of the day with two disciples from Jerusalem to Emmaus — about a seven-mile walk; He was known to them in the blessing and breaking of bread; their reflection was “did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures?”; they returned to Jerusalem to announce the appearance.


**Biblical assessment.** Accurate throughout: Emmaus lay “from Jerusalem about threescore furlongs” (Luke 24:13) — sixty stadia, roughly seven miles; the recognition at the breaking of bread (24:30–31), the burning-heart reflection quoted correctly (24:32), and the return that same hour (24:33–35) are as recorded. The all-day duration is a fair inference from “it is toward evening, and the day is far spent” (24:29). No inaccuracy.


### 2. “Not one word about what the Lord said”: the lacuna claim


**Claims as delivered.** Luke tells us nothing about what Christ said when He opened the scriptures — a glaring lacuna, a hole in the record, left by an otherwise exhaustive biographer who preserved intimate details found nowhere else; the omission of Christ’s post-resurrection exposition is unexplained.


**Biblical assessment.** Materially overstated. Luke does not preserve a verbatim exposition, but he twice states its content in summary. Of the road itself: “And **beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself**” (Luke 24:27) — the corpus (Moses and all the prophets), the method (exposition through all the scriptures), and the theme (the things concerning Himself) are all given. And in the same chapter the Lord repeats the exposition to the eleven with its thesis stated: “all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me… Thus it is written, and thus it behoved Christ to suffer, and to rise from the dead the third day” (24:44–46), Luke adding, “then opened he their understanding, that they might understand the scriptures” (24:45). What is missing is the passage-by-passage detail, not “one word” of what was taught. Two further observations. (a) The New Testament then supplies the detail the lecture calls missing: the apostolic preaching of Acts *is* the messianic exposition of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms — Peter expounding Psalm 16 and Psalm 110 of the resurrection (Acts 2:25–36), Philip expounding Isaiah 53 to the eunuch (“began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus,” Acts 8:32–35), Paul in the synagogue “**opening** and alleging, that Christ must needs have suffered, and risen again from the dead” (Acts 17:2–3 — the same verb Luke uses at Emmaus), and the Pauline summary “Christ died for our sins **according to the scriptures**… rose again the third day **according to the scriptures**” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). By the canon’s own economy, the Emmaus content is not lost; it is distributed. (b) The term “lacuna” strictly denotes a physical gap in a manuscript; its extended use for a narrative omission is acceptable, noted only for precision. **Finding:** the no-record claim is contradicted by Luke 24:27 and 44–46, and the felt gap is filled within the New Testament itself — a point that bears directly on §5 below, where the Book of Mormon is offered as the backfill.


### 3. Luke as the unnamed Emmaus disciple


**Claim as delivered.** “I believe that one of the two witnesses was in fact Luke,” who left his own name out while identifying Cleopas, and who as author of Luke–Acts would have been capable of preserving the discourse.


**Assessment.** This repeats the identification assessed at Lecture 5 §6b, with the difference — recorded to the lecture’s credit — that it is here framed as the speaker’s belief rather than asserted as fact. The standing finding is unchanged: Luke’s own preface distinguishes the author from the eyewitnesses, the things believed having been “delivered… unto us” by those “which from the beginning were eyewitnesses” (Luke 1:1–2); an author who was himself one of the two would not describe his knowledge as received from eyewitnesses. The Luke-at-Emmaus identification is a late minority conjecture standing against the Gospel’s self-description; the ancient conjectures name others (Origen mentions Simon), and the identification is unnecessary to the lecture’s argument in any case.

## II. Nephi, John, and the Proposed Backfill


### 4. The 1 Nephi 14 commission and Nephi’s use of Isaiah


**Claims as delivered.** Nephi was shown the sweep of history and forbidden to write the latter part, being told the record was entrusted to John — identifiable as the Book of Revelation; forty years later, Nephi bore his testimony by adopting Isaiah’s text from the brass plates (slightly different from the Masoretic descent), and near the end of his second book (chapter 27 in the LDS versification) shifted from quoting to *paraphrasing* Isaiah, adapting it into a specific prophecy of the Book of Mormon’s coming forth, then supplied his interpretive key.


**Assessment.** As reportage of the Book of Mormon, accurate: the prohibition and the commission of John are as described (1 Nephi 14:18–27); Nephi’s later reflective frame and his large-scale incorporation of Isaiah are as described (2 Nephi 6–24; his stated rationale at 2 Nephi 11:2, 8); and 2 Nephi 26–27 does rework Isaiah 29 into a prophecy of a sealed book coming forth in the last days, with Nephi’s own interpretive commentary following (2 Nephi 28–30). It is recorded as a point of candor that the lecture itself characterizes 2 Nephi 27 as Nephi *paraphrasing and adapting* Isaiah “to make the Isaiah text fit” the Book of Mormon’s coming forth — an accurate description of the compositional relationship between Isaiah 29 and 2 Nephi 27, and one bearing on how the chapter functions as proof when elsewhere offered as Isaiah’s independent prediction. The brass-plates-versus-Masoretic claim is internal to the record and unverifiable externally. No misreportage found.


### 5. The Book of Mormon as the backfill of the Emmaus lacuna


**Claims as delivered.** The Book of Mormon’s account of Christ’s appearance to the Nephites includes Christ opening the scriptures to show how they testify of Him — both as sacrificial lamb and as coming in the last days; Christ’s missing material from Luke is thus back-filled by the Book of Mormon; the sincere Christian, eager to know what the Lord said, will look there.


**Assessment.** The reportage is accurate: in the record, the risen Christ expounds the scriptures to the gathered people — “he expounded all the scriptures unto them” (3 Nephi 23:6, with the Isaiah commendation at 23:1–3, the supplying of Samuel the Lamanite’s omitted prophecy at 23:9–13, the delivery of Malachi 3–4 at 3 Nephi 24–25, and the summary “he did expound all things, even from the beginning” at 26:3). The backfill *inference*, however, carries two prior findings. First, its hermeneutical warrant is the latest-interprets-earliest principle assessed at Lecture 5 §4 and found to equivocate between canonical and chronological priority — the Book of Mormon exposition fills Luke’s gap authoritatively only if the record’s inspiration is first granted, which is the point in question. Second, the gap itself is smaller than claimed (§2 above): Luke states the exposition’s corpus, method, and thesis, and the New Testament’s own preaching then executes that exposition in detail; a Christian reader is not left without the Emmaus content, and the proposed backfill is therefore an addition to a summarized discourse, not the recovery of an otherwise lost one. The two records’ expositions also differ in audience and scope (the Nephite exposition includes matter — the latter-day covenant program — that Luke’s summary does not attribute to the Emmaus discourse), so “backfill” is an analogy rather than a restoration even on the record’s own terms.

## III. Joseph Smith, the King Follett Discourse, and the Neglect of the Book of Mormon


### 6. Joseph Smith’s minimal use of the Book of Mormon


**Claims as delivered.** Joseph Smith made almost no use of the Book of Mormon in his public ministry; his converts — initially predominantly followers of Alexander Campbell — were devoted to the Bible, regarded the Book of Mormon largely as a sign, and wanted teaching supported by the Bible; adapting to his audience, Joseph’s preaching expounded the Bible.


**Historical assessment.** Substantially accurate and supported by the scholarship: studies of Smith’s recorded sermons and the movement’s early preaching show the Bible overwhelmingly predominant and direct Book of Mormon exposition rare; the earliest Ohio converts were indeed drawn heavily from Campbellite (Disciples) congregations through Sidney Rigdon, a former Campbell associate — a biblicist population as described. The claim has, moreover, internal Restoration support the lecture does not cite: the 1832 revelation in the movement’s own canon condemns the church for treating lightly “the Book of Mormon and the former commandments” (D&C 84:54–57) — the neglect thesis attested from within at the movement’s founding generation. Accurate.


### 7. The King Follett discourse and the Rigdon aside


**Claims as delivered.** In the King Follett discourse — a funeral sermon for King Follett, recently killed in a well accident — Joseph Smith, in the middle of teachings on the eternities, made an aside specifically calling Sidney Rigdon by name; Rigdon “was not in the audience at the time. He wasn’t in the city of Nauvoo. He was elsewhere”; the aside was, in substance, “I suppose that the inquiry has to be supported by the Bible,” after which Joseph proved his teaching from the Bible — showing that even those closest to him at the top of the church did not want to hear anything not supported by the Bible.


**Historical assessment against the documentary record.** Checked against the four contemporaneous reports (Willard Richards, Wilford Woodruff, Thomas Bullock, William Clayton) and the Times and Seasons amalgamation, the claim divides into three parts. (a) *The setting is accurate:* the discourse was delivered April 7, 1844, at the Nauvoo general conference, memorializing King Follett, who died March 9, 1844, crushed in a well by a falling tub of rock. (b) *The aside is genuine but its content is conflated.* The named aside in the sources is “**mark it, Elder Rigdon**” (Bullock report; Times and Seasons: “what did Jesus say? (mark it elder Rigdon;)”), attached to the exposition of “as the Father hath power in himself, even so hath the Son power” — a summons to attend to the argument. The Bible-constraint sentence is a *separate* moment in the discourse, addressed not to Rigdon but to the assembly’s critics: “I suppose I am not allowed to go into an investigation of any thing that is not contained in the Bible, and I think there are so many wise men here, who would put me to death for treason; so I shall turn commentator to-day.” The lecture fuses the two — putting the Bible-constraint sentence into the Rigdon aside. (c) *The absence claim is contradicted by the record.* Rigdon was not merely in Nauvoo; he preached at the same conference at length — an address on April 6 (in which he noted that ill health had kept him largely silent for five years) and a continuation **on the morning of April 7**, the very day of the King Follett discourse, from the same stand, on the kingdom of God and Daniel’s stone; the conference minutes and the Woodruff and Bullock records document both. The “mark it, Elder Rigdon” aside reads, in its documentary setting, as an address to a colleague present at the conference, not a message to an absentee. Rigdon’s removal to Pittsburgh came later, in the summer of 1844. **Finding:** the substantive point — that Smith felt constrained to prove his teaching from the Bible to a biblicist audience — is genuinely supported by the discourse’s own “turn commentator” passage; but the aside’s content is conflated from two separate moments, and the Rigdon-absent claim is factually wrong against the conference record.


### 8. David Whitmer’s complaint


**Claims as delivered.** David Whitmer, one of the three witnesses, excommunicated and disassociated from Joseph in 1838, wrote in the late 1870s or early 1880s a pamphlet, *An Address to All Believers in Christ*, complaining that the revelations of Joseph Smith were given too much priority, while himself holding the Book of Mormon in high regard and remaining true to his witness.


**Historical assessment.** Accurate in substance with one date correction: Whitmer’s break and excommunication in 1838 are documented; the pamphlet is genuine and is fairly characterized — it criticizes reliance on Smith’s later revelations, offices, and doctrinal developments while defending the Book of Mormon, and Whitmer reaffirmed his witness to the end of his life (d. 1888). The pamphlet’s publication date is **1887**, not the late 1870s or early 1880s. The inference that Whitmer’s 1887 attitude reflected his views in Smith’s lifetime is the speaker’s assumption, flagged as such in the lecture itself (“I assume…”) and consistent with Whitmer’s own retrospective account, though retrospective.


### 9. The LDS neglect of the Book of Mormon until the 1950s; Nibley and McKay


**Claims as delivered.** The LDS Church gave no serious consideration to the Book of Mormon until the 1950s, when President David O. McKay asked BYU professor Hugh Nibley to write a year’s priesthood manual; in Nibley’s account, McKay was surprised at Nibley’s wish to focus on the Book of Mormon because no one took the book seriously; in 1950 there were church leaders who had never read it; the modern seriousness about the book stems largely from Nibley; the Book of Mormon was published before the LDS Church existed, so accepting it requires loyalty to no institution; a Baptist minister presently believes it; the most accurate edition in print is the independently prepared New Covenants volume.


**Historical assessment.** Layered. (a) The Nibley datum is accurate: Nibley’s *An Approach to the Book of Mormon* was the Melchizedek Priesthood manual for 1957, commissioned under McKay’s administration, and Nibley’s scholarship is conventionally credited with elevating the book’s academic standing among Latter-day Saints; the McKay-surprise detail is Nibley’s own recollection as retold — secondhand anecdote, unverifiable, plausible. (b) The neglect *trend* has genuine scholarly support — analyses of citation in general conference and curriculum show the Book of Mormon comparatively marginal before the mid-twentieth century, with the major institutional emphasis coming later still (the Benson era, 1980s) — and, as noted at §6, the movement’s own 1832 text asserts the neglect from the beginning. The absolute forms (“no serious consideration until the 1950s”; leaders “who had never read” it) overstate a documented trend into a universal, the latter resting on anecdote. (c) The publication-precedes-church datum is accurate: the Book of Mormon issued from the Grandin press in March 1830; the church was organized April 6, 1830. (d) The believing-Baptist-minister claim corresponds to a documented contemporary example (a Missouri Baptist pastor publicly advocating the book) and is fair. (e) “The most accurate edition currently in print” for the movement’s own New Covenants volume is a partisan superlative: the academic reference standard for the text is Royal Skousen’s critical-text work (the Yale edition, 2009, and the underlying Original and Printer’s manuscript transcriptions), on which the movement’s edition itself drew; “an edition prepared with attention to Skousen’s textual scholarship” would be accurate, the superlative is promotional. The description of the volume (New Testament and Book of Mormon bound as joint witnesses) is accurate as description.

## IV. Transmission Claims: the Bible and the Book of Mormon


### 10. The Ehrman material and the baptismal voice, again


**Claims as delivered.** The New Testament texts are copies of copies, demonstrably altered in transmission; Bart Ehrman — a one-time believer, now agnostic — compared the text with quotations in the pre-Nicene fathers and internal evidence and concluded the text deserves great skepticism; the less-altered Hebrews preserves words “drawn right out of the seventh chapter of Proverbs: ‘This is my son; today I have begotten you’ (Hebrews 5:5)”; the Gospels were altered, the baptismal statement changed to “This is my beloved son in whom I am well pleased” (Matthew 3:17) during the Christological debates of the third and fourth century, per Ehrman’s *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*.


**Assessment.** The Ehrman identifications are accurate (the biography, the monograph, the method described in general terms). The specific textual claims fail at three points, two of them already established in this series and one new. (a) *The source of the quotation:* Hebrews 5:5 (“Thou art my Son, to day have I begotten thee”) quotes **Psalm 2:7** — not the seventh chapter of Proverbs, which is the warning against the adulteress and contains no such words. A plain citation error. (b) *The scope of the variant:* as found at Lecture 2 §15, the Psalm 2:7 baptismal-voice variant exists in the manuscript tradition of **Luke 3:22 only** (the Western reading of Codex Bezae and certain fathers); this lecture goes further than Lecture 2 and names **Matthew 3:17** as the altered text — and Matthew 3:17 has no such variant in any known witness; the claim as now stated is false not merely as a majority-versus-minority judgment but as a description of the manuscript evidence. The Lecture 2 findings on mechanism also stand: Psalm 2:7’s application to Christ was never suppressed, being openly preserved at Acts 13:33, Hebrews 1:5, and 5:5 — the very verse the lecture cites — which is inexplicable on the purge hypothesis the lecture advances. (c) *The dating drift:* Lecture 2 placed the controversy in the second and third centuries; this lecture says third and fourth; Ehrman’s own subject is the second and third. Recorded as internal inconsistency. The general skepticism conclusion carries the Lecture 2 finding: the variants are identifiable precisely because the abundant, decentralized manuscript tradition preserves the competing readings — the property the comparative argument of §12 below treats as a defect.


### 11. Margaret Barker and the uncial datum


**Claims as delivered.** Margaret Barker, a Methodist scholar in England, has done for the Old Testament what Ehrman did for the New, demonstrating an earlier, older religion defeated around the Babylonian captivity, with an altered religion emerging at the return; Christians find such information threatening because inerrancy underwrites their assurance. Separately: the New Testament’s Greek was written in capitals only, without punctuation or word division; lower case, punctuation, and word separation were added by monks hundreds of years later.


**Assessment.** The Barker identification is accurate (Methodist local preacher, independent English scholar, the “temple theology” corpus), and it is recorded to the lecture’s credit that the source used *unnamed* in Lecture 4 (§5c there) is here named. The evaluative verb overreaches: Barker’s reconstruction is an influential but contested hypothesis, not a demonstration, and the Lecture 4 finding — a contested thesis presented as settled — applies to “demonstrate” here. The paleographic datum is substantially accurate: the earliest New Testament manuscripts are uncial (majuscule) in scriptio continua with minimal punctuation; minuscule bookhands enter Greek manuscript production in the ninth century, and systematic word division and punctuation are later scribal developments. Accurate as delivered, within rounding.


### 12. The comparative transmission argument


**Claims as delivered.** The Book of Mormon was preserved by a singular transmission through a line of record holders; Mormon abridged the records by command, Moroni finished and buried them; the translation came by the gift and power of God; the original English manuscript survives only in part — about 28% — after water damage in a cornerstone; the printer’s manuscript still exists entire; the translator reviewed the text for the 1837 and 1840 editions; the famous 9,000 changes are located and largely punctuation, versification, and format, with some dropped lines restored; therefore the Book of Mormon is, by an order of magnitude, more faithfully preserved and a more reliable text attesting to Christ than anything transmitted in the Bible, and the Christian insecure about the Bible’s transmission need attach no such insecurity to the Book of Mormon.


**Assessment.** The documentary data for the *printed era* are accurate: the original manuscript was deposited in the Nauvoo House cornerstone (1841), recovered water-damaged (1882), and roughly 28 percent survives — the figure matching Royal Skousen’s critical-text findings; the printer’s manuscript survives essentially complete (barely three lines lacking, so “all” is approximately accurate); Smith oversaw revisions for the 1837 and 1840 editions; and the characterization of the tabulated changes (overwhelmingly punctuation, grammar, versification, and format, with a small class of substantive restorations from the manuscripts) accords with the critical-text scholarship. The comparative conclusion, however, rests on an equivocation between two different transmission segments. The segment for which the Book of Mormon’s record is tight — dictation (1829) to print (1830) to revised editions, some 190 years under documentation — is compared against the Bible’s nineteen centuries of manuscript transmission; like is not compared with like. The segment of the Book of Mormon’s transmission that *corresponds* to the Bible’s manuscript centuries — the claimed thousand-year “singular transmission through a line of record holders,” the abridgment, and the translation — is precisely the segment for which no document is available for collation: the plates are not extant, the source texts cannot be checked, and “singular transmission” means, evidentially, a transmission with **no** surviving witnesses rather than one with many. The Bible’s “muddled” abundance — thousands of witnesses across regions and centuries — is the very property that makes verification, and Ehrman’s own discipline, possible (the Lecture 2 finding); a text with one line of descent and no surviving exemplars is not thereby *more* verified but *un*verifiable at the disputed stage. The accurate statement available to the lecture is narrower: the Book of Mormon’s *English printed text* has an unusually short, well-documented history with a nearly complete manuscript trail. The order-of-magnitude superiority claim converts the absence of checkable transmission into evidence of fidelity, which is the inverse of the evidentiary situation. **Finding:** the print-era data accurate; the comparative conclusion an equivocation that treats unverifiability as reliability.

## V. The Magi and the Global Testimony


### 13. “Two years, according to the New Testament record”


**Claims as delivered.** Wise men some distance away watched for and understood the heavenly signs; “based upon the appearance of the sign, and the journey, and their arrival, it took them two years, according to the New Testament record”; Christ is called a young lad when they present gold, frankincense, and myrrh; warned in a dream — God speaking to them also — they returned, perhaps on a two-year journey; the people on the American continent likewise watched the signs and knew of His birth and death.


**Biblical assessment.** The narrative elements are accurate (Matthew 2:1–12; the gifts, 2:11; the dream warning, 2:12), and “young child” (2:9, 11 — *paidion*) sustains the “young lad” paraphrase. The duration claim, however, has now completed an escalation the series’ assessments have tracked: Lecture 3 said “perhaps as long as two years,” flagged there (§22) as a reasonable inference from Herod’s two-year cutoff (Matthew 2:16, “according to the time which he had diligently enquired of the wise men”); the present lecture asserts “two years, **according to the New Testament record**.” No verse states the journey’s duration; the record supplies only Herod’s inquiry-based cutoff, from which the two-year figure is an inference among others (the cutoff may reflect the star’s first appearance rather than travel time, with a margin added by Herod). An inference has been promoted to a citation. The Book of Mormon reportage (signs watched, birth and death known) is accurate to the record (3 Nephi 1; 8). The Zacharias retelling is accurate (Luke 1:5–20).


### 14. The global scope of God’s testimony


**Claims as delivered.** God’s testimony of Christ at the first coming was not confined to Palestine — the magi’s distance proves a wider proclamation; how many others were there? The Book of Mormon testifies of others on the far side of the world; the purpose of the Book of Mormon includes reminding the terminal generation that His message is global.


**Biblical assessment.** The premise is consistent with the biblical witness: God “left not himself without witness” among the nations (Acts 14:17); “the heavens declare the glory of God… their line is gone out through all the earth” (Psalm 19:1–4, applied to the gospel’s reach at Romans 10:18); the magi themselves stand in the canon as Gentile witnesses from afar (Matthew 2:1–2), and further far-off believers appear (the Ethiopian, Acts 8:27). That God testified beyond Palestine is therefore biblically secure; that the Book of Mormon peoples were among the recipients is the record’s claim about itself, carrying the circularity finding of Lecture 5 (§§3–4) when offered as corroboration. The global-message principle is consistent; the specific instance depends on the record commended.

## VI. The Closing Anathema


### 15. “You are no more Christian than the Jews who crucified the Lord”


**Claims as delivered.** If God has sent a message and testimony of His Son and one dismisses it, what kind of Christian is that? The Jews rejected Jesus not because they had no religion but because their religion did not adequately encompass the truth concerning Him, so they felt comfortable rejecting Him; the Christian who refuses to take the Book of Mormon seriously is no different — no more Christian than the Jews who crucified the Lord, preferring a false, inadequate, partial tradition to a living Redeemer.


**Biblical assessment.** Three findings. (a) *The argument’s logic:* the parallel condemns the hearer only if the Book of Mormon is in fact God’s message — which is the conclusion under commendation; as at Lecture 5 §4, the argument assumes what it must prove, and in this conditional form it is available to every claimant equally (the identical anathema could be — and is — pronounced by other movements over their own books upon Christians who decline them; a test that condemns whoever declines any claimant is no test). (b) *The biblical direction of the burden:* Scripture places the duty of testing on the hearer and the burden of proof on the new claimant, and it *commends* the careful tester — the Bereans “were more noble… in that they… searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so” (Acts 17:11); “prove all things; hold fast that which is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21); “believe not every spirit, but try the spirits” (1 John 4:1); “beloved, believe not every spirit… many false prophets are gone out” (1 John 4:1); and the canonical anathema runs *against the bringer of another gospel*, not against the cautious hearer (Galatians 1:8–9). The lecture’s anathema inverts this: it condemns the tester for testing. (c) *The criterion of Christian standing:* the New Testament’s stated criteria are christological confession and its fruits — “if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved” (Romans 10:9); “every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God” (1 John 4:2); “no man can say that Jesus is the Lord, but by the Holy Ghost” (1 Corinthians 12:3). Nowhere does the New Testament condition Christian identity on the reception of any post-apostolic text. The comparison of a cautious Christian to “the Jews who crucified the Lord” is therefore without biblical warrant on its own canon’s criteria; the historical note is also recorded that crucifixion was a Roman execution carried out under Pilate at the instigation of the Jerusalem leadership (Matthew 27:1–2, 26; Acts 4:27 names Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, and the people of Israel together), so even the comparison’s vehicle is compressed. The adjacent rhetorical question — does God care less about the terminal generation than the first? — rests on a premise that is biblically sound (God’s provision for every generation, Acts 14:17; Matthew 28:20) while the inference (therefore new canonical scripture) is the criterial question the series’ assessments have carried since Lecture 1 §22: continuing care is affirmed; each claimant to embody it is tested by content against the deposit.

## VII. Summary of Findings


Assessed on historical accuracy: accurate or substantially accurate — the Emmaus retelling and distances (§1); the 1 Nephi 14 and 2 Nephi 26–27 reportage, with the lecture’s own candid description of Nephi’s adaptive paraphrase recorded (§4); the 3 Nephi exposition reportage (§5); Joseph Smith’s Bible-centered preaching and the Campbellite convert base, with D&C 84:54–57 as internal support (§6); the King Follett setting and the genuineness of the “mark it, Elder Rigdon” aside (§7a–b); the Whitmer pamphlet’s existence, thesis, and the 1838 break (§8); the Nibley 1957 manual and the neglect trend, the 1830 publication-before-organization datum, and the believing-Baptist example (§9); the Ehrman and Barker identifications and the uncial/scriptio-continua datum (§§10–11); and the Book of Mormon’s print-era manuscript data — the 28 percent original, the essentially complete printer’s manuscript, the 1837/1840 revisions, and the character of the tabulated changes (§12). Inaccurate or materially overstated: “not one word” of the Emmaus discourse recorded, against Luke 24:27 and 44–46 (§2); the Rigdon-absence claim, against the conference record of Rigdon preaching from the same stand the morning of April 7, 1844, and the conflation of the “mark it, Elder Rigdon” aside with the separate turn-commentator passage (§7c); the Whitmer pamphlet dated late 1870s–early 1880s for 1887 (§8); the absolute forms of the neglect claim and the “most accurate edition” superlative against the Skousen critical-text standard (§9); “Proverbs 7” for Psalm 2:7 (§10a); Matthew 3:17 named as the altered baptismal text, a variant existing in no known witness — an escalation beyond even the contested Luke 3:22 claim of Lecture 2 (§10b); Barker’s hypothesis as demonstration (§11); the order-of-magnitude transmission superiority, resting on the equivocation of documented print era with undocumentable plate era (§12); and “two years, according to the New Testament record,” the promotion of Lecture 3’s flagged inference into a citation (§13).
