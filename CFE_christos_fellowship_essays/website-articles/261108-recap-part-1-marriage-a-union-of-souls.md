# What Marriage Is:


### A Union of Souls, a Bond Through Children, and the Limits of Law


At the Sunday Fellowship meeting of 8 November 2026, the conversation opened with marriage. Michael Sherman read a letter he had written to Thomas after the previous week’s discussion, and Leonard Hofheins, Charlie Gutierrez, and Thomas took up the questions it raised: what marriage is at its essence, where children belong in it, and what the law can and cannot do to it.


## Fullness, not value


Michael had come away from the previous meeting believing that Thomas had said a person without children lacks value. Thomas said that was not his point at all. He had been speaking of the fullness of life, not its value. A person who stays in his room his whole life is not worthless, but his life is not as full as it would be if he went outside. Children are one of life’s rooms. Without them, something is missing, and if they were missing for everyone, there would be no next generation and life would end.


Michael accepted the correction and changed the word in his letter from *value* to *fullness*. He then pressed the point from two directions.


First, he tested whether “fullness through children” proves too much. Would a man without children achieve fullness by raping a thirteen-year-old girl and fathering a child she could not abort because the state forbids it? Thomas answered that this was neither his point nor anything he would support. A good end does not sanctify an evil means. The fullness he meant grows inside a union freely given.


Second, Michael’s letter argued that if reproduction measured worth, the bacterium *E. coli* would be worth far more than any human being. He wrote that a different measure of fullness is the joy a person gives others. Addressing Thomas directly, he said that everyone in the group would say Thomas has a deeply full life, and that countless people have grown in heart through his joy in living. What Michael has loved most through their sixty years of friendship is Thomas’s laugh, which comes from his whole self. Some people laugh because it seems appropriate; Thomas laughs because he sees how wonderfully hilarious life is. Michael concluded that personal fullness of life far transcends the number of one’s offspring.


Thomas said he found nothing in the letter to disagree with.


## Two meanings of one word


Michael then separated two things that share the name *marriage*.


The first is the union of souls: two people who love each other fully and thoroughly for a lifetime. This is what he thinks of when he hears the word, and it does not depend on a legal document or a state’s permission. It is two people who simply connect, an intimate intertwining of souls through which both grow beyond measure. He called it one of the most beautiful things he has experienced or seen.


The second is legal marriage, which, with or without ceremony, defines financial and logistical rules. Some of those rules have been good, such as laws against domestic violence that tell a man he may not keep beating his wife. Others have been harmful: widows who could not inherit, wives required to bow to their husbands’ preferences as servants to masters, majorities empowered to impose on minorities, partners barred from visiting a soulmate in the hospital or from inheriting, and religious restrictions written into law. Christians, Muslims, or any other group can restrict marriage in the legal sense.


Michael concluded that the law can affect marriage only at its edges. It cannot detract from the beauty of the connection itself, and marriage is far more beautiful than anything the law could ever impose.


Leonard said his libertarian leanings agree with much of this. Who is the state to insert itself into the relationship between a man and a woman? But in the way of God, Leonard said, God does have a place in a marriage, even in a common-law union where two people simply covenant with each other.


## Is a private covenant enough?


Charlie observed that common-law marriage has disadvantages. It is too easy to get into and out of.


Michael answered from his own life. He and Jenny have been together sixty years, and it has not been in and out. They never formally married. Leonard asked whether they had at least made a pact or exchanged private promises. Michael said that just knowing who they were was enough.


Leonard called that true love, and said that if we could all be that trustworthy to each other, things would be different, because many people are not. Michael replied with a question: What is the point of being untrustworthy? You only hurt yourself. It is like anger or hatred, a weight carried on the soul, and you do not need it. For Michael, trustworthiness is not a question to be settled case by case. It is a way of life.


Michael extended the idea to friendship. He has known Thomas since he was thirteen, and they have never taken an oath or signed a contract, yet they share life’s joys. Charlie remarked that because they have known each other forever, they are perfectly free to tease each other, and that it was entertaining to watch.


Michael recalled one conversation from about sixty years ago, when they rode the school bus together. Michael was a freshman, Thomas a sophomore taking chemistry, and to Michael chemical formulas looked like something out of a science-fiction film. He asked Thomas to tell him something from chemistry. With complete seriousness, as if delivering the answer to the universe, Thomas said: “Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric.” Neither of them could stop laughing, and the phrase has been their private mantra ever since. Chemistry later became central to Michael’s career, but the joke has never stopped being funny. However sophisticated they become, there is humor in it all, and joy in having it.


## The essence of marriage


Thomas said that the group had been trying to determine the essence of marriage, and that what Michael described is inside the set of all things marriage is. He would put children in that set, too. And he added that without the connection of souls Michael described, the bond through children is not stable at all.


Michael disagreed strongly. Picture an abusive parent and a loving parent, he said. Their connection does nothing good for the child. In a wife-beating household, it only teaches the child that this is how marriage works.


Thomas said they had said the same thing in different words, and Leonard agreed that Michael had misunderstood. Thomas explained that what Michael called a celestial union of souls was exactly what he meant: that is the foundation, that is what marriage is. What Michael described with the abusive husband Thomas would not call a marriage. It is a boxing match, or a legal arrangement someone named marriage and then said, *Now go at it.* Thomas said he thinks most marriages are not marriage in this sense. They are closer to a cold war or some other arrangement. The people in them may be striving toward the real thing, but they are not doing it yet. An abusive relationship that happens to include sex, a legal tie, and even children is still not marriage. Marriage is the connection of souls.


## Why children cannot be taken out of the definition


Thomas acknowledged that he had brought marriage and children together in one sentence and connected them strongly, and he explained why.


When children are placed inside a union, even one that is not a marriage of souls, a dynamic follows. As Michael said, the child learns that abuse is what marriage is about. That flawed model bears awful fruit. But because the child exists, the relationship continues regardless of what the parents do. They may go their separate ways and find other partners, yet a bond has been created through the child. It binds two people who may be completely incompatible, who made poor vows and treated each other badly. However well or poorly they live the union of souls, once children are in the mix, the bond will never end, and it is stronger than any vows they made or how they treat each other. Looking at that plain reality, Thomas said, children cannot be eliminated from the equation of marriage. They are intimately connected to it.


## Three relationships, and what the third one teaches


Michael answered by drawing three distinctions. When children enter a family, three separate and intertwined relationships begin: mother and child, father and child, and what the mother and father’s relationship teaches the child.


The first two are one-on-one, and each is different. That is the art of parenting, and Michael said he has enormous respect for it. The third is something more. Parents teach the child not only their wisdom but how people join their lives, how people engage together, and how people love each other. The caliber of the parents’ relationship is the lesson. Mostly, they do not teach it intentionally; they teach it because the child is intuitive and watches how the people raising him relate.


Thomas called this a correct and astute observation and said he agreed completely.


Leonard described living that lesson on purpose. He wanted his daughters to understand that his wife came first, and he has treated her that way since they met. He made it a point never to argue with her, in the sense of raised voices and thrown things, and he says they never have. By his actions, he wanted to show his girls that they could have such a marriage if they chose the right partner.


One daughter chose wisely; a young man Leonard called a great kid. Another married a man who did not respect her, and he was unfaithful after nine years of marriage. She has since chosen a man who gives her far more respect, love, and trust than the first. Leonard said it was good to see that she learned.


## The first marriage was a triangle


Leonard then pointed to the first marriage in creation. It took place in paradise, and God joined the man and the woman. God was part of their relationship, so there were three bonds: man and God, woman and God, and man and woman. Leonard noted that a triangle is the strongest structural element in architecture and design. If two people can have that relationship with each other and also bring God into it, they have a positive, effective formula for raising new generations.


Scripture describes that joining: *“Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh”* **(Genesis 2:24)**.


Even so, Leonard said, everyone had his own agency. Most of Adam and Eve’s children rejected what they were taught and chose to do other things. Some did not, and their story runs all the way down to Noah. If our relationships with our wives, and theirs with us, were like that first one, our lives could be a piece of heaven.


Leonard also observed that there was no government in the Garden of Eden: no overseer and no laws other than what God gave. Whether we take that model and apply it legally, lawfully, or neither, we can find success, joy, love, and happiness.

>
**Note:** The triangle image has a direct scriptural parallel: *“and a threefold cord is not quickly broken”* **(Ecclesiastes 4:12)**.


## Looking outward together


Michael closed this part of the conversation with four notes he had written.


The first was a line he attributed to Saint-Exupéry’s *The Little Prince*, which he related to Adam and Eve: love is not only gazing endlessly into each other’s eyes, but looking outward together. There were two people, and then there was the whole world. Marriage is not only that we combined; it is us looking out at the world.

>
**Note:** This saying comes from Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s *Wind, Sand and Stars* (*Terre des hommes*, 1939), not *The Little Prince*. A common English rendering is: “Love does not consist in gazing at each other but in looking outward together in the same direction.”


The second was a mother’s answer, given long before Michael had a child, when he asked what surprised her most about parenting. She said she had not known children would have so much of their own agenda. She had planned to train her child a certain way, and the child turned out to be someone else entirely.


The third was the moment of his own son’s birth. Michael had imagined the scene of mother, child, and breast. Instead, at the first moment, his son had a look on his face that seemed to say, *This is different from what I was projecting would happen.* A fully functioning person was already there.


The fourth was the legal side. Throughout history, authorities from outside the sacred union of parent and child have imposed restrictions on parenting, childhood, and marriage. Those impositions come from outside the union; they do not create it.

# Was the World Made for God? Freedom, Evil, and the Experience God Desires


At the Sunday Fellowship meeting of 8 November 2026, after the discussion of marriage, Thomas returned to the point he had been making the week before: this world was not made for man; it was made for God. Michael Sherman found the statement hilarious and spent the next half hour testing it. Charlie Gutierrez and Susan Gutierrez joined in after Michael left. This essay follows that exchange.


## The claim


Thomas said that the world is God’s experience of Himself. God did not make it for us; He made it for Himself. But making it for us was integral to making it for Himself, so the two cannot be separated. Nothing in life happens except God’s experience of Himself.


Michael asked him to confirm that he had really said the world was made for God. Thomas said yes. Michael said he hadn’t expected that and was trying to figure out where to begin.

>
**Note:** Scripture states the claim directly: *“for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created”* **(Revelation 4:11)**, and *“all things were created by him, and for him”* **(Colossians 1:16)**.


## Michael’s objection: a novelist writing for an audience of one


Michael began by noting that he sees no evidence that a God is needed for the world to exist. But he granted the premise for argument’s sake, noting that it raises an infinite number of questions.


If the world is for God, Michael said, then God is like a writer creating a story that is His alone to write: “I want some evil. I want some good. I want some people to go to hell. I want some people who will disobey.” Otherwise there is no contrast; if everyone simply sang with harps, there would be no story. So the author adds punishment, pain, and death, a Hitler with concentration camps, a plague. And since God is the only one in the audience, He writes the story He most wants to see: these terrors, these joys, these advances. To Michael, the idea that this could be what everything is for was the funniest he had heard.


Michael added that he appreciates the story with or without an author, the way he watches a movie without caring who wrote it, judging only whether it was well put together.


## Thomas’s reply: love must be free to be love


Thomas said Michael’s version made God sound like a sadist who gets pleasure from suffering, and that this was not what he meant.


God, Thomas said, desires an experience. To have the full experience He desires, all possibilities must be available, and people must actually choose how they will act. He wants to be appreciated, to be in relationship, to be loved.


So Thomas asked: were the people who ran the ovens in the Holocaust getting the fullest joy of life? Were the people thrown into them? Was God living through them? The answer is no. Those outcomes were not desirable, but they were unavoidable for a world that could give God what He really wanted: to be fully loved by everyone.


## Why not write a world where everyone loves Him?


Michael asked why the author would not simply write everyone as loving Him. It is a blank page.


Thomas said that would not be satisfying. It is trivial. It is like setting up toy soldiers and moving them around for a battle, or playing chess against yourself. Did you win or lose? It is not satisfying, because you are only playing with yourself.


Michael restated the point: what satisfies is creating the possibility that someone will not do what you want, and it is not satisfying enough unless someone actually refuses. Thomas said Michael had missed it. God wants people to be free and to choose Him. Toy soldiers aren’t free, and a chessboard isn’t free if you play alone. For love to satisfy, someone truly independent of you has to choose to love you.


Thomas put it in terms of Michael’s partner of sixty years. If Jenny were a Stepford wife, programmed to love him, would that be as satisfying as the real Jenny, who could walk out any day? Michael answered at once that a Stepford Jenny would not be her; it would be the opposite of who she is. Thomas agreed. The real Jenny is satisfying. A programmed Jenny would leave a man thinking, *This person loves me only because she cannot do anything else.*


## “So God needs someone to say no”


Michael pressed further. It sounded to him as if God needs somebody with the power to say no, or He is not satisfied. Even a script in which everyone voluntarily chooses Him would still be boring: a waste of a film. So He creates people who will say no, because the script requires it. “There. He said no. And now what do I get to do with him? Cause him unimaginable pain. Now we’re talking.” Is that the game? Michael asked.


Thomas said the game he was describing has real consequences and real free will, and it satisfies the people who chose it. It also has to be random. You do not know the story or even what the game is. You only know that you are in it to survive, and you try to get the best out of it.


Michael restated the stages as he heard them: God does not want everyone bowing down, which is boring, so He needs someone with free will who will refuse, and then the punishments begin. Thomas said, “Not really.”


## The antagonist


Thomas explained that God wrote the story with an antagonist, and the antagonist takes joy in everything God hates. He loves the fire, the pain, the death, the murder, and mayhem. And he wants to pull as many people as possible into loving his game and enjoying his world of death and blood the way God enjoys His. Thomas compared it to the short cartoon *Bambi Meets Godzilla*.


Good and evil both have to be present, Thomas said: there must be a good guy and the bad guy, a beauty and the Jaws theme, or it is not a four-star movie. Then everyone is put into this world in different situations. One child grows up with loving, wonderful parents and goes out to do the same. Another grows up with parents at each other’s throats and goes out to do that in his own life. Two people go down two different roads.


God wants everyone to follow the good path. But some choose the bad path, because, as anyone who has lived knows, there is some satisfaction in watching death, mayhem, and destruction. If we were completely repulsed by it, we would not go to movies that contain it. We would watch only Bambi, and Godzilla would never show up. The fact is that we are drawn to both sides.


So we can be seduced by evil, and God says, *That is not My plan. I want you to follow My ways and resist the evil.* The joy of the play is in the overcoming. Nobody had to follow Satan. Those who did got the fruit of it; they learned to like that life and trained their children to go out and be gangsters too. There are two paths, but in the long run you can change the road you’re on.


## “Good guys and bad guys” and the justification of killing


Michael answered that good guys versus bad guys is the plot of most action films and cowboy films, black hats and white hats. It runs through history: Romans against those trying to end their empire, the British against the American rebels. Most people in literature, he said, consider that comic-book stuff. It is also what Hitler said: Jews bad, Germans good. Stalin said Germans bad, Russians good. Protestants good, Catholics bad, or the other way around, and the zillion other Christian fights that have killed so many. Christians good, Muslims bad, or Muslims good, Christians bad. We kill each other because we hold a concept of good that means the other is bad, and it makes us feel powerful when we kill.


Real literature is different, Michael said. Shakespeare is not about good and bad, and it has lasted five hundred years; it is the most printed literature besides the Bible. Literature and history get into the complexity: both sides feel they are good. Catholics and Protestants, Eastern Orthodox and Catholics for centuries, each knows it has the truth, and each kills the other believing it has done God’s will. And he returned to the child: a child is not bad because he did something bad. You modify the behavior so he knows not to do it.


Thomas gave two answers.


First, he said there is more to the play than good guys and bad guys. There is an element of randomness. You do not know whether you will be born a Roman or a Hun, and whichever side you are born on, you will call it the good side. Within that randomness, God defined joy, peace, and love as good archetypes, and their opposites as evil. He created spirits that tempt people toward both. People in the physical world hear those spirits without knowing they are hearing them, and their environment tempts them one way or the other. Then they form the tribe of good and the tribe of evil, and the play goes on.


Second, he said that believing something is true does not make it true, or make it God’s will. God’s standard is still God’s standard. God defined good, and God defined evil. Michael asked whether, then, the right version of Christianity determines whose killing of the other is virtuous. Thomas said no: God determines who is virtuous. Neither side does; each may have its own ideas, but self-determination doesn’t decide it. Michael answered that God, then, had set things up so that two factions of Christianity would kill each other by the millions. Good plan.


Thomas replied that biblical revelation reveals God’s standard of good and evil. When two people are trying to kill each other, the reasonable thing is to come together, look at the revelation, and be honestly reflective: *Is this true? Am I good? Am I evil?* Short of an actual revelation inside ourselves, the words of the Bible are the closest record we have of God’s standard. And Thomas said that anyone who looks there will have a hard time defending Christians killing Christians.


Michael’s parting reply, before he left for a student, was that every Nazi wore the words *God is with us* on his belt.

>
**Note:** The German army’s belt buckles bore *Gott mit uns* (“God with us”); the SS buckle carried a different motto. On the gap between claiming God and obeying Him: *“Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven”* **(Matthew 7:21)**.


## The players and the stage


After Michael left, Thomas asked Charlie what he thought. Charlie said his head was spinning; it had been a very entertaining discourse on marriage.


Thomas said the conversation always comes down to the same questions: What is life? What is its purpose? What story did God intend us to play out, given that He set up every possibility of the plot and we then took the screenwriter’s role? Charlie added that one of the plot’s big problems is the players trying to figure out whether it is real. Are we really on stage? Are we on candid camera? What is this?


Thomas said Michael found the idea that the play was for God the most ridiculous thing in the whole meeting. It was the comedy line that got the biggest laugh. Yet in Thomas’s view it was the most important point of the discussion, and it received the most ridicule. He described two people looking at life, one conventionally and one not, each believing his way is correct. Thomas thinks that almost everyone agrees with Michael, and that only a few see life as he does, because the truth has been hidden so well.


## Susan’s alternative framing


Susan Gutierrez said she sees much Scripture indicating that we are in God, and that Thomas’s idea could be correct. But she thinks that saying we live for God’s pleasure leads people, as it led Michael, down the wrong path, toward a God who enjoys our suffering. She said she knows that is not what Thomas means, nor does he mean that we are here merely to entertain God.


She prefers to focus on this: we are here to learn to obey God. We can learn it by obeying Him and having a relationship with Him, with meaning, purpose, and joy. Or we can learn it by disobeying Him, reaching some miserable crisis, and coming to the point where we definitely do not want to live that way. Then we notice someone who is obeying God and think, *I want to try that.* One way or another, people are learning that they want to obey God.


In her framing, God wants to live with us forever, but we must obey Him, and we learn that here on earth. Some are devout from childhood, and some are not, and none of us will be perfect, because we live in a fallen world. His commandments are designed to create love, happiness, and right action among people, and that is what He wants to see.


She would present it this way, rather than as God having a good experience: God wants to live with us forever. If we are to live with Him forever with any kind of freedom, we must have freedom now, and freedom opens the possibility of murder, mayhem, and all the rest. But to those who love Him, He gives His Spirit, His guidance, His love, and His blessings, so that we can endure the wrong in this world. Even if our head is cut off, God has the power to raise us again, so the killer does not have the last word. God does. This shifts the focus from whether God enjoys terrible things to what He has done so we can live with Him forever. She acknowledged that Thomas may be exactly right in how he thinks of it. She simply does not think of it that way herself. Thomas said, “No one does.”


## Why Thomas keeps returning to his framing


Thomas said he keeps returning to his perspective because it demands the existence of God.


Susan asked how hers does not. Thomas answered that her framing places God over there and man over here, and the two try to find each other, and if they do, it is beautiful. He recalled a poster his first girlfriend gave him, with a line from Fritz Perls: *I am me, and you are you, and if we find each other, it’s beautiful.* In that picture, man can be conceived without God; God is a partner he may or may not meet. In Thomas’s picture, no man exists apart from God, because nothing happens except God’s experience of Himself.

>
**Note:** Fritz Perls (1893–1970) was a psychiatrist and the founder of Gestalt therapy. The line comes from his “Gestalt Prayer” (1969), which ends: “I am I, and you are you, and if by chance we find each other, it’s beautiful. If not, it can’t be helped.”

# In Him We Live: Being, Choosing, and the Contradictions of Scripture


At the Sunday Fellowship meeting of 8 November 2026, Thomas, Leonard Hofheins, and Susan Gutierrez turned from the question of whether the world was made for God to a closely related one. If we live and move and have our being in God, how can anyone be separated from Him? That question led to two others: what a “personal relationship” with God means, and how a reader should handle apparent contradictions in the Bible.


## God is the boat and God is the boatman


Thomas said the idea that God is the entirety of the play is rare because it has been so well hidden. He recalled a song he used to sing with the Sikhs: *God is the boat and God is the boatman, and by His grace we’ll cross this ocean*, like the sun, like the rain that falls the same on everyone. That God is both the boat and the boatman is hard to wrap the mind around. Thomas said he doesn’t see daily life that way, and he doesn’t know why anyone else would. But he can go to that place and see God as the boat and as the boatman, though he has to switch places to look from each side. Having examined the other possibilities, he sees no other way it could be, and he believes Scripture confirms it. He noted that Susan has found places where Scripture indicates it.


Thomas also said that Michael’s laughter earlier in the meeting was the normal response, and probably the appropriate one. He believes his own perspective is true, but he does not think anyone was supposed to see it easily. It has been hidden very well.


Later in the meeting, Susan described her own path to the idea. When Thomas first said it, she thought it looked ridiculous. Then, as she read Scripture, she kept finding verses consistent with it, one after another, until she concluded that it looks as if it is so, even though she cannot grasp how. She put it this way: somehow all of this is created by Christ’s thoughts, and Christ came forth from God’s thoughts, and trying to picture it is like entering the Twilight Zone. Thomas agreed that it is absolutely the Twilight Zone.


## Leonard: “extra-scriptural”


Leonard said much of what the group was discussing was not scriptural at all, and that Thomas’s concept of God, in particular, is extra-scriptural. Nothing like it appears in the Bible.


Thomas said he both disagreed and agreed. He could see Leonard’s side, because many passages do not say what he says. But there are others that do.


## The verse and the contradiction


Thomas identified the passage at the center of the question, the one Susan had pointed to: *“That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: For in him we live, and move, and have our being; as certain also of your own poets have said, For we are also his offspring”* **(Acts 17:27–28)**.


That verse, Thomas said, says we are actually one with God. Yet other verses say that if we do evil, we are put out of His presence and separated from Him. How can both be true? If our very essence is in Him, how can we ever be separated from it?

>
**Note:** For the separation side, see *“But your iniquities have separated between you and your God”* **(Isaiah 59:2)**. Paul makes the universal claim elsewhere: *“One God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in you all”* **(Ephesians 4:6)**.


## Leonard’s answer: agency


Leonard answered at once: because you choose to. Agency is number one.


God makes it rain on the wicked and the righteous. He created us all and gave us our spirits, and we have the energy in these bodies to move, act, and be agents unto ourselves. But when you make a decision that is not aligned with God’s way of truth and light, you have transgressed. You have sinned. That is why He wants us to change and repent and return to Him. That is the whole concept of the gospel, and it is why Christ came, suffered, died, and was resurrected.


Our agency, Leonard said, gives us the chance to learn the difference between God’s way and our own way, or Satan’s way, or someone else’s. When we sin against the Spirit given to all of us, we must repent. The point of this life is to learn, grow, and become like Him: *“Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect”* **(Matthew 5:48)**. We can be perfect only through His Son. It is grace, after all we can do, but we have to do something.


Thomas said they were talking about two different categories: ontology on one side and decisions on the other. Being in God concerns what we are. Separation concerns what we choose.


## Is a “personal relationship” with God scriptural?


Leonard turned to a podcast he had heard that morning about what the Latter-day Saint church is teaching its youth. The program had folded the idea of a personal relationship with Christ into its teaching. Leonard pointed out that Scripture never states a personal relationship with Christ. An individual relationship, yes, but “personal” is something different.


He traced the phrase to the era after World War II, to Billy Graham and televised evangelism, with its call to develop a personal relationship with God. What has happened since, he said, is that many people treat God as a therapist: you go to Him, and He makes you feel good. Leonard considers that erroneous. God does not want you to feel good about what you are doing. He wants you to feel good in changing, in becoming more like Him. That is why Christ came and taught as He did. He set the example of a perfect man, doing everything according to His Father’s will.


Leonard then addressed Thomas’s view directly. The idea that God created us to entertain Himself, or to have an experience, does not sound right to him. It sounds hedonistic, a deity who looks down on us, smiles, laughs, and is entertained, having an experience He has never had before. That is where their beliefs differ.


Thomas said he did not even disagree with Leonard. He simply felt misunderstood; the way Leonard framed it was not his concept. He said that explaining it is very difficult, but he would try again.


First, Susan answered Leonard about the personal relationship. She believes many people have a personal relationship with God that has nothing to do with feeling good. They are being chastised. They come to understand more and more what their sin is and learn to depart from it. It is not an easy walk, because you are at war with yourself while you try to follow the Lord. That, she said, is her own experience. She acknowledged that Leonard probably knows people whose “personal relationship” is the therapeutic kind he described, and that such people exist too.

>
**Note:** The phrase is indeed not in Scripture, but the reality it names is: *“Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me”* **(Revelation 3:20)**; *“I have called you friends”* **(John 15:15)**; *“And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent”* **(John 17:3)**.


## Two mints in one


Thomas said that listening to everyone’s viewpoints reminded him of an old television commercial for Certs: one person insists it is a breath mint, the other that it is a candy mint, and the answer is that it is two mints in one. Leonard laughed and agreed.


It is all of these things, Thomas said. God watching. God living inside the play. God as a counselor. God with you. God standing at the door and knocking, and coming in to sup with us if we open, the verse Charlie often cites. Every one of these perspectives is true. God is the boat, and God is the boatman. Which one is He? Not one of them. All of them.


So Thomas told Leonard that he could see his point; it was the most reasonable criticism of his perspective and well grounded. But there is another way of looking at it, in which God is entirely experiencing Thomas, and Thomas is God experiencing the world. God is trying to help the world understand through the words Thomas is speaking, and is frustrated because nobody gets it. And the people listening, who say, *“That’s not right; it actually looks like this,”* are also God, experiencing life from that point of view and wanting the world to see it their way. And God is outside, watching both.


## The Father, the Son, and the gate


Thomas continued. God made the side that was perfect and good, and He made the side of murder and mayhem. Then God the Father, as Thomas frames Him, gave the whole work of personification to Christ and said, in effect, *You will be the one who makes all of that manifest, because I will embody only perfection: truth, love, joy, peace, all the fruits of the Spirit.* Nothing impure, nothing outside His definition of perfection, gets through the gate to Him. Christ is the gatekeeper to that experience of the Father’s perfection.


Susan asked whether Thomas meant that God the Father is not the one watching all the sin, that His view of it is shielded. Thomas said yes; he thinks the Father shields it. Susan said that is what she thinks as well: the Son sees it.

>
**Note:** *“I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved”* **(John 10:9)**; *“Thou art of purer eyes than to behold evil, and canst not look on iniquity”* **(Habakkuk 1:13)**.


## Susan’s reading of Acts 17


Susan then read the verse in its context, beginning at verse 27: *that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after Him and find Him.* Because Paul begins with seeking, she thinks *“for in him we live, and move, and have our being” applies to those who choose* to be in Him. Thomas said she reads it as the state of every person.


Thomas said it is a state of being, and it is unavoidable. But he said Susan’s point of view is also correct, which led back to Leonard’s question: what do we do with contradictions?


## Two ways to reconcile contradictions


Thomas described his method. Stand in one position, where two statements look contradictory. Then move to another position, where they turn out to be parts of one set: this thing, and this thing, and this thing, each sitting separately, not opposed at all. You look at the same scenario with different criteria. That is how you resolve contradictions, he said. Otherwise the Bible is nonsensical, because on its face it contradicts itself.


Susan described her own method by comparing it to Charlie’s experience with a vision therapist. Charlie’s eyes did not line up, and once the suppression was broken, he had to do intense, exhausting exercises every day to bring them into alignment. Her study of Scripture is like that, though without machines. When she sees a contradiction, she sets this verse beside that verse, and beside others across the whole Bible, and struggles with whether they can be reconciled. She does not assume that they can. Scripture says, *“Prove all things; hold fast that which is good”* **(1 Thessalonians 5:21)**, and that is her attitude. Through this process, she has come to see that many passages that look contradictory on the surface could both be true. But she works thing by thing. She does not apply a blanket rule that everything can be reconciled.


Thomas said he does. If the Bible is true, every word can be reconciled, and he has taken that as his foundational assumption. If the Bible is true, there are no logical contradictions. A thing cannot be both A and not-A. So when he seems to see A and not-A, he steps aside and finds a B behind the A that he had not seen. If you see only the A, the passage looks impossible. If you see an A and a B, both can be true.


Susan said she does not know much about inductive and deductive reasoning, but she thinks that is the difference between them, though she does not know which is which. Thomas starts from the assumption that every word is true. She does not. She sees an astonishing amount of truth in the Bible, but she puts the Bible itself in the category of “prove all things.” Every word still has to be proved.


Thomas said he does that too. He takes nothing at face value.


## Proof-texting is not reconciling


Thomas illustrated what reconciling is not with an old joke he and a friend once enacted. Looking for guidance about what to do in life, they opened the Bible at random and read that Judas *“went and hanged himself”* **(Matthew 27:5)**. That was not a helpful prophecy, so they tried again and opened to *“Go, and do thou likewise”* **(Luke 10:37)**.


The joke is absurd on purpose, Thomas said. Two verses that are each true are not therefore connected. That kind of proof-texting is nonsense.


He turned to a harder case, one Charlie likes to raise. God commanded Israel to wipe out certain peoples entirely, men, women, children, and even the animals, and to have no part in anything they touched. Israel did not fully obey. They kept the women, thinking the women were wonderful, and the women brought them into bondage. So are we supposed to go out and kill everyone who is our enemy? No. Jesus said, *“Go, and sin no more”* **(John 8:11)**. Anyone can change his heart and mind. Thomas said he does not see anyone as unsavable.


But he does read that story as teaching something. God recognized that the evil in those people was so great that it would pull His people into practicing it, so He said to destroy them and have nothing to do with them. That is our stance toward evil: we do not take just a little of it. We do not do evil at all. That is the level of purity we are supposed to live. Thomas said he thinks we are incapable of it, because life is too complex to make it that pure.

>
**Note:** The account of Israel keeping the Midianite women, and Moses’ rebuke that these women had led Israel into sin at Peor, is in **(Numbers 31:15–16)**; the earlier event is **(Numbers 25:1–3)**. The command against intermarriage with the seven nations, the Girgashites among them, gives the same reason: *“For they will turn away thy son from following me, that they may serve other gods”* **(Deuteronomy 7:4)**.


## Can anyone live that purely?


Susan answered that Jesus came into a world full of evil and lived a sinless life. We can’t do it on our own, but He can lead us if we really put down our will and follow His. That is a huge challenge, she said, but she is not ready to call it impossible.


Thomas said he does not know that it is impossible either. Jesus could do it because He was in complete communion with the Father, and we are not. To say that if we fully had Jesus in us we could do it is really to restate what Jesus did.


Susan said that, at least in theory, it should be possible for someone to put down his own will, accept Christ’s will and guidance, learn to walk in Christ as Scripture says, and reach the point of living without sin.


Thomas said the problem with that theory is the one he had raised with Michael: randomness. There are too many situations we have never met before, and we do not know what to do. Without perfect communion with the Father, or, in Susan’s terms, with the Son, when something comes that we did not see coming, we are liable to be naive, like children. He also recalled the idea that life is for learning. If we really had it all down, simply listening to what the Holy Spirit says, we would only be following something we already knew, through a guide we never misread. The question is whether the channel can be cleansed so completely that we literally hear the Lord speak and know in every situation whether to turn right, left, or go straight.


Perhaps, Thomas said, that is what Elijah did. He was so perfectly in the Spirit that he had no further purpose on earth; he was already fully present with the Lord. Susan suggested that Elijah’s remaining purpose on earth was for other people, and that he too had to progress, but that he must have reached the point of living perfectly right. Thomas agreed. He would not have been able to go to the Father otherwise, since *“the wages of sin is death”* **(Romans 6:23)**. Having lived God, renounced his mistakes, and been forgiven, Elijah was cleansed and could walk to the Father without going through the door of death.


Susan said that Elijah’s story is a good reason to think it is actually possible. Thomas agreed: it has been done, so it is possible. He places it in the realm of the unlikely, given how many situations catch us unprepared, and the only way around that he can see is a full, unobstructed view, with no dirt on the lens, clear all the way to the Father. Susan added that even if our own minds do not understand everything, we have a God who does. Thomas added that we must be committed to following the Father’s leading.

>
**Note:** Elijah’s ascent is recorded in **(2 Kings 2:11)**. Scripture records one other person who did not die: *“By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death”* **(Hebrews 11:5)**; see also **(Genesis 5:24)**.


## Elijahs in training


Thomas said he thinks the best we can do now, as Elijahs in training, is to ask in every situation: what is my best understanding of God’s way in this moment? Then go with it, never trying to get around it, never reasoning that we can grab this earthly goodie and it will be all right. If we are not trying to finagle, if we are literally trying to do God’s will at every moment, that is good.


Susan added one thing. Thomas was describing a thinking process, and she would add that we do that while always begging God to lead us. If it is only a thinking process, done by our own thinking, she thinks we will fail.


Thomas agreed. Thinking is where the thousand branch points are: maybe this way, maybe that way. The result is confusion, which is not the same as *this is His will.* What Susan described is knowing His will and walking in peace in it. Susan said that even when we do not know His will all the time, we can have the kind of relationship in which we recognize, *I am supposed to do this. I don’t understand why, but He is clearly guiding me to do it.* She believes that level of relationship is possible, though she said she does not have it yet.

# Will All Be Reconciled? Universal Salvation, Degrees of Glory, and the Fate of Satan


At the Sunday Fellowship meeting of 8 November 2026, Susan Gutierrez raised the question of whether everyone will finally be saved. Thomas shared her hope while naming the scriptural case against it. Leonard Hofheins explained the Three Degrees of Glory taught in his Covenant Christian community, which shares Joseph Smith’s revelations. The discussion ended with the question of whether Satan himself could ever return.


## A rational heresy


Susan said she feels God has designed things in such a way that ultimately everyone will find Him. Thomas said he agrees, and added that ultimate reconciliation is completely heretical. Susan said only a very small minority of Christians believe it, but that it is so rational it has been persuading her.


Thomas agreed that it is rational, and said it also goes against much of the New Testament: the judgment that comes after death, and Jesus’ words, *“I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me”* **(John 14:6)**. There are many scriptures that counteract it, he said; it is not a minor theme.


Susan took that very verse as her example. She believes it: no one comes to the Father except through Christ. She does not think it contradicts the idea that everyone will eventually come. Thomas restated her position: everyone will eventually go through Christ. Susan said that is the only way. It has to be by choice, but Christ will be the only way to the Father. She believes that anyone who does not choose Christ now would choose Him if he really saw the reality.


Leonard interjected, “If they choose to,” and said he was not sure about that.

>
**Note:** The texts most often cited on each side: for universal reconciliation, *“For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive”* **(1 Corinthians 15:22)**, *“Who will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth”* **(1 Timothy 2:4)**, and *“by him to reconcile all things unto himself”* **(Colossians 1:20)**; against it, *“And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment”* **(Hebrews 9:27)** and *“And these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal”* **(Matthew 25:46)**.


## Leonard on the Three Degrees of Glory


Later in the meeting Susan returned to the subject. Universalists are a small minority, she said, but Latter-day Saint theology includes something much like universalism, the Three Degrees of Glory. She asked Leonard whether that teaching is part of Covenant Christian theology, and how he would explain it.


Leonard began with the meaning of salvation. Scripture speaks of being damned, and *damned* means stopped, blocked. Eternity is a very long time. Susan said it is so long it is not a time, and Leonard agreed: there is no time in eternity.


But Leonard was cautious about leaning on that. It resembles the evolutionists’ argument that if you give it enough time, a monkey can compose an opera. That is not what the revelation teaches.


Joseph Smith received a vision of three degrees of glory. The celestial is the highest, where God lives. The terrestrial is Christ’s realm. The telestial is the realm of the Holy Ghost, which is the realm we are in now. Within them are many degrees, the mansions Christ spoke of: *“In my Father’s house are many mansions… I go to prepare a place for you”* **(John 14:2)**.


By coming into this realm, this test of mortality, Leonard said, all of us will be resurrected. That is what Christ did: He broke the bands of death. We are no longer all damned; He freed us from death and hell. Where we end up depends on us and what we do.


Confessing is one thing, Leonard said, and doing is a whole different thing. If you confess Christ to be your Savior, then do what He asks. Words alone are just words. You have to do what He commands if you want the blessings of obeying Him. His doctrine was to believe, be baptized, receive the Holy Ghost, repent, and endure to the end. You have to change and improve, and that is all a matter of degrees and of agency: the freedom to choose, and then learning to choose righteously. For three or four years, Leonard said, that was his prayer for his daughters, that they would choose righteously. For the most part they did, and they have received the blessings of doing so. He believes the Lord approves.


So to say as a blanket statement that everybody is saved, Leonard said, may be correct in eternity. But the Lord directed us to be like Him, and He did so by obeying the Father. The Father provides the way through His commandments. The Son provides the example; He came and showed us the way, and that is how we can return to our Father. We will all return to the Father who gave us life after this life. Leonard said his scriptures teach that. But that does not mean we will stay with the Father. Scripture does not say that.


Susan asked whether, in his theology, everyone eventually fits into one of the three degrees of glory. Leonard said yes.

>
**Note:** The vision of the three degrees is recorded in Doctrine and Covenants 76 (1832). There the terrestrial “receive of the presence of the Son, but not of the fulness of the Father” (D&C 76:77), and the telestial receive the Holy Spirit through the ministration of the terrestrial (D&C 76:86).


## Sons of perdition


Susan asked about the sons of perdition, whom she understood Latter-day Saint teaching to exclude. Leonard said that perdition is not a kingdom of glory, and very few attain to it. You would have to deny the sun at noonday. Who would do such a thing? Susan asked. Leonard said some have: Lucifer, Satan, the third of the hosts of heaven who followed him, all the demons. With all the evidence of a God and Father who loves them, they denied it and chose another way, against the Creator who created them. They borrow light and life from Him, and if you go against the source you borrow from, you must suffer the consequences.


Christ cannot save you in your sins, Leonard said. He cannot. If you choose not to obey and to sin, you set yourself at odds with the Creator, and He cannot save you, because you chose. So you need to change; you need to repent. That is the whole concept of the gospel. He has provided all of us an off-ramp: repentance through the Son, who will cover our sins. Susan said she agreed completely.


Leonard offered a modern image. By what He did, Christ put an eternal amount of Bitcoin into our accounts. We just need to access it, and He alone has the passcodes. He stands at the gate and lets no one else in. So we need to stay focused on Him. Susan said that was a good one.

>
**Note:** The phrase about denying the sun at noonday echoes Joseph Smith’s King Follett sermon (April 1844), which described the unpardonable sin as saying the sun does not shine while one sees it. The vision of the sons of perdition is D&C 76:31–38. The statement that the Lord cannot save men in their sins is from the Book of Mormon (Alma 11:37).


## Could Satan repent?


Susan then asked: if Christ’s sacrifice and atonement are infinite, can Lucifer and the demons, the rebellious angels, repent too?


“Eventually,” Leonard said. “Eternity’s a long time.” He would not put it past the Lord to accept Satan, just like the prodigal son. Susan said she hoped so.


But Leonard continued, they have a role to play, and they love it. Satan, Lucifer, and the demons have created their own little kingdom, and they love being against all that is good. It is part of them.


Leonard described an idea he has been studying, which he said was new to him. Satan’s hatred of God, he believes, came first from not being chosen to be the Savior, and second from not being chosen to be Adam. Satan wanted to be the first man, and God chose Michael instead. That was it; from then on Satan has had eternal hatred toward God and tries to do everything he can to thwart the work of God.

>
**Note:** In Latter-day Saint teaching, Adam is identified with Michael the archangel (D&C 107:54), and Lucifer’s rejected bid to be the Redeemer appears in the Pearl of Great Price (Moses 4:1–4; Abraham 3:27, where the offer is “Here am I, send me”). The idea that Lucifer wanted to be Adam is Leonard’s own reading, not standard doctrine.


## What is the work of God?


Leonard said Thomas has an interesting view of God’s work: that God is having an experience of humanity through us. His own tradition believes that God has already attained that and gone beyond it, and that His work and glory is to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man. Susan helped him recall the wording. Leonard located it in the Doctrine and Covenants.

>
**Note:** The verse is from the Pearl of Great Price, not the Book of Mormon or the Doctrine and Covenants: “For behold, this is my work and my glory—to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man” (Moses 1:39).


## Thomas on Satan


Susan asked Thomas whether his hopeful theology of everyone being saved includes Lucifer and the demons.


Thomas said he does not see any scriptural basis for it. In the Bible, Satan is locked up in eternal separation. So he has to go to his sense of what is logical.


He places Satan in the role of the one who is embodied evil. If Satan were a soul like Susan or Thomas who happened to get caught in the Satan role, then Thomas thinks the answer is yes, he could return. But if the story requires someone to fill that role, the question is sticky. It goes back to an earlier discussion between Thomas and Susan: was Satan created as he is, or did he fall? The story works either way for Thomas. All it took was someone to fill the role.

>
**Note:** *“And the devil that deceived them was cast into the lake of fire and brimstone, where the beast and the false prophet are, and shall be tormented day and night for ever and ever”* **(Revelation 20:10)**.


Susan said she was glad it was not her, and that she felt sorry for the poor devil who got that role. Thomas said he is enjoying it.


Leonard said he does not know whether Satan is enjoying it, but he chose it. There are always opposites. God is pure love; that is His power, and salvation is derived from that love. Christ atoned for us because He loved us. Satan’s hatred is as broad, as deep, and as black as God’s love is broad, high, and light.


Could Satan repent? Leonard does not think so. He chose that existence, and because of the blackness and darkness he chose, he could not bear to be in God’s presence. Scripture says he was cast out, but Leonard thinks he left. He said, in effect, *I’m out of here*, and that is where he is and always will be, because his hatred is eternal: the deepest, blackest hatred for everything that is light and God. And Satan had been a child of God. He was Lucifer, the bearer of light, who had climbed Jacob’s ladder to a high position, high enough to stand in the presence of God and volunteer, *Send me.* God knew his heart and chose another. Leonard believes God would forgive him if he repented, but that he never will.


## Could God ever rest?


Susan asked how God could ever be happy, or rest, knowing that one lost soul, His child, was out there unreclaimed.


Leonard acknowledged the weight of that. But God can do nothing about it, he said, because when you reject God you put yourself out of His reach. Hence the concept of the sons of perdition: people who have gone so far that there is no forgiveness, because they do not want to be forgiven. Whether that could ever change, he does not know. They have chosen the darkness. And they have companions who chose the same, just as on this earth there are evil people who hold secret meetings about how to control the world, and who love the feeling of power and control.


Susan said she thought everyone in the group believed they would eventually come back. Leonard said he does not. They do not want to come back. Why would they, having attained such a level of darkness?


Susan pointed out that his theology still gives even them a place. Leonard said it gives them an existence, but not a kingdom of glory. The three degrees are places of glory; perdition, outer darkness, is not. There are existences with no glory.


“Like Earth?” Susan asked. Leonard said yes, exactly. Susan said she would not mind being here; she likes it.


Leonard said he has come to understand that, of all the creations and worlds made, this is a very unusual one. It is very unusual for God to condescend to this level to save it and bring it back up to where it was supposed to be. Susan summed up the range of views around the table: that is just Leonard, that is just Susan, that is just Thomas.


## Closing prayer


As Leonard prepared to leave, the group asked Susan to pray. She thanked God for the small group that comes together to learn of Him and to edify one another, and for the way its members have influenced each other even where they still see things differently. She asked Him to go with them through the week, to help them serve Him and understand His word, to bless the world and call out to everyone who might turn to Him, and to send workers into the field to sow, water, harvest, and teach the gospel.

# Why America Must Stand: Iran, Repentance, and a Nation Undivided


After the closing prayer at the Sunday Fellowship meeting of 8 November 2026, Thomas and Susan Gutierrez stayed on. Susan noted that the group had not discussed the essay Thomas had circulated on the war with Iran. Their conversation covered three things: why the war is America’s war and not only Israel’s, why unity at home requires repentance, and whether the way to bring opponents to Christ is to hold the line or to walk with them step by step.


## An essay meant to have legs


Susan said she had read only the first part of Thomas’s summary; foreign policy is not her strength, and she did not expect to contribute much. She was sorry that Thomas had put so much work into it and the group had not discussed it at all. She understood him to be saying that everyone needs to get on the same page about the war in Iran, and that Christ and the teaching of the gospel are fundamental to what he thinks America needs to do. She said she has no problem with that.

With help from Joelle and Isak, he plans an animation, a speech, Instagram posts, a letter-writing campaign, and a network of people committed to a different way. What underlies it all, he said, is the sanctification of America. There has to be a reason why we do what we do.


## Not Israel’s lapdog


Thomas said he has been shocked at how widely the anti-Israel campaign has taken hold. People believe that this is not our war: that it is only Israel’s, that America was sucked in through entangling alliances, and that we are basically Israel’s lapdog.


Thomas said that is wrong. America has as much at stake as Israel does, or more.


Susan gave two reasons she agrees. The first is history. The American government was instrumental in bringing the wrong people to power in Iran, and so she feels America has an obligation to help that be overcome. Many people think it is wrong to get involved in Iran, but America’s involvement there happened a long time ago.


Thomas agreed that America’s entanglement with Iran, removing one leader and installing the Shah, places the country within the realm of causation and culpability. But he said that is not the real problem. The real problem is the present threat.

## A real threat

Now, Thomas said, imagine leaving them with 440 kilograms of highly enriched uranium and saying, *Who are we to tell you not to have a nuclear weapon?* If America does not stand now, it never will, and there will be a nuclear Iran, which Thomas can only see as the kingdom of Satan, Satan’s hand extended.


To say we have no right is wrong, Thomas said. We have every right. This is God’s world. We do not say to someone who wants to blow it up, *We have no right to stop you.* We have to do what is right, which is to say no to people who are destroying God’s creation.

>
**Note:** The International Atomic Energy Agency reported that as of 13 June 2025, Iran held 440.9 kg of uranium enriched up to 60%, against the agency’s yardstick of about 42 kg for one weapon if enriched further to 90%. The agency has not been able to verify the stockpile’s status or location since the strikes of June 2025 ([AP via Defense News](https://www.defensenews.com/global/mideast-africa/2025/09/03/iran-upped-near-weapons-grade-uranium-stock-before-israel-attack-iaea/); [Reuters via Daily Maverick](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/much-of-irans-nearbombgrade-uranium-likely-to-be-in-isfahan-iaeas-grossi-says)). The phrase “we love death as you love life” is more often attributed to al-Qaeda, Hamas, and Hezbollah figures than to Iranian officials. The regime’s ideology does exalt martyrdom, and belief in the Mahdi’s return is part of Twelver Shi’ism, but how much that belief drives state policy is debated.


## Will God intervene?


Thomas said God could intervene miraculously, putting His big thumb on Iran’s nuclear program and squashing it. He does not think that is how God plays the game. He thinks God says, *I want you to go down and actually make this work.* And Thomas thinks God would let the fire come, and it would be terrible.


## The first thing God wants


Susan agreed that God wants Americans to do something. But she said the first thing He wants is for us to repent, to pray to Him, and to ask Him for help.

Susan said everybody should, for all the sins here in America. # One Center, Many Portals: Why Thomas Holds a Vision Few People See


In the last hour of the Sunday Fellowship meeting on 8 November 2026, Thomas and Susan Gutierrez talked about the gap between Thomas’s central conviction and how most people hear it. The conviction is that there is one God, He is all, and there is nothing but Him; we are living Him. Their conversation compared Thomas’s way of presenting the gospel with a more conventional one, traced where his conviction came from, and ended with his decision to stand publicly for it.


## A perceptual gap


Thomas said that talking that morning made him see the gap between himself and Michael Sherman. Michael found the idea that all this exists for God’s pleasure as funny as their old private joke, “Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric”: a knee-slapper, stand-up comedy material.


Thomas said he found that interesting, because it showed him how far out of step he is with what is, to him, the most real thing in reality. He has triangulated this point for forty years, pressing on it from every angle: Does it hold up here? Does it hold up there? Everywhere he looks, it is the only thing that makes sense.


He said Michael’s response is the heart and soul of comedy. The comedian says, *Look at this. God created this thing. Isn’t it a knee-slapper that God would create a world like this?* And the audience answers, *Yes, brother, you are right.* Thomas thinks most people would side with Michael and say, *Take it to him, man; this guy is ridiculous.* He said Michael has not grasped the gravity and holiness of the idea, and probably most people are in the same position.


## Why Thomas holds the center


Thomas said that because his perspective is so unusual, he has had to compare it with every other perspective he can find. He could take a middle position and try to bring people to it, and he often does. But that is not what he is called to do. Too many secondary questions lie down that road.


His calling, he said, is to take his stand and show how it relates to this and to that, in a thousand different ways. That is the only tool he has, and it is the only reason he is doing this at all. The perspective is so powerful that it solves everything. It is the center spoke of every hub; every problem goes back to it. From the center you can travel out to the spokes, and they all connect, fit, and are beautiful. But away from the center things grow more complex, and the arguments multiply: what about this, and this, and this. He could spend his life in one such conversation with one person and never leave it.


What he is trying to do, he said, is equip every person to hold on to what he has: there is one God, He is all, there is nothing but Him. We are living Him. He is the potter, and we are the clay. In Him we live, move, and have our being. *“But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter; and we all are the work of thy hand”* **(Isaiah 64:8)**.


And then, Thomas asked, how does that translate down to the fact that my pancakes just got burned? He can make that move in an instant. He has traveled these roads in every direction he can imagine, so he has no trouble putting himself in the shoes of someone completely lost. He described a counseling call that week from someone grieving the loss of a sibling who had been his only confidant through a very hard family life, and who did not know how to go on. Thomas understood the situation and knew the answer. But most people caught in such a whirlpool do not navigate far enough to see that God is at the center of it. Most go where Michael went.


## Susan’s presentation


Susan said she was not claiming Thomas’s view is untrue. Her point was about influencing others. She offered a perspective that she thinks can do what Thomas described: ground people and give them something to hold on to, in a form more people can grasp.


God created us and wants to live with us for all eternity. But He cannot live with us and remain a righteous God if we are doing wrong to each other. He needs us to choose to do right and follow Him, so He has given us this place where we can choose.


She believes the story of Adam is real, not merely figurative, and Thomas said he does too. Adam had a real opportunity to keep obeying God, and as long as he stayed obedient, God would have taught him line by line everything he needed to choose wisely, to have the knowledge of good and evil and choose the good. Both choices were before him, but he lacked wisdom, and he likely chose not to obey. He chose not to obey, so we have a world where God the Father is not present.


But we have Jesus, *“the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world”* **(John 1:9)**. He came to live in this world and died for our sins, because no one is without sin, and made a way for us to get back to the Father. The whole point is for us to live eternally with the Father. That is what They want, Susan said, and They do it out of love. They give us a choice. Everyone chooses some right things and some wrong things, but there is a way back.


If we put our trust in Christ, He makes us new and gives us new hearts. We will still struggle, because we live among people doing wrong, but now we have a heart that wants to obey and serve Him. Others will see the peace, love, and joy and want it too, and we get to help Christ draw them; we do our part, and He does His. There is much wrong in the world, including wrong done by people who say they believe in Christ, which is why Christ gave us a way to recognize His true followers: *“By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another”* **(John 13:35)**. When you come to Christ, He changes you in good ways. It is not always easy or fun, but it is very rewarding. It feels good inside to want to do good to others instead of being filled with *I want this,* and *I need that*. Every single person can have that. Christ will not leave anyone behind as too big a project. He wants every single person to come to Him, because He has something glorious for each one.


What does someone say to that? Susan asked. It is not a comedy act. He says, *Wow. I don’t know if I believe that, but it’s a beautiful picture.* It helps him understand why God allows wrong in the world, and what he can do when he is wronged. Among all the perspectives, it is something he can hold on to, just as Thomas described, but something more people can grasp, even if they don’t yet believe it.

>
**Note:** *“A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you”* **(Ezekiel 36:26)**.


## Why Thomas still rides his own horse


Thomas said Susan was describing what works to enroll people, and clearly his method does not, because nobody knows what he is talking about. *Why would I believe that? That’s absurd.* Susan’s point of view is, in a way, conventional. It’s how the gospel went from one man and twelve disciples to the world’s largest religion. Clearly that method works.


But Thomas said he looks out and sees six and a half billion people who do not go along with the Christian perspective, and he asks why. He was one of them. He was an Eastern mystic, searching for God the way they search. He does not believe they found it; he was around them and never met anyone he thought had it. But he thinks he did. He believes he has something to say to these people that their teachers are not saying, and that where they think they want to go is not where they actually want to go.


Susan asked whether he thinks his perspective will reach them because they believe God is one. Thomas said that “all is God and God is all” is a common idea. He remembered the Indian Sikhs he met, who always said *God is one*; it was almost a greeting. He does not think they knew what it meant; it was a mantra and a catchphrase. Christians do the same with *God is good.* Do we grasp the depth of that? Probably not. God is probably so good that we have no concept of what good is. Susan said we can mean it even if we do not understand it. Thomas agreed, and said he is sure the Sikhs meant *God is one* as well; they simply had no idea of its depth. The Buddhists’ whole path is about entering the oneness. It is an Eastern emphasis, but Thomas does not think it contradicts the Christian faith.


## Can the conventional presentation reach the oneness?


Susan said she had to leave soon, but asked whether her presentation failed to reach the people Thomas wants to reach because it did not address the oneness. Thomas said it did not; hers addressed the need for God’s grace and God’s goodness. Susan said she would put it as the love of God and His ultimate purpose of bringing us back to Him. His mindset is, *This is the end I want, and all of this is necessary to get there.* It gives Him no pleasure to see all the wickedness, but it is ultimately necessary for the end goal. Thomas agreed.


Susan said she thinks she could also address the oneness from the traditional point of view, for someone whose foundation is that God is one. God the Father, His Son Jesus, and the Holy Spirit are three persons but one God, like a bowl of fruit: one bowl with many pieces. They are in complete harmony, not competing at all, and all three are working toward mankind coming back to live with Them. She has recently come to think the Father and the Holy Spirit might be the same being, though she does not know for sure. God teaches us that the way to harmony and truth, the way back to Him, is to obey Him, and He sent His Son to give us the new heart we need. We had a heart that loves evil, and He gives us, perhaps in stages, a heart with the love of God in it and joy in doing right. God is drawing us into oneness with Himself so that we can all be one. She believes we will still be separate persons, but there will be no evil, no competition, no hatred. That oneness exists now within the one God who is three persons, and we can begin to share in it now. She asked Thomas, who had been one of those seekers when she had not, whether he thought that would work for them.

>
**Note:** *“That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us”* **(John 17:21)**.


## Portals to the center


Thomas said he really didn’t know what worked for anybody. He only knows what worked for him: the hobby horse he rides, because nothing else worked. He does not know how much anyone cares where everything came from, how the oneness trickled down into the many, or how three are one, each with His own purpose and joy. Everyone lives in his own world.


So his message, he said, is: this is the center, and this is what it says about the big questions. Then he tries to cover as many small questions as possible. He has up to about 950 articles on his website, and Isak mind-mapped and connected them the week before, so a reader can see the family of essays on any topic and how it links to others. He is trying to create as many portals as possible, each of which can be followed back to the center. If someone finds one that works, Thomas hopes he will use it, share it in his circle, and follow it back to the truth that Jesus is Lord, because that is the center of it all.


God created everything, Thomas said, and He created it through the Son. Everything is His play. The Iran war is His play. The Muslims, Ishmael and Isaac, are His play. The Nazi guard and the Holocaust victim are His play. Everything is His doing, and the only question is how one sees it through this lens. Whatever the story, whether it is about the Ku Klux Klan or about cloning DNA, he takes it back to the source. The best his talent allows is to create as many vignettes as possible, so someone might latch onto one.


He hopes for twelve: twelve people who see completely where he is coming from, and who say, *I’m a fisherman, I’m going to teach it this way*, or *I’m a tax collector, I’m going to teach it this way*, and go out and have their own ministries. He cannot do it all. It is an untried theory, he said; he is simply doing it, because it has completely taken him over.


Susan said Thomas has influenced her quite a bit. The idea that we are all in God looked ridiculous to her when he first said it. But as she read Scripture, she kept finding verses consistent with it, until she came to feel it is consistent with what the Bible says over and over, even though she cannot quite grasp how.


## The householder’s path


Thomas recalled the first lecture he attended when he became a yogi. The group recommended *How to Know God*, a rendering of Patanjali’s ancient sutras on enlightenment, and he thought that was what he was signing up for. The sutras teach stilling the mind until nothing one is attached to disturbs it, and in that peace recognizing the complete oneness of God.


The group turned out to be something else entirely, he said; as a sales pitch it was a red herring. Its members drove taxis, ran theaters and health food stores, and had babies. But it was exactly what he needed. It was the path of the householder: seeing the same oneness while being completely overwhelmed by life. That is a realistic path, because it is the one everybody lives on.


It is the path he teaches: have babies, go to work, take care of the yard, travel, teach others, try to mend and fix the world. In the center of all that chaos, there is God. You will not find Him by escaping it, but you will find the way to live like Him if you follow Christ, because Christ shows the way of the One who is One. That is what pleases the One who lives you and lives everyone. Thomas said it is a simple message that no one he studied with ever taught. He came to it by his own deduction, as the common theme through everything he had seen. He keeps checking whether it works, and it does, so that is where he hangs his hat.

>
**Note:** *How to Know God: The Yoga Aphorisms of Patanjali* is the 1953 translation and commentary by Swami Prabhavananda and Christopher Isherwood. Patanjali’s *Yoga Sutras* are usually dated to roughly the 2nd century BC through the 5th century AD, so whether they preceded Christ is uncertain.


## Data points


Thomas said he hopes a few people will grasp this and teach it more like Susan does, because his own way seems inaccessible. That has surprised him. He had assumed that since it was obvious to him, all he needed to do was explain it a little better. Instead, a perceptual gap makes it nearly imperceptible. He saw the same thing throughout the Buddhist and yogi community: he never met anyone who said he had absolutely seen it.


He did collect a few data points. Before he had any such experience himself, a girlfriend named Trudy told him that one day, while looking out a window, she suddenly realized that she was the window, the outside was the window, and everything was literally one. It never happened to her before or since, and they lost touch, but she was a good woman, and her account became one data point in the development of his theory.


In his own drug experiences in those years, Thomas said he had the sense that everything happening was happening for a purpose, for him, at that moment, all converging. That experience faded. But the sense that everything was one and connected stayed with him as an idea and kept returning. Those were hints. He has never lived in the God-centered state where all is one.


Some people have, he said. Sri Ramakrishna, an Indian teacher who did little but meditate. The Vedanta Society is based on his teaching. A teacher Thomas followed, Daniel, pursued that state of non-creation. He wanted to stay there, but kept coming back out. He did not want to exist, and taught the way to eternal non-existence, but could not find it himself. He died alone. Daniel had a group that affected Thomas deeply; he taught that all is one and from the source of God. Thomas didn’t think Daniel’s goal of non-existence was the right goal to aim for, but it did illuminate the idea that God arose from a nothingness. Thomas did feel that Daniel’s conception of God as the source of all true, and that we must go beyond the oneness and leave God. Daniel’s difficulty reaching that state of non-existence illustrated our intimate connection with God. His spiritual struggle to get out, and getting pulled back into creation, was its own testimony.

>
**Note:** Ramakrishna lived from 1836 to 1886. The Vedanta Society was founded in New York in 1894 by his disciple Swami Vivekananda.


## Meditation: emptying the mind or filling it?


Susan asked whether Thomas still meditates by emptying his mind. Not really, he said. It is probably a good exercise; it may give a kind of neurological reset, and there seem to be real benefits. But he called himself the world’s worst meditator. He thinks about the cat, the field, and the work he needs to get done, and then remembers the to-do list and writes it down.


Susan said that when the Bible speaks of meditating, in all but two places it means meditating on the Word of God and the things of God. It is not describing emptying the mind. She thinks that is how we are supposed to meditate, and that emptying the mind can give Satan an opportunity to come in.


Thomas said that is probably true. *“Be still, and know that I am God”* **(Psalm 46:10)** is not emptying the mind. It is meditating on knowing that God exists.

>
**Note:** *“This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night”* **(Joshua 1:8)**; see also **(Psalm 1:2)**. On the danger of an emptied house, see Jesus’ account of the unclean spirit that returns to find its former dwelling *“empty, swept, and garnished”* **(Matthew 12:43–45)**.


## Where did God come from?


Thomas said that as for what is outside God, where God came from, or how God could exist at all, he is literally clueless. Susan said she is too, and thinks everyone is. She has never heard anyone explain where God came from. The Latter-day Saints teach that there have been many Gods before ours, but that still does not explain where the first one came from. Thomas cited the saying *As man now is, God once was*, and asked, ” Fine, but what is the bottom turtle? Where did the first God come from? Susan compared it to looking into a mirror that faces another mirror, with reflections going on forever.

>
**Note:** The couplet is Lorenzo Snow’s: “As man now is, God once was: As God now is, man may be.”


## Ready to stand


As she left, Susan said that every time they talk, she understands Thomas’s perspective a little more, and that was true this time too. Thomas said that encourages him, because it means the vision is transmissible. Every bit of progress brings someone closer to the goal. It requires a rare person, and Susan is one, and a few others are, too.


The job is too big for him, Thomas said, so he will try to multiply himself a thousandfold. In the last week or two, he said he is ready to be martyred. He is standing against Islam, and against the homosexual and transgender causes. He is simply standing for Christ, against all of these sins. He is no longer apologetic or in the closet, and he will soon be very public. To make that witness, he had to go through that door, and this past week, in a conversation with Isak, he said, *All right. I’m ready to take the step.*


Susan asked whether this was an actual milestone, something that happened that week. Thomas said yes. He is still incorporating it; something this large takes a while for the body to catch up with. But he is doing it whether or not he feels fully committed, and he assumes it will eventually become simply who he is. When he ran a presidential campaign in 2024, he never made the campaign website public; no one could search for it and find it. That showed his reluctance, because he knew the cost. This time he has let go of that. He is willing to pay the price to stand out and to be the face behind the name and the stand.


Thomas said that whoever comes alongside him will probably face the same requirement. This can’t be done privately. People can talk to others privately, and perhaps for some that is all that is needed, but he is being public. If he can find a dozen people to come along, it is only because that is what Christ did, and it worked. He is not trying to cram Christ down anyone’s throat. He is trying to share a message that works and keeps going. He knows what it did for him, and he wants it for everybody.
