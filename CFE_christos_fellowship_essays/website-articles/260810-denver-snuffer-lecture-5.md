# An Academic Assessment of Denver Snuffer’s 500th-Anniversary Lecture 5


## Historical Accuracy and Biblical Consistency


**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 10, 2026**


[**S****ource:**](https://restorationarchives.com/blog/index.php/2020/06/04/5th-address-to-christians/) Denver Snuffer Jr., fifth lecture of the eight-lecture series delivered on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of the Protestant Reformation. The transcript provided is the lecture body, without introduction or question period. The series and its supporting materials are published at https://www.christianreformation500years.info and at https://www.learnofchrist.org. Readers are encouraged to hear or read the lecture in full. **Method:** As in the assessments of Lectures 1–4, this essay is an academic evaluation only. Each substantive claim is extracted and assessed on two axes: (1) **historical accuracy** against the documented record and (2) **biblical consistency** against the text of Scripture (King James Version). This lecture is an extended commendation of the Book of Mormon, so a third, subsidiary check is applied where the lecture makes claims *about* the Book of Mormon text: whether the record says what the lecture reports it as saying. Claims are paraphrased; quotation is minimal; no judgment is offered on rhetorical importance or persuasive weight. Claims already assessed in earlier essays are cross-referenced rather than re-argued.

## I. The Planting and the Voyage


### 1. The intermediate planting between the two captivities


**Claims as delivered.** Between the loss of the Northern Kingdom and the captivity of the Southern Kingdom, another planting of Israel occurred: a small group in the Southern Kingdom, warned by God to flee before Babylon’s destruction of Jerusalem, migrated across the Arabian Peninsula, built a ship, and settled in the Americas; the account is contained in the Book of Mormon.


**Assessment.** As a report of the Book of Mormon’s own narrative, accurate: the record opens “in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah” (1 Nephi 1:4, conventionally c. 597 BC), with Lehi warned to depart before the destruction (1 Nephi 2:1–2), the Arabian traverse (1 Nephi 16–17), and the shipbuilding at Bountiful (1 Nephi 17:8; 18:1–4). The chronological placement “between” the two falls is serviceable if the southern terminus is the 586 BC destruction, though the departure as internally dated falls after the first Babylonian deportation (597 BC) rather than in a clean interval between the kingdoms’ falls. As history, the planting has no attestation independent of the Book of Mormon itself; that is the standing evidentiary situation of the text, recorded without adjudication. Biblically, the motif invoked — branches of Israel broken off and planted elsewhere — has genuine anchors (the vineyard of Isaiah 5; “the outcasts of Israel” and “the dispersed of Judah” gathered “from the four corners of the earth,” Isaiah 11:12; the natural branches of Romans 11), so the category is not unbiblical; the specific instance rests on the record commended.


### 2. The voyage route: Africa, the Cape, and the North Atlantic


**Claims as delivered.** The route “appears, from the record,” to run down the eastern edge of Africa, around the cape at the bottom of Africa — where a terrific storm coincided with a family rebellion — then up the western edge of Africa, “across the English Channel,” past Iceland, Greenland, and the northern Canadian shore, and down into the Americas.


**Assessment against the record cited.** The Book of Mormon text contains none of these waypoints. The recorded itinerary is: travel “nearly a south-southeast direction” along the Red Sea (1 Nephi 16:13–14, 33), then “nearly eastward from that time forth” (1 Nephi 17:1) to the coastal land Bountiful; the ship is then “driven forth before the wind towards the promised land” (1 Nephi 18:8–9). The storm is indeed recorded and is indeed tied to the rebellion — a great tempest of four days arising after Nephi is bound, ceasing when he is loosed (1 Nephi 18:11–21) — but the text locates it only “upon the waters”; no African coast, no cape, no channel, no northern islands, and no directional data beyond the eastward departure appear anywhere in the record. An eastward departure from the Arabian coast, taken at face value, points into the Indian Ocean, which is why the reconstructions common in Book of Mormon studies run eastward across the Indian and Pacific Oceans; the Africa-and-North-Atlantic itinerary is a modern minority reconstruction (associated with the lecturer’s own writings and with North American-setting geographies) and is here presented as what “appears from the record” when the record supplies none of it. As navigation history, a c. 590 BC crossing by way of Iceland and Greenland would be without ancient parallel — the attested crossings on that route are Norse, c. AD 1000. **Finding:** the storm-rebellion linkage is accurate to the text; the itinerary is not in the text, runs against the text’s one directional datum, and is a contested modern reconstruction presented as the record’s own appearance.


### 3. A civilization uninfluenced by the image


**Claims as delivered.** The planting was never exposed to the head of gold, the Medes and Persians, the Greeks, or the Romans; it preserved its civilization about a thousand years, practicing the earlier, more ancient religion, with its own prophets and revelations, and was ultimately visited by Christ as one of the scattered branches.


**Assessment.** Internally accurate to the record’s frame: the narrative spans c. 600 BC to Moroni’s final entries “four hundred and twenty” years after Christ (Moroni 10:1), roughly a millennium, geographically insulated from the Near Eastern succession. The uninfluenced-witness argument built on it — that the record therefore supplies a measuring stick independent of the corrupted stream — is an argument from the record’s own claims about itself; its force depends entirely on the record’s authenticity, which is the question under commendation. Recorded as internally consistent and evidentially circular at the point where it is offered as independent corroboration.

## II. The Hermeneutical Argument


### 4. Walter Martin’s principle and its extension to the Book of Mormon


**Claims as delivered.** Walter Martin, “the Bible Answer Man,” taught as the first principle of biblical hermeneutics that one interprets the old in light of the new — the Old Testament means what the New Testament says it means; prophecy is understood only in its fulfillment; the Book of Mormon, though composed 600 BC–AD 400, came forth in 1830 and is therefore the latest in time; accepting Martin’s principle therefore requires the faithful Christian to interpret both Testaments by the Book of Mormon.


**Historical assessment.** The identifications are accurate: Walter Martin (1928–1989) hosted the *Bible Answer Man* broadcast, founded the Christian Research Institute, and authored *The Kingdom of the Cults* (1965), the era’s principal evangelical polemic against Mormonism among other movements — a fact the lecture itself notes later in recounting the speaker’s own upbringing. The interpret-the-old-by-the-new principle is genuinely standard in evangelical hermeneutics and fairly attributed.


**Assessment of the argument.** The extension commits an identifiable equivocation. The principle, as held by Martin and the tradition he spoke for, is *canonical*, not *chronological*: the New Testament interprets the Old not because it is later in time but because it is received as the inspired apostolic witness to the fulfillment — later-ness is incidental to the warrant. Detached from that warrant and made purely temporal, the principle proves far too much: the Qur’an (seventh century), Swedenborg’s writings, *Science and Health* (1875), and any subsequent claimed revelation would each in turn become the governing interpreter of all that preceded it, the Book of Mormon included — a consequence the lecture would not accept, and which exposes the suppressed premise. That premise — that the Book of Mormon is inspired scripture of canonical standing — is precisely the conclusion the argument is deployed to commend; assuming it in the hermeneutical step is a *petitio principii*. The Bible’s own instruction runs the other direction: the later claimant is tested by the earlier deposit, not the deposit by the claimant (“though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel… let him be accursed,” Galatians 1:8; “to the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word…,” Isaiah 8:20; the Bereans testing the apostle himself by the scriptures, Acts 17:11). The subsidiary point — that prophecy is fully understood only in fulfillment — is biblically sound and was assessed at Lecture 2 §26 and Lecture 3 §22 (John 13:19; 14:29; 1 Peter 1:10–12), and the Herod-and-the-magi illustration is accurately retold (Matthew 2:1–8, with 2:6 citing Micah 5:2); the sound sub-point does not repair the equivocation in the main argument.

## III. The Other Sheep and the Resurrection Appearances


### 5. John 10:16 and the Book of Mormon’s interpretation


**Claims as delivered.** The risen Christ told the gathered faithful in the Americas that they were the “other sheep” of John 10:16; the Jerusalem disciples had not asked and had wrongly supposed the saying meant the Gentiles would be reached through their ministry; Christ meant He would personally visit scattered remnants of Israel, the Book of Mormon peoples among them, and still others besides; the later record thus interprets the earlier saying.


**Assessment.** The reportage is accurate: 3 Nephi 15:16–24 and 16:1–3 say what the lecture says they say, including the express statement that the Gentiles were *not* meant and that the sheep are scattered Israel to be visited in person. John 10:16 is quoted accurately. On the biblical axis, the interpretation must be weighed against the New Testament’s own internal gloss, and there it meets direct counter-texts. The reading “other sheep = scattered Israel” is not impossible on John 10:16 in isolation — sheep language elsewhere designates Israel (“the lost sheep of the house of Israel,” Matthew 10:6; 15:24). But the Johannine corpus supplies its own interpretation of the gathering-into-one: Caiaphas prophesied “that Jesus should die for that nation; **and not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad**” (John 11:51–52), which the fourth Gospel’s frame extends beyond Israel; and the apostolic writings present the one-fold-one-shepherd consummation as the union of Jew and Gentile in one body — “he… hath made both one… for to make in himself of twain **one new man**… that he might reconcile **both** unto God in one body” (Ephesians 2:14–16); “there shall be one fold, and one shepherd” language taken up in the Gentile ingathering of Acts 10–11 and 15:14–17. The Book of Mormon reading requires that the Gentile understanding be a *misunderstanding*; the New Testament presents the Gentile ingathering as fulfillment, in texts the lecture does not engage. **Finding:** accurate as reportage; as interpretation, it stands against the New Testament’s own resolution of the saying, and its acceptance depends on the prior acceptance of the interpreting record (§4).


### 6. The catalogue of resurrection appearances


**Claims as delivered.** The risen Christ appeared first to two women; He spent the better part of the day on the Emmaus road with two disciples — Cleopas and an unidentified other “who wrote the record, Luke”; then to the twelve; later to Paul; and at His ascension “there were above 500 gathered together… when He ascended from the mount”; multiple witnesses and audiences, paralleled by the Book of Mormon appearance.


**Biblical assessment.** The catalogue’s frame (multiple appearances to multiple audiences; 1 Corinthians 15:5–8) is sound; three details are contradicted by the texts. (a) “First to two women” follows Matthew 28:1, 9 (Mary Magdalene and the other Mary) but conflicts with the explicit statement of Mark 16:9 — “he appeared **first to Mary Magdalene**” — and with John 20:11–18, where Magdalene meets Him alone; the harmonizations differ, and “first to two women” states one strand as the whole. (b) The identification of the unnamed Emmaus disciple as Luke, the record’s author, is contradicted by Luke’s own preface, which distinguishes the author from the eyewitnesses: the things believed were “delivered… unto us” by “them… which **from the beginning were eyewitnesses**” (Luke 1:1–2) — the evangelist expressly writes as a recipient of testimony, not a participant; the Luke-at-Emmaus identification is a late minority conjecture, and it is here asserted as fact against the Gospel’s self-description. (c) The five hundred are not placed at the ascension by any text: Paul says Christ “was seen of above five hundred brethren at once” (1 Corinthians 15:6) with no location; the ascension accounts involve the apostles on Olivet near Bethany (Luke 24:50–51; Acts 1:9–12, “men of Galilee”); interpreters who venture a setting for the five hundred usually propose the appointed mountain in Galilee (Matthew 28:16), not the ascension. The lecture fuses 1 Corinthians 15:6 with Acts 1 into an event Scripture nowhere records. Three details, each checkable, each inaccurate to the record; the argumentative use (abundant witness) survives on the corrected data.

## IV. The Two Capitals and Babylon


### 7. Zion and Jerusalem as two locations; the New Jerusalem


**Claims as delivered.** The Old Testament prophecies of Zion and Jerusalem at the Lord’s return speak of two separate locations on opposite sides of the world, so that the sun will never set on His kingdom; He will establish a kingdom at old Jerusalem and a second part at the New Jerusalem, in Zion; out of Zion and out of Jerusalem will go the law and the teachings; a small religious body will build the New Jerusalem as antecedent to Zion.


**Biblical assessment.** This is the two-capitals claim assessed at Lecture 3 §19, and the finding there governs: the proof-text’s own parallelism identifies the two names as one place — “out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the LORD **from Jerusalem**” (Isaiah 2:3; Micah 4:2) — Hebrew poetic parallelism, with Zion elsewhere expressly equated with the city of David (2 Samuel 5:7; Psalm 76:2). Two additions in this lecture require their own notes. (a) The sun-never-sets rationale is attested in no scripture; it is a modern imperial idiom (proverbially the Spanish and British empires) imported as though it were a prophetic datum. (b) The New Jerusalem of the Bible is not a city built by a religious body in advance of the consummation: in its two canonical occurrences it **descends from God out of heaven** (Revelation 3:12; 21:2, 10) at the end. The built-in-America New Jerusalem, constructed by a gathered people as Zion’s antecedent, is Restoration doctrine (Ether 13:2–8; D&C 84:2–4; 45:66–67), stated here without identification of its source. On the two axes: the two-locations reading is unsupported by the texts cited and the building program is contrary to the direction of motion in Revelation’s own New Jerusalem texts.


### 8. Babylon the great as the persisting head of gold


**Claims as delivered.** John’s prophecy of the fall of Babylon the great shows the head of gold still with us — in banking, profit motives, culture, education, the desire for power and wealth; John strikes at the head because when the head is destroyed the rest unravels.


**Assessment.** This develops the persistence-of-influence interpretation of Daniel 2:35 assessed at Lecture 4 §6, where it was recorded as a defensible interpretation with canonical resonance (Revelation 17–18; “Babylon” for Rome at 1 Peter 5:13). The additional head-first claim is homiletic: in Daniel’s text all components are broken “together” (2:35), and Revelation’s Babylon is judged alongside, not prior to, the beast and kings (Revelation 19). The moral application (pervasive commerce-and-power corruption; compare Revelation 18:11–19’s merchant lament and 1 John 2:16) is consistent as application. No new finding beyond the prior assessment.

## V. Zenos and Primeval Christology


### 9. The prophet Zenos and his excision from the Old Testament


**Claims as delivered.** The Book of Mormon quotes a pre-Isaiah prophet, Zenos — over 3,000 words by the speaker’s tally — whom the Book of Mormon’s vernacular designates “the prophet”; Zenos was apparently a Northern Kingdom prophet, Isaiah a Southern; Zenos spoke of Christ bluntly where Isaiah is poetic and construable (the suffering servant interpretable as corporate Israel); therefore Zenos could not be reformed to eliminate Christology and was obliterated from the scriptures re-gathered at the time of Ezra and Nehemiah, while Isaiah survived; the planting carried Zenos with them, which explains the Book of Mormon’s Christology.


**Assessment.** Layer by layer. (a) *Reportage:* the Book of Mormon does quote and cite Zenos extensively — the olive-tree allegory occupying Jacob 5, the prayer material of Alma 33:3–11, the signs-of-death passage of 1 Nephi 19:10–12, with Helaman 8:19 adding that he “was slain” for his testimony — so the existence and bulk of the Zenos material within the record is accurately reported (the 3,200-word tally is the speaker’s own count, plausible for Jacob 5 alone); whether the record’s occasional absolute “the prophet” designates Zenos is the speaker’s inference, not a statement of the text. (b) *External attestation:* Zenos is attested nowhere outside the Book of Mormon — not in the Hebrew Bible, the versions, Qumran, the Apocrypha or pseudepigrapha, Josephus, or rabbinic literature. A lost prophet is not, as a category, unbiblical: the Old Testament itself cites vanished books (the book of Jasher, Joshua 10:13; the books of Nathan and Gad, 1 Chronicles 29:29; the prophecy of Ahijah and visions of Iddo, 2 Chronicles 9:29), and Jude quotes a prophecy of Enoch preserved only extra-canonically (Jude 14–15). But those lost works left canonical citations as their trace; Zenos’s existence has no trace outside the record whose authenticity is at issue — the claim is unfalsifiable by construction. (c) *The excision mechanism:* the account of a deliberate dropping of Zenos “at the time of Ezra and Nehemiah” depends on the scripture-reconstitution narrative assessed at Lecture 4 §5, where the discovered-book episode was found misplaced by two centuries (Josiah, 2 Kings 22) and the lost-and-rebuilt-canon element traced to the apocryphal 2 Esdras 14 legend; a suppression executed within an event that did not occur as described inherits the misdating. (d) *The Isaiah contrast:* the claim that the servant songs’ construability preserved Isaiah is a speculative narrative; as history of interpretation, the corporate-Israel reading of Isaiah 53 is characteristic of *medieval* Jewish exegesis (Rashi and successors), while the principal ancient Jewish rendering (Targum Jonathan) applies the servant of 52:13 to Messiah — the interpretive history postdates the alleged editorial moment by a millennium and cannot evidence its motive. (e) *The kingdom assignments* (Zenos northern, Isaiah southern) are inference; the record does not state Zenos’s kingdom, and Isaiah’s Jerusalem setting is accurate. **Finding:** accurate as a report of the Book of Mormon’s contents; as history, the excision narrative is without documentary trace, rests on a misdated reconstruction, and supports the record’s Christology only if the record is first accepted — the same circle noted at §3 and §4.


### 10. Christology, baptism, and sacrifice from the first fathers


**Claims as delivered.** Through the Book of Mormon we learn the first fathers had from the beginning knowledge of the coming Savior, the practice of baptism, and animal sacrifice as type and shadow of Christ’s sacrifice; this Christology ran from Adam through Enoch, Abraham, and Moses; because of Egypt’s slave culture and corruption, Moses brought the people along with a lesser law of carnal performances pointing to the infinite and eternal sacrifice; as in Adam all die, so in Christ shall all be made alive.


**Biblical assessment.** Component by component. *Sacrifice from the beginning:* biblically attested as practice — Abel’s acceptable offering of the firstlings (Genesis 4:4; Hebrews 11:4), Noah’s altar (Genesis 8:20), the patriarchal altars — and the typological reading of sacrifice is the New Testament’s own (Hebrews 9–10, assessed at Lecture 4 §14); what Genesis does not state is that the offerers understood the type Christologically — that gloss is supplied by the Restoration source (Moses 5:6–8, where an angel expounds the sacrifice as “a similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten”), here unidentified. *Baptism from the beginning:* attested in no biblical text; baptism enters the biblical record with John (Matthew 3; John 1:33), and the New Testament’s retrojections are expressly typological — Israel “baptized unto Moses in the cloud and in the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:2), the ark as the “like figure” of baptism (1 Peter 3:20–21) — not reports of the ordinance’s antediluvian practice. Adamic baptism is the doctrine of Moses 6:64–68 in the Pearl of Great Price, presented here without identification, continuing the series’ documented pattern. *Christ known from Adam:* the New Testament asserts anticipatory faith in select figures in a veiled mode — Abraham “rejoiced to see my day” (John 8:56), Moses esteeming “the reproach of Christ” (Hebrews 11:26), the prophets searching “what, or what manner of time the Spirit of Christ… did signify” (1 Peter 1:10–11) — a portrait of forward-reaching faith, not of open, detailed Messianic knowledge from Adam; the open-knowledge-from-the-beginning claim is the Book of Moses’ frame (Moses 5–8). *The Egyptian-corruption occasion of the lesser law:* partial biblical footing (Ezekiel 20:7–8, the idols of Egypt not forsaken; Acts 7:39–43, hearts turned back into Egypt), functioning here as a variant of the greater-religion-refused claim assessed at Lecture 4 §13, whose split finding governs: lesser-and-preparatory is biblical (Galatians 3:19, 24; Hebrews 7:19; 10:1); a withdrawn greater dispensation is the Restoration reading against Exodus 34:1. *The second Adam:* Paul’s terms are “the last Adam” and “the second man” (1 Corinthians 15:45, 47) — “second Adam” is conventional shorthand — and 1 Corinthians 15:22 is quoted accurately. **Finding:** the sacrifice typology and the all-made-alive doctrine are biblically consistent; conscious primeval Christology and primeval baptism are unattested biblically and derive from the Book of Moses, unidentified.

## VI. The Institutional and Universal Claims


### 11. Every institution as Babylon


**Claims as delivered.** Christ’s work must now be done independent of institutional control, because every institution, by reason of being institutional — subject to laws, taxes, rules — is part of the great conglomerate reaching back to the head of gold; the speaker holds up no institution as the model.


**Assessment.** This generalizes the buildings-and-lawyers argument of Lecture 1 (§16 context) and meets the finding of Lecture 2 §10: the New Testament church itself possessed institutional features — appointed elders in every church and city (Acts 14:23; Titus 1:5), addressed offices (Philippians 1:1), legislated qualifications (1 Timothy 3), and a trans-local council issuing binding decisions (Acts 15) — while subsisting under Roman law and taxation (Romans 13:1–7, “pay ye tribute also”) without the apostles treating legal existence as Babylonian contamination. The by-reason-of-being-institutional premise, applied consistently, would condemn the apostolic assembly it appeals to. The prudential kernel — that institutions accrete worldly entanglements — has biblical resonance (2 Timothy 2:4; the cares-of-the-world warnings) and historical support the series has documented; the universal ontological claim exceeds it.


### 12. The Book of Mormon’s condemnation of all Christianity; the ensign


**Claims as delivered.** The Book of Mormon, used as a measuring stick, condemns all the institutions of Christianity — everyone except the few humble followers of Christ, who nevertheless err in many instances, being taught by the precepts of men (2 Nephi 28:14, quoted); the religion of Jesus Christ is hardly practiced anywhere on earth; the Book of Mormon is the standard planted in the last days, the ensign to which all Christians must rally; “wherefore murmur ye, because that ye shall receive more of my word?” (2 Nephi 29:8, paraphrased and attributed).


**Assessment.** The quotations are accurately drawn and attributed to the Book of Mormon — noted, as this lecture is the series’ most consistent in identifying its Restoration sources when quoting them directly (2 Nephi 28:14; 29:8), even as its doctrinal claims elsewhere rest on unidentified sources (§§7, 10). On the biblical axis, the condemns-all-but-a-few proposition is the universal-corruption thesis assessed at Lecture 1 §15 and carried since: it stands in tension with the preservation promises (Matthew 16:18; 28:20; Ephesians 3:21) and the remnant pattern of Revelation 2–3, in which corruption is real, named, and non-total; the same finding governs “hardly practiced anywhere on this earth.” The ensign figure is biblical in origin (Isaiah 5:26; 11:10, 12 — where the ensign is the LORD’s signal, and in 11:10 “a root of Jesse,” read messianically at Romans 15:12); its application to the Book of Mormon as the rallying standard is the Restoration’s application (compare 2 Nephi 29’s self-referential frame), and it is subject to the criterial finding of Lecture 1 §22 and Lecture 2 §22: the psychological rebuke of 2 Nephi 29:8 (“wherefore murmur ye…”) answers reluctance toward more scripture in principle, but does not supply the test by which a particular claimant is verified — that test remaining, on the Bible’s own instruction, conformity to the deposit (Galatians 1:8–9; Isaiah 8:20; Acts 17:11), which is the standard this series of assessments has been applying.


### 13. Biographical and bibliographic claims


**Claims as delivered.** The speaker was raised by a Baptist mother and a Masonic, believing but non-denominational father; he had read Walter Martin’s *The Kingdom of the Cults* before the missionaries came; the Book of Mormon grew on him slowly, over years of reading and teaching; he is not a member of the LDS Church, which has recently asserted that its name is not “the Mormon church” but the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints; he was excommunicated for prizing historical truth above institutional loyalty.


**Assessment.** The bibliographic identifications are accurate (*The Kingdom of the Cults*, 1965 and subsequent editions; the LDS Church’s 2018 style guidance deprecating “Mormon church” under President Nelson). The autobiographical matter is not independently checkable and is recorded as testimony; the excommunication’s occasion (the book *Passing the Heavenly Gift*, September 2013) is documented and was noted in the Lecture 1 assessment’s source description. The characterization of the institution’s motives is the speaker’s evaluation, outside the two axes.

## VII. Summary of Findings


Assessed on historical accuracy: accurate or substantially accurate — the Walter Martin identifications and the fair attribution of the hermeneutical slogan (§4); the Herod-and-magi retelling (§4); the reportage of 3 Nephi 15–16’s content (§5); the multiple-witness frame of the appearances (§6); the Daniel-to-Revelation Babylon linkage as interpretation (§8); the reportage of the Book of Mormon’s Zenos material and of 2 Nephi 28:14 and 29:8 (§§9, 12); the lost-books category as biblically attested (§9b); the sacrifice-from-the-beginning practice (§10); and the bibliographic and name-change data (§13). Inaccurate or materially overstated: the voyage itinerary presented as what “appears from the record,” when the record contains no African, Atlantic, or northern waypoints and its one directional datum points eastward (§2); “first to two women” against Mark 16:9 (§6a); Luke as the unnamed Emmaus disciple, against Luke 1:1–2 (§6b); the five hundred placed at the ascension, an event no text records (§6c); the Zenos-excision narrative, without documentary trace and dependent on the misdated reconstitution of Lecture 4 §5, with an interpretive-history argument (the corporate-Israel reading of Isaiah 53) that postdates the alleged editing by a millennium (§9); and the sun-never-sets rationale as a prophetic datum (§7).


Assessed on biblical consistency: consistent — the scattering-and-planting category (§1); prophecy understood in fulfillment (§4, per the prior findings); the moral application of Babylon (§8); the sacrificial typology and 1 Corinthians 15:22 (§10); and the prudential kernel of institutional wariness (§11). Inconsistent or unsupported: the chronological hermeneutic as a warrant for Book-of-Mormon priority, a *petitio principii* against the direction of Galatians 1:8, Isaiah 8:20, and Acts 17:11 (§4); the other-sheep interpretation as against the New Testament’s own resolution in John 11:51–52, Ephesians 2:14–16, and Acts 10–15 (§5); the two-locations reading of Zion and Jerusalem against the parallelism of Isaiah 2:3 and Micah 4:2, and the built New Jerusalem against the heaven-descending city of Revelation 3:12 and 21:2 (§7); primeval baptism and open primeval Christology, unattested biblically and sourced to Moses 5–6 (§10); the every-institution-is-Babylon premise against the institutional features of the apostolic church (§11); and the condemns-all-but-a-few thesis, per the standing finding on the preservation promises (§12).


As a matter of source criticism, this lecture is the most explicit in the series in citing the Book of Mormon by name when quoting it (3 Nephi, 2 Nephi 28–29), while its distinctive doctrinal claims continue to rest on unidentified Restoration sources: the built New Jerusalem (Ether 13; D&C 84), primeval baptism and expounded sacrifice (Moses 5–6), and the greater-religion frame (per Lecture 4’s findings). The lecture’s structural argument runs: an uninfluenced witness was planted apart from the image’s dominion (§§1–3), the latest word interprets the earlier (§4), that witness resolves the Bible’s obscurities (§§5, 9) and locates the coming kingdom’s second capital (§7), therefore the sincere Christian must rally to it (§12). The assessment finds the uninfluenced-witness and resolved-obscurities premises to be circular where offered as corroboration, the hermeneutical premise to equivocate between canonical and chronological priority, the voyage and appearance details to fail against the very records cited, and the two-capitals geography to be contradicted by its proof-texts. As in the prior lectures, the conclusion inherits the defects of the premises; and as throughout, these findings concern the arguments as delivered, not the sincerity of the witness delivering them.
