**Claude 5/29/2026: **This is a substantive reply that deserves a substantive read. Let me work through it honestly, because there are several different things going on in it that need separating.

So far, so good.


### What Peter gets wrong on the biblical exegesis


The Matthew 17 passage does not say what Peter wants it to say. Read carefully:


Jesus asks Peter whether kings collect tribute from their *own children* (their own sons, their own household) or from *strangers* (foreigners, outsiders, those outside the royal household). Peter answers correctly: *from strangers*. Jesus says: *then are the children free*.


The point Jesus is making is about *His own divine sonship*. He is the Son of the King of heaven; the temple is His Father’s house; in principle He owes no temple-tax to His own Father’s house. The *children* in this passage are the Son and, by extension through union with Him, the disciples — not American citizens versus foreigners. The passage is Christological, not constitutional.


And then Christ pays the tax anyway. *Notwithstanding, lest we should offend them.* He miraculously provides the coin and instructs Peter to pay for both of them. The pericope ends not with Christ asserting freedom from civil obligation, but with Christ — who actually does have a defensible exemption claim — *choosing to pay anyway* in order not to give offense. The lesson Christ draws from His own freedom is *therefore I will pay*. This is the opposite of the conclusion Peter the lawyer draws from it.


Mark 12 (the *render unto Caesar* passage proper) reinforces the same point. The Pharisees and Herodians come to *catch him in his words*. Christ asks for the coin. The coin bears Caesar’s image and inscription. Christ’s answer — *render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s* — is not a coded message about which jurisdictions Caesar may and may not tax. It is a statement that the coin itself, bearing Caesar’s image, belongs in Caesar’s jurisdiction, and that the human soul, bearing God’s image, belongs in God’s. Both renderings are commanded. The passage does not establish a category of citizens-versus-foreigners exemption.


So the two passages, read together, establish: Christ pays the tax even when He could claim exemption (Matthew 17); Christ instructs others to pay the tax while reserving for God what belongs to God (Mark 12). Peter is reading both passages against their grain.

The argument that Title 26 of the U.S. Code taxes only foreigners and not citizens — which is the substantive legal claim Peter is making — is the standard tax-protester argument. The video he links, *Theft by Deception*, is a well-known artifact of that movement. The argument has been adjudicated repeatedly, by every federal circuit court of appeals, by the Tax Court, and by the U.S. Supreme Court. It has lost every single time. Every. Single. Time. The IRS publishes a document called *The Truth About Frivolous Tax Arguments* that catalogs the variants and the case law dismissing them. The tax-protester position is, as a matter of established American law, frivolous in the technical legal sense — meaning that courts impose sanctions on litigants who advance it because the argument has been so thoroughly resolved that advancing it constitutes abuse of process.


I am not saying the federal income tax is morally right. I am not saying the IRS is well-administered. I am not saying there is no legitimate concern about who effectively pays what under the contemporary tax code. Reasonable Christians can hold a range of positions on tax policy. What I am saying is that the *specific legal claim* — that Title 26 does not apply to citizens — has been comprehensively rejected by every level of the American judicial system, and people who act on that claim by not paying their taxes face civil penalties, criminal prosecution, asset seizure, and prison. Several have gone to prison. The claim is not a quiet doctrinal disagreement; it is a position the legal system has decisively closed.

### The civil disobedience question — Peter is doing it wrong


This is the part where my prior critique of Peter (in the fellowship-meeting essay) becomes especially relevant. Peter’s email frames himself as *the Sovereign movement merely attempts to reassert the laws of God for his people. Principled Christian civil disobedience has a recognizable structure that goes back to Shadrach, Daniel, the apostles in Acts 5, the early-church martyrs, the lesser-magistrate Reformers, Bonhoeffer, the civil-rights movement. The structure includes:


- **Openness.** The objector announces the disobedience publicly, names the moral basis, and accepts the visibility of the act.
