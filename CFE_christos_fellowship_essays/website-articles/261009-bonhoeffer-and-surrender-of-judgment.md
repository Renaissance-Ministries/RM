# 261009 - Bonhoeffer and Surrender of Judgment

# The Uniform and the Fear of God: What Bonhoeffer Actually Said About Stupidity


*Renaissance Ministries Fellowship Essay | 09 October 2026*


A short video of Dietrich Bonhoeffer’s remarks on stupidity has circulated widely. It is well made and mostly accurate, and nearly everyone who shares it has the same person in mind: someone else. The progressive sees the populist crowd, and the conservative sees the campus. Each nods along, reassured that the stupid are over there. That reaction is the first sign the message hasn’t landed. When every listener uses a diagnosis only for his opponents, it functions as a slogan, and slogans are exactly what Bonhoeffer warned about.


In late 1942, after ten years of watching his country under Hitler, Bonhoeffer wrote a private reflection for a handful of friends in the resistance. One section addresses what he called stupidity, and he meant something specific by the word. He did not mean low intelligence. He observed that intellectually quick men can be stupid in his sense, and slow men quite free of it. For him, stupidity is a condition a man enters when he surrenders his judgment to a movement, a party, or a prevailing mood. Bonhoeffer thought it was less a psychological problem than a sociological one: every strong surge of power in public life, political or religious, infects a large share of people with it. Power seems to require the stupidity of the many, and the many supply it.


This is why he judged stupidity more dangerous than malice. Evil can be opposed. It can be exposed, protested, and if necessary stopped by force, and it tends to leave even its perpetrators uneasy. Stupidity offers no such handle. The stupid man simply does not believe facts that contradict his position, or waves them off as exceptions. He is fully satisfied with himself, and when pressed he becomes irritable and goes on the attack. In conversation, Bonhoeffer said, one has the sense of dealing not with the man himself but with catchwords that have taken possession of him. The video’s image for this is a good one: borrowed reason, worn like a uniform.


So far the video and Bonhoeffer agree. They part ways at the cure. The video says the cure is courage, the moment a man decides he would rather think alone than not think at all. That is half right, and the missing half matters more. Bonhoeffer said that stupidity is overcome not by instruction but by an act of liberation, and he said plainly what that liberation is. He quoted the biblical word that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), and concluded that the inner liberation of a man to live responsibly before God is the only genuine way out.


The reason this matters is practical, not merely pious. The video diagnoses the problem correctly: the man has decided that his group’s approval matters more than his own judgment. But approval is a real good, and exile from one’s own people is a real cost. Telling a man to “have courage” names the virtue he needs without telling him where to get it. A man does not stop fearing the crowd by an act of will. He stops fearing the crowd when he fears something greater. The Gospel of John records the same condition in the first century: many among the rulers believed in Jesus but would not confess Him, “for they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God” (John 12:42–43). The problem was not information, since they already believed. The problem was whose verdict they lived under.


This also answers a weakness in the secular version of the cure. The man who merely “thinks alone” has escaped one master and acquired another, namely himself. His independent judgment is still subject to vanity, resentment, and the pleasure of being a contrarian, which is its own kind of uniform. Bonhoeffer’s phrase is more exact: the responsible life *before God*. The man who answers to God has a fixed point outside both the crowd and his own ego. He can disagree with his group without despising it, and he can change his mind without losing himself, because his identity was never on loan from either. Scripture states the rule in a single line: “Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil” (Exodus 23:2). It states the means in another: “Be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind” (Romans 12:2).


Two further points in Bonhoeffer’s text are dropped by the video, and both guard against misuse. First, he concluded that his analysis forbids treating people as simply stupid. The condition is induced by circumstances, and much depends on whether those who hold power expect more from people’s stupidity or from their inner independence. That places a heavy responsibility on anyone who leads, whether a nation, a church, or a household. A leader who rewards agreement and punishes honest questions is manufacturing the very condition Bonhoeffer described, however good his cause. Second, in the same reflection Bonhoeffer warned against contempt for humanity, observing that the man who despises another can never make anything of him. Bonhoeffer’s purpose was to explain why argument fails and where the real remedy lies. Using “stupid” as a label for opponents, which relieves one of the duty to love and to reason with them, abandons that purpose. He also noted, soberly, that inner liberation usually has to wait for outer liberation. While the spell of power holds, persuasion mostly fails, and patience is not the same as surrender.


What follows for a Christian fellowship is fairly concrete. If the cost of thinking is exile, then the remedy at the community level is a community where exile is not the price of honesty. A fellowship ought to be the one place where a man can say the thing that will not be applauded and still belong, because its unity rests on a common Lord, not on a common opinion. Such a group does not produce uniformity. It produces men who can stand alone when they must, because they are not in fact alone.


The video closes with two good questions: when did you last change your mind about something that mattered, and when did you last say something to your own side that you knew would not be welcomed? Bonhoeffer would press one question further back. Before whom do you stand when you form your judgments? A man who cannot answer that will borrow his answers from whoever is nearest and loudest. A man who can answer it has found the beginning of wisdom.


---


*A note on sources.* The passage on stupidity comes from “After Ten Years,” written by Bonhoeffer at Christmas 1942 for a few fellow conspirators, several months before his arrest in April 1943. It was later printed at the front of *Letters and Papers from Prison*, which is likely why it is often described as written from his cell. He was executed at Flossenbürg on April 9, 1945.
