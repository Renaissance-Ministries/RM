# 260819 - Triangulating Truth

# The God Who Lives Us: A Theological Examination of a Dialogue


**Fellowship Essay |****19 August 2026****

****Evaluation of a conversation between Thomas Abshier and John**

**“Faith at the Boundary of Method” (see below)**

## Introduction


This essay is based on a conversation between John and me last Tuesday. That conversation is analyzed more philosophically in the essay below this one. This essay examines that same dialogue theologically: in this essay, I have stated each substantive point the participants raised, anchored it to Scripture, and evaluated it based on its relationship to Scripture. The governing observation is this: nearly every position taken in the conversation, both John’s position and mine, is anticipated within the Bible itself. eliever is not a modern invention; it occurs, in various forms, within the canon, which both diagnoses the skeptic’s position and dignifies his honesty. The evaluation offered throughout is that Scripture substantially confirms Thomas’s framework at its load-bearing points, that John’s positions are often Biblical *conditions* stated by a man who has not yet affirmed the Biblical authority and validity.


## I. The Limits of Willpower


**The point.** John contrasted two broken lives: the gym trainer who conquered alcoholism and obesity by “pure willpower,” and John’s father, who “hated life itself,” attempted suicide repeatedly, and was never restored. The implicit question, made explicit in the dialogue: can a man reform himself by will alone, or do some lives require rescue from beyond the self?


**Scripture.** The Bible affirms both halves of the observed data. Human resolve is real and commanded — *“Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might”* **(Ecclesiastes 9:10)** — and yet its limit is stated without sentiment: *“Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? then may ye also do good, that are accustomed to do evil”* **(Jeremiah 13:23)**. Paul supplies the interior phenomenology of John’s father: *“For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do… O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?”* **(Romans 7:19, 24)**. The answer to Paul’s question is not the will: *“I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me”* **(Philippians 4:13)**; *“without me ye can do nothing”* **(John 15:5)**.


**Evaluation.** The trainer and the father are not counterexamples to each other; they mark the two biblical categories. Common grace equips many for moral self-repair within a life that retains hope; Scripture never denies this, and the trainer’s testimony requires no supernatural explanation. But the settled hatred of being — what John rightly distinguished as a negation of life rather than a disorder within it — is precisely the condition for which Scripture reserves the language of deliverance rather than discipline. John’s father is Romans 7 without Romans 8. The theological weight of John’s own biography is that he possesses lifelong evidence that willpower has a floor through which some men fall, and his response — a childhood of total compliance offered as a substitute atonement (“maybe he’ll be happy if I do it”) — is a child’s unwitting enactment of the truth that someone must bear another’s brokenness. That office belongs to Christ: *“Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows”* **(Isaiah 53:4)**. The boy’s instinct was correct; only the appointed sin-bearer was misidentified.


## II. The Warning Dream


**The point.** Thomas recounted Grandma Mary’s 1922 dream that death awaited the family in the Prairie Grove house they had just purchased; the down payment was forfeited, the family left for California, and the child of the family that did move in died of the contaminated well. John neither affirmed nor dismissed it: “too chilling to discount.”


**Scripture.** The warning dream is an established biblical instrument of providence: Joseph was *“warned of God in a dream”* to flee to Egypt, and the child’s life was preserved **(Matthew 2:13)**; the wise men were *“warned of God in a dream that they should not return to Herod”*** (Matthew 2:12)**. Elihu states the doctrine generally: *“For God speaketh once, yea twice, yet man perceiveth it not. In a dream, in a vision of the night… he openeth the ears of men, and sealeth their instruction… to keep back his soul from the pit”* **(Job 33:14–18)**.


**Evaluation.** Thomas’s inference — that his father, and therefore his own existence and ministry, may have been preserved by direct divine warning — is not an extravagance; it is Job 33 applied to family history. The theological difficulty he honestly raised (why was Mary warned and the incoming family not?) is the difficulty of election in miniature, and Scripture declines to resolve it: *“The secret things belong unto the LORD our God”* **(Deuteronomy 29:29)**. John’s suspended judgment is, in this instance, the correct posture for one without the interpretive framework — but it should be noted that his refusal to discount the datum concedes the essential point: the event is evidence, and evidence accumulates. One warning dream is an anomaly; the biblical pattern plus the family instance plus the universal human testimony of such warnings is a triangulation.


## III. Materialism as the Costlier Faith


**The point.** Thomas argued that the physics community’s tacit position — mass simply “arose” or existed forever — is not methodological neutrality but a faith commitment; that its motive is “they don’t want to be subject to God, because if there is a God, there are rules”; and that it requires more faith to believe in materialism than in “Genesis and Jesus.” John agreed that such men “are not scientists” but “men of faith.”


**Scripture.** The argument is Romans 1 restated. *“The invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse: because that, when they knew God, they glorified him not as God… Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools”* **(Romans 1:20–22)**. The motive Thomas named is given in the same epistle: *“the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be”* **(Romans 8:7)**, and in the Gospel: *“men loved darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil”* **(John 3:19)**. The faith-structure of all cosmology is stated in Hebrews: *“Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear”* **(Hebrews 11:3)**.


**Evaluation.** The symmetry thesis — that materialists and theists are both believers, differing in object — is fully scriptural, and Hebrews 11:3 is its precise warrant: the origin of the visible from the invisible is known by faith on either account, since no observer attended the framing of the worlds. Thomas’s argument exceeds Romans 1 in its charity: Paul does not treat suppression as an intellectual error but as culpable refusal (“without excuse”), whereas the dialogue treats it as a correctable category confusion. The evaluation must also note the argument’s limit, which the conversation itself respected: demonstrating that materialism is faith does not demonstrate that Christianity is true; it clears the field of the pretense that unbelief is the neutral position. **Psalm 14:1** assigns the materialist confession its biblical genre — *“The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God”* — locating the utterance in the *heart*, the seat of commitment, exactly where Thomas located it.


## IV. Provisional Truth and the Nature of Faith


**The point.** John holds all truth provisionally — even a demonstrated God would be believed “only as long as it holds.” Thomas answered: “What you’ve just described is having faith,” and generalized that all functional commitments — to tomorrow, to the past, to an external world — are acts of faith.


**Scripture.** *“Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen”* **(Hebrews 11:1)**. *“For we walk by faith, not by sight”* **(2 Corinthians 5:7)**. *“For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part”* **(1 Corinthians 13:12)**.


**Evaluation.** Hebrews 11:1 vindicates Thomas’s reframing with precision: faith is there defined not as certainty but as *evidence* (elenchos — proof, conviction) of the unseen — that is, warranted commitment beyond direct observation, which is exactly the epistemic state John described and disowned the name of. First Corinthians 13:12 grants the provisionalist his strongest premise from within the faith: the believer also knows *“in part,”* and Thomas elsewhere applies the verse to himself (“I think I look through a glass darkly”). The genuine theological correction to be entered against both men’s usage is that biblical faith is not merely an epistemic posture but a personal fidelity: its object is not a proposition held provisionally but a Person trusted wholly — *“Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding”* **(Proverbs 3:5**). One may hold cosmological propositions provisionally while trusting God absolutely, as a child may be uncertain of his father’s reasons while certain of his father. The dialogue’s equation of the two faiths is epistemically true but covenantally incomplete.


## V. Statistics, the One-Off, and Abrahamic Commitment


**The point.** Against John’s claim to greater faith (in investments, in natural processes), Thomas distinguished ensemble-confidence (the Pachinko distribution over a million trials) from singular commitment (one ball, one spin), professing full faith in the former and none in the latter, since the variables of a one-off exceed all monitoring.


**Scripture.** *“Go to now, ye that say, To day or to morrow we will go into such a city, and buy and sell, and get gain: whereas ye know not what shall be on the morrow… For that ye ought to say, If the Lord will, we shall live, and do this, or that”*** (James 4:13–15)**. *“I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift… but time and chance happeneth to them all”* **(Ecclesiastes 9:11)**. And the counter-case: *“By faith Abraham, when he was called to go out into a place which he should after receive for an inheritance, obeyed; and he went out, not knowing whither he went”* **(Hebrews 11:8)**.


**Evaluation.** Thomas’s refusal of one-off market faith is James 4 exactly: the singular future is the Lord’s, not the statistician’s. But the distinction runs deeper than the investment question, and the unstated corollary deserves explicit theological statement: saving faith is itself a one-off—a singular, unrepeatable staking of a life, formally identical to the wager Thomas declines in markets. Abraham did not diversify. What makes the singular commitment of faith rational where the singular trade is not is the character of the counterparty: *“he is faithful that promised*” **(Hebrews 10:23)**. Ensemble-faith rests on the law of large numbers; covenant-faith rests on the faithfulness of God, which is not a statistical property but a personal one — *“God is not a man, that he should lie”* **(Numbers 23:19)**. The dialogue thus arrived, without naming it, at the correct location of religious warrant: not in the ensemble, and not in the blind one-off, but in the trustworthiness of the One on whom the single bet is placed.


## VI. Testimony as Evidence


**The point.** Thomas argued that the vast, structurally identical population of conversion testimonies — ruin, futile remedies, decisive encounter, drastic transformation — constitutes evidence by convergence (“swarm” logic), lowering the plausibility of hallucination or placebo, while carefully limiting the claim: transformation “may” follow, it is “not cause-effect,” and the proper speech-act is invitation.


**Scripture.** Scripture institutionalizes convergent testimony as its own evidentiary standard: *“In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established”* **(2 Corinthians 13:1; cf. Deuteronomy 19:15)**. The healed man’s testimony is the canonical form of the argument’s individual unit: *“one thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see”* **(John 9:25)**. The apostolic proclamation is explicitly testimonial: *“That which we have seen and heard declare we unto you”* **(1 John 1:3)**; *“For we have not followed cunningly devised fables… but were eyewitnesses of his majesty”* **(2 Peter 1:16)**. The eschatological verdict on the method: *“they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb, and by the word of their testimony*“** (Revelation 12:11)**. And Thomas’s crucial “may”: *“But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God”*** (John 1:12)**, with the Spirit’s sovereign unpredictability — *“The wind bloweth where it listeth… so is every one that is born of the Spirit”* **(John 3:8)**.


**Evaluation.** The swarm argument is not a modern apologetic imposed on Scripture; it is Scripture’s own canon of proof, extended from courtroom to phenomenon. The evaluation should sharpen one element the conversation left implicit: the *independence* of the witnesses is the argument’s engine, and Christian testimony satisfies it unusually well — the converts span every century, culture, class, temperament, and prior religion, which is exactly the profile that defeats explanations from social contagion. John’s dread in the Boise car — waiting to be asked for his own account — is itself a datum the argument predicts: testimony demands testimony, and the man without one feels the vacancy. His honesty about the dread is more evidentially interesting than his skepticism.


## VII. The Cross as “Sales Pitch”


**The point.** John’s candid reading: the Gospel narrative of maximal sacrifice struck him as “a big sales pitch… brilliant” — a pedagogical exaggeration for “people that are not that bright,” who cannot grasp abstractly that life requires sacrifice.


**Scripture.** Paul states John’s thesis before John does, and assigns it: *“For the preaching of the cross is to them that perish foolishness; but unto us which are saved it is the power of God”* **(1 Corinthians 1:18)**. *“The Greeks seek after wisdom: but we preach Christ crucified… unto the Greeks foolishness”* **(1 Corinthians 1:22–23)**. And the inversion: *“God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise”* **(1 Corinthians 1:27)**; *“the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned”* **(1 Corinthians 2:14)**.


**Evaluation.** John’s reading is anticipated verbatim in the canon — which is theologically significant in both directions. It confirms that his reaction is the *expected* reaction of the educated Greek mind, not a modern discovery that embarrasses the faith; and it warns that the expected reaction is diagnostic rather than probative: foolishness-to-the-perishing is what the cross *predicted* it would look like from outside. John’s specific claim — that the story exists for the simple — inverts the actual sociology twice over: the argument of the Twelve Postulates is that the cross is intelligible only to the most rigorous examination, and Paul’s argument is that its apparent folly is aimed precisely at humbling the wise, not accommodating the dull. The evaluation must also credit John’s instinct: he correctly perceived that the cross is a *communication* —* “God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us”* **(Romans 5:8)**. He mistook the rhetoric of ultimate love for the rhetoric of sales; the genre error is understandable in a man who has only ever been sold to.


## VIII. The Lament and Its Presupposition


**The point.** John’s governing sorrow: “if it all is not true, it’s just sad to me that human beings need something that isn’t true just to maintain their sanity.” Thomas identified the presupposition: the lament pre-judges the something as imaginary — “it wasn’t an evidence-based statement; it was a preconception.” John conceded, restating it as a possibility; Thomas completed the symmetry: “it’s possible that it is.”


**Scripture.** Paul himself asserts John’s conditional, from inside the faith: *“If Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain… If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable”* **(1 Corinthians 15:14, 19)**. And on answering before hearing: *“He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him”* **(Proverbs 18:13)**.


**Evaluation.** This is the dialogue’s most theologically elegant moment, because John’s lament is *biblically licensed as a conditional*. Paul agrees without reservation: if it is not true, Christians are pitiable, and the sadness is warranted. The entire dispute therefore compresses into the antecedent — and Paul stakes the antecedent not on pragmatic benefit but on testimonial fact: *“he was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve: after that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once”* **(1 Corinthians 15:5–6)** — the swarm argument again, at the faith’s foundation. The theological verdict on the exchange: John’s sorrow is the sorrow Paul commands us to feel under the hypothesis; his error was holding the hypothesis as a settled premise; his concession under Thomas’s correction was an act of intellectual repentance in miniature — the very motion (metanoia, the changed mind) that conversion scales up.


## IX. Sacrifice as Investment


**The point.** John naturalized sacrifice as *investment* — deferred gratification for long-term return — reading ancient ritual as misattributed causal work (Peterson), Socrates’ cock to Asclepius as satire, and his own marital fidelity as a ledger: the “penny” of the available woman against the “countless millions” of a lifetime with Jeannette. Thomas reframed: the sacrifice asked of us is the surrender of unhealthy pleasures, and its ground is that God, who lives us, receives the long-term joy.


**Scripture.** Scripture is strikingly unembarrassed by investment logic: *“The kingdom of heaven is like unto treasure hid in a field; the which when a man hath found, he hideth, and for joy thereof goeth and selleth all that he hath, and buyeth that field”* **(Matthew 13:44)**. *“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost?”* **(Luke 14:28)**. *“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”* **(Mark 8:36)**. Even the atonement itself is described in the ledger’s grammar: *“who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame”* **(Hebrews 12:2)**. Moses *“esteemed the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt: for he had respect unto the recompence of the reward”*** (Hebrews 11:26)**. Yet the corrective is equally canonical: *“To obey is better than sacrifice”* **(1 Samuel 15:22)**; *“Thou desirest not sacrifice… The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit”* **(Psalm 51:16–17)**; *“though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing”* **(1 Corinthians 13:3);** *“I beseech you therefore, brethren… that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service”* **(Romans 12:1)**.


**Evaluation.** John’s investment theory is far more biblical than either man noted in the moment: Jesus routinely argues from reward, and Hebrews applies the identical calculus to the cross itself. The theory’s deficiency is not its ledger but its *beneficiary*. In John’s economy the return accrues to the self (fulfillment, conscience, the marriage’s millions); in the biblical economy the transaction terminates in a Person — the field is bought for the treasure, and the treasure is communion. First Corinthians 13:3 is the decisive audit: a maximal sacrifice with no love profits *nothing* — the ledger without the Beloved does not merely underperform; it zeroes out. Thomas’s reframe supplies exactly this missing term: the return on righteous sacrifice is God’s own enjoyment of the life He lives in us, which converts investment into gift and gift into worship. Romans 12:1 unites the two vocabularies — sacrifice that is *living*, bodily, and “reasonable” (logikēn — rational, calculated) — as if written to adjudicate this very conversation.


## X. Conscience and Its Lawgiver


**The point.** John’s bedrock: the clear conscience (his aunt: “the most important thing in life… if you don’t have that, you don’t have anything”) and honesty when discovery is impossible (Roberta’s grandfather). Thomas pressed beneath: a conscience can only be clear if something could cloud it — who constitutes the standard? John’s terminus: “my own self-respect; that’s as deep as I can go.” Thomas’s published evaluation added that habitual override can re-polarize the heart, so universal moral recognition is a datum, not a proof, and the question of its source remains.


**Scripture.** Paul supplies the precise mechanism: the Gentiles *“shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another”* **(Romans 2:15)**. The grandfather’s counsel: *“He that walketh uprightly walketh surely”* **(Proverbs 10:9)**; *“all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do”* **(Hebrews 4:13)**; *“there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed”* **(Luke 12:2)**. Paul’s own practice: *“herein do I exercise myself, to have always a conscience void of offence toward God, and toward men”*** (Acts 24:16)**. The degradation Thomas warned of: *“having their conscience seared with a hot iron”* **(1 Timothy 4:2)**. And the cleansing John’s economy cannot supply: *“How much more shall the blood of Christ… purge your conscience from dead works to serve the living God?”* **(Hebrews 9:14)**.


**Evaluation.** Romans 2:15 answers Thomas’s question in canonical form: conscience is a *witness*, and a witness testifies to something other than itself — the law written on the heart implies a Writer. John’s self-respect terminus is therefore not false but truncated: self-respect is real, and it is the phenomenal surface of the accusing-and-excusing court Paul describes; what it cannot do is convene itself. Two further biblical notes complete the evaluation. First, 1 Timothy 4:2 confirms Thomas’s caution against treating conscience as an infallible universal: the witness can be seared, which is why moral consensus is a swarm-datum rather than a proof. Second — and this is the pastoral edge the conversation approached but did not state — a merely clear conscience is not the Gospel’s ceiling. Paul: *“I know nothing by myself; yet am I not hereby justified: but he that judgeth me is the Lord”* **(1 Corinthians 4:4)**. John possesses the cleanest conscience natural diligence can produce; Hebrews 9:14 names the cleansing it still awaits.


## XI. “God Lives You”: The Divine Indwelling of Experience


**The point.** Thomas’s signature doctrine: God actually experiences every human body from the inside; righteous living gives God the long-term enjoyment of “living you”; self-respect and fulfillment are the phenomenal surface of this indwelling.


**Scripture.** *“For in him we live, and move, and have our being”* **(Acts 17:28)**. *“Know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you… therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God’s”* **(1 Corinthians 6:19–20)**. *“I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me”* **(Galatians 2:20**). *“Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me”* **(Matthew 25:40)**. *“And he is before all things, and by him all things consist*” **(Colossians 1:17)**.


**Evaluation.** The doctrine’s scriptural footing is broader than a proof-text: Acts 17:28 places all human existence *within* God; Matthew 25:40 makes Christ the receiver of what is done to every human body, which is the experiential claim in judicial form — the Judge testifies that He underwent what the least underwent. Galatians 2:20 states the redeemed case explicitly. The necessary theological guardrail, which Thomas’s larger system must and does maintain, is the Creator-creature distinction: the God who lives us is not exhausted by us — *“the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee”* **(1 Kings 8:27)**; *“my thoughts are not your thoughts”* **(Isaiah 55:8)**. Rightly bounded, the doctrine is panentheistic in the sense of Acts 17 (all things in God) and never pantheistic (God merely the sum of things); the conversation’s formulation — God as the Experiencer who remains the Standard — keeps the boundary. Its ethical yield is immense and thoroughly biblical: every private act becomes an act toward God **(Psalm 51:4**, *“Against thee, thee only, have I sinned”)*, and every service to neighbor becomes service to Christ (Matthew 25). The doctrine does not merely ground conscience; it re-personalizes the entire moral field.


## XII. Qualia, the Swarm, and the Realm of God


**The point.** The being of red — with cinnamon’s taste and the caress’s sweetness — will never yield to experiment; spectroscopy measures 640 nanometers, not the experience. Perception requires polarity (“if something is always white, you don’t know it’s ever white”). The uniformity of qualia across all perceivers defeats the inverted-spectrum and group-hallucination hypotheses by swarm analysis, indicating “an underlying something that actually is true.” Conclusion: “The realm of God existing is in the same realm as red” — maximally certain from the inside, constitutionally inaccessible to third-person method.


**Scripture.** On the divine origin of the perceptual faculties themselves: *“He that planted the ear, shall he not hear? he that formed the eye, shall he not see?”* **(Psalm 94:9)**; *“The hearing ear, and the seeing eye, the LORD hath made even both of them”* **(Proverbs 20:12)**. On polarity as creation’s method: *“And God divided the light from the darkness”* **(Genesis 1:4)**; *“I form the light, and create darkness”*** (Isaiah 45:7)**; *“God also hath set the one over against the other”* **(Ecclesiastes 7:14)**. On interior knowledge inaccessible to method: *“For what man knoweth the things of a man, save the spirit of man which is in him? even so the things of God knoweth no man, but the Spirit of God”*** (1 Corinthians 2:11)**; *“The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God”* **(Romans 8:16)**. On the invitation to first-person verification: *“O taste and see that the LORD is good”* **(Psalm 34:8)**.


**Evaluation.** The identification of God’s reality with the realm of qualia is the conversation’s most original apologetic move, and Scripture supports it at every joint. First Corinthians 2:11 draws exactly the parallel Thomas drew: the inner life of a man and the things of God share an epistemic type — knowable only by the indwelling spirit, never by external instrument. Psalm 34:8 is the biblical form of the conclusion: the imperative is *taste*, a first-person, non-transferable verification — the only kind the subject matter admits. Genesis 1 grounds the polarity condition: creation proceeds by separations (light/darkness, waters/waters, day/night), as if contrast were the grammar of manifestation — which is what Thomas’s structural condition asserts. Psalm 94:9 quietly reverses the materialist’s burden: the argument from planted ear to hearing God is precisely an inference from qualia to their Source. One caution completes the evaluation: **Romans 1:20** insists the invisible things are *“clearly seen… by the things that are made”* — general revelation licenses the triangulation outward from experience but forbids retreat into pure interiority; the God known like red is also the God whose handiwork the heavens declare publicly (Psalm 19:1). Thomas’s method honors both poles: private certainty, public convergence.


## XIII. Fact, Faith, and Speculation


**The point.** The dialogue jointly assembled a three-tier epistemology: demonstrated fact (the method’s domain); warranted faith (triangulated conviction beyond method); mere speculation (conjecture without convergent support). Thomas locates theism in the second tier by swarm-triangulation; and the word “speculation,” both agreed, wrongly imports an aura of fancy into warranted belief — as “myth” has been degraded into “falsehood.”


**Scripture.** *“Prove all things; hold fast that which is good”*** (1 Thessalonians 5:21)**. The Bereans *“were more noble… in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so”* **(Acts 17:11)**. *“Be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you”* **(1 Peter 3:15)**. *“For we have not followed cunningly devised fables”*** (2 Peter 1:16)**.


**Evaluation.** The three tiers are biblical practice made explicit. Scripture commands proving (tier one where available), commends reasoned faith (tier two — the “reason of the hope,” the searched scriptures), and repudiates the third tier by name (fables, *“profane and vain babblings,”* **1 Timothy 6:20**). Second Peter 1:16 is especially pointed for this dialogue: the apostle explicitly denies that the faith is myth-in-the-degraded-sense and stakes it on eyewitness testimony — placing Christianity, by its own founding documents, in the evidence-bearing tier and inviting exactly the scrutiny John’s method demands. The evaluation may fairly conclude that John’s method, consistently applied to the actual evidentiary corpus of the faith, is not the faith’s enemy but its assigned examiner: *“Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD”* **(Isaiah 1:18)**.


## XIV. The Twelve Postulates and the Necessity of the Atonement


**The point.** Thomas’s Twelve Postulates argue that Incarnation, unrighteous death, and resurrection were not merely actual but *necessary* — “you couldn’t have created a universe any other way” — reverse-engineering the cosmos from the datum of the atonement, in answer to his lifelong question: “What kind of world are we living in, where that could even be true?”


**Scripture.** The New Testament itself provides the vocabulary. *“Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?”*** (Luke 24:26)**. Paul reasoned *“that Christ must needs have suffered” ***(Acts 17:3)***. “Without shedding of blood is no remission” ***(Hebrews 9:22)**. The atonement’s logical structure — justice and justification simultaneously satisfied — is stated as a solved constraint problem: *“that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus”*** (Romans 3:26)**. The exchange formula: *“For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him”* **(2 Corinthians 5:21)**. And the project’s warrant against the charge of presumption: *“Come now, and let us reason together”* **(Isaiah 1:18)**, bounded by *“the secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us”* **(Deuteronomy 29:29)**.


**Evaluation.** The dei — the divine “must” — running through Luke and Acts establishes that necessity-of-atonement is not a rationalist intrusion but a dominical category: Christ Himself taught the Emmaus disciples that the suffering was *necessary*, and faulted them for not deriving it (*“O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken,”* **Luke 24:25**). Anselm’s cur Deus homo and Thomas’s Twelve Postulates stand in this commissioned tradition: faith seeking the understanding of what it already believes. Romans 3:26 confirms that the atonement has an intelligible internal logic — God’s justice and mercy are reconciled, not merely juxtaposed — which is precisely what a postulate-set attempts to expose. The boundary is Deuteronomy 29:29: the revealed logic may be traced; the unrevealed depths may not be exhausted; and any derivation must remain corrigible to the text it explicates. Against John’s “sales pitch,” the Postulates constitute the maximal counter-claim: the cross is not rhetoric for the simple but the unique solution to the constraint equations of a moral universe — foolishness to the Greeks, wisdom to the examined (1 Corinthians 1:24).


## XV. Heidegger, the Question of Being, and the I AM


**The point.** Heidegger’s thesis — philosophy since Plato attended to beings and forgot Being, “the fact that anything is at all,” with technological thinking issuing in nihilism — was recognized by both men as the question the whole evening had been asking: What is the being of red? What is mass? What am I in all this? Alongside stood Heidegger’s counsel of presence (live in the washing of the dishes), the authenticity question (one’s own motivations versus the mob), and the biographical scandal of his Nazism and the Arendt affair.


**Scripture.** The question of Being receives, in the canon, a Name for its answer: *“And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM”* **(Exodus 3:14)** — Being self-declared as a Person. *“Before Abraham was, I am”* **(John 8:58)**. *“By him all things consist”* **(Colossians 1:17)**; *“Thou art worthy, O Lord… for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created”*** (Revelation 4:11)**. On presence in the task: *“And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men”* **(Colossians 3:23)**. On authenticity against the mob: *“Be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind”* **(Romans 12:2)**; *“For do I now persuade men, or God?… for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ”* **(Galatians 1:10)**. On the forgetting itself: they *“worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator”*** (Romans 1:25)**. On brilliance without righteousness: *“Knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth”*** (1 Corinthians 8:1)**; *“Though I understand all mysteries, and all knowledge… and have not charity, I am nothing”* **(1 Corinthians 13:2**).


**Evaluation.** Heidegger asked the right question and stood in the wrong bush. The philosopher who recovered the astonishment that anything is at all had, in Exodus 3, a predecessor who met the astonishment answering back — and the biblical claim is precisely that Being is not an “it” to be thought but an “I” who addresses: the question of Being has an Answerer, which is where the two friends located their entire disagreement. Heidegger’s presence-in-the-task is **Colossians 3:23** without its *“as to the Lord”*; his authenticity is Romans 12:2 without the renewing Mind; his agrarian piety is Sabbath-longing without the Lord of the Sabbath — in each case the structure survives, and the Person is deleted, which is Romans 1:25 performed at the highest level of sophistication. His moral catastrophe then serves as the evaluation’s exhibit: the man who thought Being most profoundly in his century joined its century’s most efficient destroyers of beings, confirming 1 Corinthians 13:2—all mysteries without charity is nothing—and demonstrating that unrepentant ontology is no safeguard. The Arendt coda, two enemies-by-category forgiving across the abyss, gestures at the one thing Heidegger’s philosophy could not derive and the Gospel makes central: *“forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you”* **(Ephesians 4:32)**.


## XVI. Beauty, Trained Senses, and the Divine Experiencer


**The point.** John denied objective beauty, then undermined that denial: appreciation is a function of formation—the trained ear hears what the untrained cannot. Thomas resolved the tension: God is the Experiencer; each nervous system, native or cultivated, is a differently trained organ of His experiencing; aesthetic formation is the education of God’s instruments. John’s historicism (each era’s music voices its “groove”) and Peterson’s dictum (“you don’t hold ideas; ideas hold you”) closed the evening.


**Scripture.** On formed discernment: *“But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil”* **(Hebrews 5:14)**. On objective beauty grounded in God: *“The heavens declare the glory of God”* **(Psalm 19:1)**; *“worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness”* **(Psalm 96:9)**; “*whatsoever things are lovely… think on these things”* **(Philippians 4:8)**. On ideas that hold men: *“Casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ”* (2 **Corinthians 10:5)**; *“Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men”* **(Colossians 2:8)**; *“For as he thinketh in his heart, so is he*” **(Proverbs 23:7)**;* “And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free”* **(John 8:32)**.


**Evaluation.** Hebrews 5:14 is the exact biblical warrant for John’s own observation against his own subjectivism: the senses are *exercised by use* to discern — discernment is real, trainable, and ranked, which entails a standard being discerned. Philippians 4:8 commands attention to the lovely as a category, unintelligible if loveliness is merely private. Thomas’s resolution — the universal located in the Experiencer rather than the object — harmonizes the data: tastes differ as instruments differ, while the One who experiences through all instruments grounds the reality of beauty itself; Psalm 96:9 unites the terms, beauty and holiness, in a single act of worship. The Peterson dictum receives its full biblical weight in 2 Corinthians 10:5: Scripture agrees that ideas hold men — it calls them strongholds and imaginations — and prescribes the only emancipation the dialogue’s own logic permits: not the exchange of one holding idea for another, but captivity to the Truth who is a Person (John 14:6), whose holding is freedom (John 8:32, 36). The evening’s final theological word is thus its quietest: two men examining the ideas that have them were, on the biblical account, being examined — *“the word of God is quick, and powerful… a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart”* **(Hebrews 4:12)**.


## Conclusion


Evaluated theologically, the conversation resolves into a single finding: John’s positions are Scripture’s conditionals, and Thomas’s positions are Scripture’s conclusions. The lament (“if it is not true…”) is 1 Corinthians 15:19 with the antecedent unexamined; the investment theory of sacrifice is Hebrews 12:2 with the Beneficiary deleted; the clear conscience is Romans 2:15 with the Lawgiver unnamed; the provisional method is Acts 17:11 awaiting its Berean object; the question of Being is Exodus 3 before the bush speaks. Nothing John holds must be surrendered for him to come; everything he holds must merely be completed. That is the classical shape of the Gospel’s approach to the honest pagan — *“Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you”* **(Acts 17:23)** — and it is the theological justification for the dialogue’s method of clarification over conquest. The conversation’s own deepest doctrine supplies its closing assurance: if God indeed lives every man, then He was present in the dialogue on both sides of it — the Experiencer of Thomas’s witness and of John’s honesty alike — and the completion of what John holds is not finally an argument’s work: *“No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him”* **(John 6:44)**. The friends’ obligation is the one they discharged: to reason together, to bear witness, and to leave the drawing to the Father — *“I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase”* **(1 Corinthians 3:6)**.

# Faith at the Boundary of Method: Philosophical and Theological Dimensions of a Dialogue


**Fellowship Essay: 19 August 2026

Conversation between Thomas Abshier and John (college friend)**


---


## Introduction


On its surface, the conversation is a wide-ranging exchange between two old friends covering local politics, longevity supplements, family history, and plans for a 1920s jazz orchestra. Beneath that surface, however, runs a sustained, philosophically serious dialogue between two well-defined epistemological positions: a provisional, method-bound empiricism, articulated by John, and a theistic realism grounded in what Thomas calls triangulation. The method seeks evidence of truth through the convergence of independent lines of evidence. By this method, that which cannot be observed directly (the existence of God, the validity of the Biblical message of salvation and sanctification, the reality of a conscious universe) exists by inference, but whose reality no single experiment can confirm. The conversation is notable because neither party is evangelizing in the ordinary sense. John repeatedly insists that he applauds religious belief in others and will never argue anyone out of the commitments that organize their lives; Thomas, for his part, does not press for conversion but for conceptual clarification — repeatedly asking John what his own terms (sacrifice, self-respect, fulfillment, speculation) ultimately rest upon. The result is a Socratic exchange in which the deepest disagreements turn out to be disagreements about the scope of the scientific method, the nature of faith, and the ontological status of experience itself. The dialogue culminates in Heidegger—a philosopher who focused on the “question of being.”


## I. Willpower, Inheritance, and the Problem of the Broken Life


The conversation’s first philosophical consideration is anthropological: what accounts for the difference between a life that recovers and a life that collapses? John recounts the testimony of a gym trainer who, by his own account, overcame alcoholism and hundred-pound obesity through “pure willpower.” He was inspired by stories of extraordinary discipline, such as the young ballplayer who hit five hundred balls a day regardless of illness or fatigue. The trainer’s creed was that when the will is total, success follows, because no one else will match that expenditure of effort. John remarked that the trainer’s son had received a great gift in witnessing his father weak and then witnessing him overcome. This was the gift that John never received.


John’s told the story of his father, who was overcome by brokenness. He was captured by an addiction, and “hated life itself,” attempted suicide repeatedly, and found nothing in existence enjoyable or good. The philosophical distinction John draws was between the trainer’s episodic addiction in a fundamentally upbeat person and a settled, global hatred of being. This is a distinction between a disorder within a life and a negation of life. John’s childhood response was to assume total responsibility for his father’s happiness — “I did everything he wanted me to do, every single thing, without arguing… maybe he’ll be happy if I do it.” John did not believe he was the cause of his dad’s dysfunction, but thought maybe he could change him by pleasing him with compliance and excellent performance. Thomas observes that this pattern is common: the child attempts to rescue, or take responsibility for, the parent’s suffering. The question is whether human beings can, in fact, “get it together” by will alone, or whether some lives require a supernatural rescue, a belief in God and the hope and power that exceeds the self.


This reminded Thomas of what may have been a supernatural intervention in his family’s life, possibly one that impacted his very existence as a born entity. His paternal grandparents, Mack and Mary, had put a down payment on a house in Prairie Grove, Arkansas, in 1922. It was a significant investment for them as newlyweds. Prior to moving in, Grandma Mary dreamed that death awaited the family in their new home. Both Mack and Mary took her dream so seriously that they forfeited the down payment and left for California; the well proved contaminated, and a child of the family who moved in died. Thomas’s father could have been that child who died. Perhaps this was divine intervention, a rescue so important that God directly intervened in warning of the impending tragedy. Why was Mary warned and the new family was not? We don’t know, but we might not be meeting today if Grandma Mary hadn’t had that dream and they heeded it.


John’s response is epistemologically exemplary of his whole stance: “I wish we could come down on… how that really works, if it works at all. I just don’t know. But it’s too chilling to discount.” The premonition story functions in the conversation as a datum. It is knowledge without mechanism. John’s refusal to affirm or dismiss it is an appropriate provisional acknowledgment of facts with no category that connects to known, accepted, and integrated cause-and-effect validity.


## II. Does Physics Demand Belief? Materialism as Faith


The explicit philosophical argument begins when Thomas raises John’s written objection to his August 1 essay: John had denied that physics “demands belief in anything.” The ensuing exchange carefully disambiguates this. John defends the scientific method as belief-free in principle: science proposes nothing without evidence, holds even its best-supported claims as temporary, and, on the question of the origin of mass, says only “we haven’t got a clue” — which is not a belief but an honest confession of ignorance. Thomas concedes that this is how science *should* speak, then relocates his claim: his target was never the method but the culture—what he calls “a culture of physics materialism.” John immediately ratified the distinction: there is the scientific method, and there is the scientific community, and they are not the same thing.


On this shared ground, Thomas builds his stronger thesis. The scientific community, he argues, enforces a taboo: any speculation about the origin of mass involving any theistic speculation is inadmissible in a physics paper and discredits its author. The tacit assumption that mass simply “arose” or “existed forever” passes without controversy. This asymmetry, he contends, is not methodological neutrality but a substantive metaphysical commitment.


John offers a sociological alternative — perhaps the community is merely closed-minded or politically captured. Thomas postulates that the motivational drive for materialistic explanation is that “they don’t want to be subject to God. They know that if there is a God, then there are rules imposed upon His creation.” John’s response is that anyone who reasons that way “is really not scientific.” Thomas agrees, as this was his point. They both agree that the materialist physicist is not a scientist but “a man of faith,” whose faith happens to be in materialism.


Thomas, quoting from his original essay: it requires *more* faith to believe in materialism — that everything popped out of nothing, formed itself, and became capable of thought, feeling, and meaning — than to accept “Genesis and Jesus.” John agrees: “Well said.” The argument’s structure deserves note: it does not attempt to prove theism. It attempts to erase the presumed asymmetry between the believer and the unbeliever by showing that the unbeliever’s position, at the level of ultimate origins, is equally a commitment beyond evidence — and, on Thomas’s accounting, a costlier one.


## III. The Anatomy of Faith: Provisionality, Statistics, and the One-Off


The conversation’s central conceptual move occurs when John articulates his epistemology of provisional truth. Even if he were persuaded by accumulated evidence that God exists, he explains, his assent would remain provisional — held “only as long as it holds,” permanently open to countervailing evidence, never a settled possession: “I don’t think of truth that way. Truth… needs to be continually refined over centuries.” Thomas’s reply reframes the entire discussion: “What you’ve just described is having faith.” He then generalizes: nothing is certain — not the truth of the Bible, not the nature of mass, not the arrival of tomorrow, not the reality of the past, not the existence of a world beyond one’s own neurons. All functional commitments to these things are “entirely acts of faith.” The believer and the empiricist are thus not divided by the presence or absence of faith but by its objects and its warrant.


John tests that statement: he claims to have exhibited, on occasion, *greater* faith than Thomas — for instance, faith in investments, or faith in natural processes generally. Thomas’s rebuttal introduces a genuine philosophical distinction: the categorical difference between statistical confidence and singular commitment (belief that a single choice will result in a desired outcome). He has complete faith that a Pachinko machine played a million times will yield a Gaussian distribution; he has no faith at all about where a single ball will land. Confidence in law-like regularity over ensembles is not the same act as staking oneself on a one-off outcome — “putting your money on red two and spinning the wheel” — is like betting on an outcome in a world whose relevant variables are too numerous to monitor. John’s professed faith in “natural processes,” on this analysis, is ensemble-faith masquerading as singular faith, and the investment case is precisely the singular kind.


The exchange leaves an implicit, unstated corollary hanging over the dialogue: religious faith, as Thomas practices it, is not ensemble-faith either—it is a singular, existential commitment, and its warrant must therefore come from somewhere other than statistics. The conversation’s later movements address where it comes from: triangulation.


## IV. Testimony, the Bandwagon, and the Logic of Religious Evidence


Thomas supplies part of that warrant in his treatment of testimony, describing (with reference to Susan, a member of his Sunday fellowship, who collects such accounts) the canonical structure of conversion narrative: a life not going well; many attempted remedies; a decisive experience — sometimes at the threshold of death — of God’s reality; and a subsequent transformation drastic enough to demand explanation. His analysis is carefully hedged and explicitly probabilistic. A single such story proves nothing; but the *pattern* — enormous numbers of independent instances (“evidenced by testimony and evidenced by bandwagoning”) converging on the same structure with the same magnitude of change — lowers the plausibility of deflationary explanations such as hallucination, placebo, or self-deception. He is scrupulous about the limits: transformation is not guaranteed (“the key word is *may*“), the phenomenon is “definitely not a cause-effect,” and the appropriate speech-act is invitation rather than promise: “This happened to me. I encourage you to try it too.” The argument is thus an inference to the best explanation operating over a population of testimonies — the same “swarm” logic he will later apply to the uniformity of color perception.


John’s counter-material is autobiographical and equally structured. He recounts the car ride to Boise during which two acquaintances traded testimonies of ruin and redemption through Jesus, embracing “like two Republicans that had never really spent quiet time with each other,” while John sat in dread of being asked for his own account. His interpretation of what he heard is the conversation’s most candid statement of the skeptic’s suspicion: the Gospel narrative struck him “almost like a big sales pitch… brilliant” — a story of maximal sacrifice engineered to make the necessity of sacrifice vivid “for people that are not that bright… that they just can’t get it otherwise.” On this reading, the Cross is a pedagogical exaggeration: a moral truth (that life requires sacrifice) packaged in an extreme narrative for those who cannot grasp the truth abstractly.


Two further autobiographical episodes complete John’s religious self-portrait. In the first, from his twenties, a churchgoing passenger in his car invited him to church; John replied that he already lived honestly and with good character and felt no need of it; the man answered, “If you’re that much of a man that you can live up to that kind of a standard without this, then I applaud you — but that’s a very unusual thing.” John has carried the exchange for fifty years, wondering whether he is genuinely the “one-off” the man described. In the second, prompted by Grok’s recommendation of the Ethical Culture Society as a non-religious ethical community (which John investigated and rejected as left-leaning nature-worship), John states his governing lament: when he hears that people needed religion to reassemble their lives, “if it all is not true, it’s just sad to me that human beings need something that isn’t true just to be able to maintain their sanity.” People *should* be able to get their lives together “without ever thinking about the universe.”


Thomas’s rejoinder is a precise piece of informal logic: John’s sadness presupposes the very conclusion at issue. To grieve that people needed “something that isn’t true” is to have already judged the something imaginary — “it wasn’t an evidence-based statement; it was a preconception.” John immediately concedes the overstep: he meant only that it *could* be untrue. Thomas completes the symmetry: “It’s always possible. But the other half is that it’s possible that it is.” The exchange models the dialogue’s ethic—a genuine correction, genuinely received—and it converts John’s lament from a verdict into an open question, which is exactly where Thomas’s theory of testimony wants it.


## V. Sacrifice: Investment, the Chicken of Asclepius, and Being Lived by God


The conversation’s richest theological exchange concerns sacrifice. Pressed by Thomas — if Jesus’s death is the exemplar of sacrifice, what sacrifice is the believer now called to make? — John develops a fully naturalized theory. “Sacrifice,” he argues, is an ancient term whose modern translation is *investment*: deferring immediate satisfaction for longer-term return. Drawing explicitly on Jordan Peterson, he treats the sacrificial systems of antiquity as confused externalizations of a real economy — the farmer who sacrifices to the gods for rain is, in fact, sacrificing his own leisure to plow the field, and the ritual misattributes the causal work. He seasons the account with Socrates’ dying instruction to offer a cock to Asclepius, which John reads as the philosopher’s final ironic joke at the expense of the whole apparatus — the absurdity of supposing that killing an unrelated animal improves one’s life. On this analysis, the word “sacrifice” is defective: it “emphasizes what you’re giving up, not what you’re investing for,” and is apt only “in the framework of someone who can’t think big enough.” Everything — diet, fidelity, education, service — is a trade-off; wisdom is the art of making the trades whose long-term return is fulfillment. His concrete example is marital: confronted hypothetically with an available young woman, he runs the ledger — the decades of trust with Jeanette, the corrupted conscience whether or not she ever learned, the “penny back” against “countless millions” — and passes. The calculation, he says, is as deep as he can go.


John cites his aunt to validate this valuation (“the most important thing in life is not health but a clear conscience; if you don’t have that, you don’t have anything”) and his former girlfriend’s grandfather (“be honest when nobody could ever find out — that’s when you need it most”).


John treats a clear conscience as a bedrock value. But a conscience can only be clear if something exists that could cloud it; who or what constitutes the standard?


Thomas accepts the psychology and then asks what lies *beneath* the motivation for a clean conscience. If John’s fidelity is finally grounded in “self-respect,” what grounds self-respect? Why does a clear conscience mean anything at all?


Thomas agrees with John’s evaluation of a clear conscience and justifies the experience of his stand using his signature theological formulation: God actually experiences your body—**God lives you.** The sacrifice of the unhealthy pleasure is therefore not merely a trade within one life’s balance sheet of pleasure and pain; it is the choice to give God, who undergoes every human experience from the inside, the long-term enjoyment of “living you” well rather than the short-term pleasure of the transgression. Self-respect, on this account, is the phenomenal surface of a deeper ontological fact: the divine indwelling of experience.


John’s response is: “I have no problem with that, but for me, it’s more metaphorical.”


Thomas’s counter-question — *if it is a metaphor, what is the reality underneath the metaphor?* John replies with his final position: “my own self-respect; that’s as deep as I can go.”


The disagreement is thereby located not over how to live, on which the two men substantially agree, but over whether the ground floor of moral experience is the self or God. My sense is that the human heart can adopt almost any moral polarity given enough exposure to an environment and practice. In other words, what was once the prick of conscience of the God within can be overridden by habitually overriding its voice. The postulate that there is good and evil, Godly and unGodly, cannot be proven by the fact of a common, near-universal recognition of good and evil behavior. Empirics can only place it as one dot on the swarm analysis graph. It is not a proof; it is a datapoint, and the question remains: what is the underlying source of that species-wide consistency?


# VI. The Problem of Red: Qualia, the Swarm, and the Limits of Method


From ethics the dialogue ascends to what contemporary philosophy of mind calls the hard problem of consciousness, though neither speaker uses the term. Thomas introduces it through a spontaneous analogy: the sense that a clear conscience matters belongs to the same family of questions as “where does red come from? where does the taste of cinnamon come from? where does the enjoyableness of a caress come from?” These questions concern the experienced quality of experience, and Thomas claims they all share a single source. His constructive proposal is twofold.


First, a structural condition: perception requires polarity — “if something is always white, you don’t ever know it’s white.” Every possible experience presupposes a contrast-structure, and that contrast-structure is not incidental; God established polarity as reality’s underlying nature. Creation proceeds by distinctions, and perception lives on them.


Second, the thing left unknown, the missing concept. Granting photoreceptors, rhodopsin, and optic nerves, the question of how the *appearing* of red arises is grasped only lightly: what is the substance underlying that perception? The Conscious Point Physics theory, as presently formulated, posits no entity that carries red, cinnamon, or pungency. This is the frontier Thomas confronts constantly. The engineering of qualia — “how did He do that?” — is presented as a genuine open problem. (Note: I have below, within this section, attempted to elaborate on this concept and offer a plausible perspective by which the intangibility of qualia may be processed and presented to the observer for conscious experience.)


John raises the classical inverted-spectrum hypothesis: perhaps each person has lived a lifetime seeing “red” where another sees “yellow,” with no possibility of comparison. Thomas’s dismissal introduces a named methodological instrument from his physics: **swarm analysis**. When a thousand people living utterly different lives converge on identical judgments and identical reactions, the convergence itself is evidence of a common underlying reality rather than coordinated private variation. The inverted spectrum is “interesting philosophy and science fiction — it might be true, but I don’t think so.” John supplies the parsimony argument that ratifies the verdict: members of one species, with a common brain design, are more rationally presumed to perceive alike than differently.


Thomas then states his epistemology: no one ever perceives anything but internal brain stimulation. “The only perception we have is of the brain inside the cranium; we only experience the activation patterns of the brain from sensory stimulation. That is all anyone ever sees.” The existence of the external world is therefore an inference — an extrapolation from the consistency of the inner display. What licenses the inference is the display’s uniformity across all perceivers: the fact that we all triangulate on the same world and call it by the same names makes it overwhelmingly unlikely that each person’s color, taste, tone, and touch are private inventions. The uniformity of perception argues for a single underlying reality that all souls perceive in common.


That conclusion opens directly onto the metaphysical hypothesis at the center of Thomas’s system: the single underlying reality is a single underlying *consciousness* — God’s existence as the only existence. Each of us filters that one reality through the senses, through the machinery of neural transmission, and through the neural-net weighting of sensory, memory, and meta-cognitive inputs. The individual perceiver is not the ground floor of reality; he is a localized perspective within it.


The mechanism Thomas proposes for this localization is the aggregation of micro-consciousness. The elemental units are the postulated Conscious Points: each capable of perceiving, remembering, processing, and acting upon the states of other Conscious Points. Their aggregates take two visible forms. Some aggregates are living beings possessing a sense of “I.” Others are the Conscious Points composing ordinary mass, whose internal coordination is so simple, diffuse, and static that it fully disguises their fundamental nature as conscious entities. On this postulate, the macro-consciousness we each know as “I” — a unified point of view that recognizes itself as a perceiver and recognizes others as aggregates of the same type — arises from an alliance of micro-consciousnesses. The localization of self into a unified perspective, inside the larger body of consciousness that is the source of all perception, is the miracle of God creating “other” within Himself.


The creative sequence, so conceived, runs in two movements: atomization, then reassembly. God first individuates elemental units of perspective — the Conscious Points — and then assembles them into masses capable of perceiving self and other. The atomization is not an accident of the design; it is the required step. A fully individuated, self-aware, multi-perspective consciousness can only exist inside an only-existent, nothing-but-this-exists singular consciousness if the singular consciousness is first divided into elements and the elements are then recomposed. And one criterion governs the recomposition: disguise. The reassembled units must be aware of themselves without being aware of the unity that is the source of all awareness. The goal is creatures who genuinely experience being “other” — selves who do not see the seam joining them to the One.


Why would the One do this? The unifying fact of existence is that there is a single consciousness that is the source of all consciousness. But a solitary consciousness, perceiving only itself, unchanging and unopposed, is — if we may put it so — uninteresting even to itself. If we permit ourselves to anthropomorphize desire, the motive follows naturally: the desire for variety, for relationship, for perception of self from a vantage outside the self. Having established the motive, we can state the postulate: the Singularity — the One Consciousness, the self-existent One — internally individuated Himself by imagining, visualizing, and instantiating the perception of Himself from innumerable perspectives.


This framework yields a definition of qualia. The elemental units of perception assemble into coordinated units of consciousness, each with its own distinctive organizational type and functional capability, and each type of assembly produces a characteristic experience when perceived. That characteristic experience — the gestalt of a configuration of Conscious Points, as registered by another configuration of Conscious Points — is what we call the qualia of an experience. Perception is always an encounter between two assemblies: the perceiver and the perceived; each has its own configuration of elemental consciousness, and the relationship between the two configurations produces the felt quality of the encounter. “Red,” on this account, is not a property smeared onto photons; it is the gestalt of a particular receptor-assembly meeting a particular object-assembly, apprehended by the larger “I” within which both sub-assemblies report. There are likely subunits of awareness below the fully integrated consciousness we recognize as “I” — layers of perceptive capability whose contributions arrive already synthesized. Even mass itself fits the scheme: mass is the “I”‘s perception of those aggregates whose internal coordination is too simple and static to present as anything but object.


The alternative to all this is the brain-in-a-vat picture: each of us alone, experiencing nothing but input, the apparent world a private or collective hallucination. The swarm dismisses it. The convergence of all perceivers on one coherent world indicates “an underlying something that actually is true.” The structure of the argument should be noted: it is the testimony argument of Section IV transposed into the philosophy of perception. Convergent independent witnesses — whether to spiritual conversion or to redness — point beyond themselves to a common real, at once physical and spiritual.


The exchange then turns to John’s defense of his word “speculation,” which Thomas meets with a diagnosis: John has silently categorized all experience of life into the realm of things whose truth science can determine. He has promoted the scientific method from a tool into an ontology — an elemental construct upon which the world is built. John registers the observation as genuinely new: “I never thought — I have to think about that one.”


Thomas’s demonstration case is red itself. Spectroscopy can detect 640 nanometers, but the question was never the wavelength — it was the reality of the experience, and that will never be detected or determined by experiment. John spontaneously supplies the aesthetic parallel that seals the agreement: analyzing a piece of music can never answer “why did that cause my heart to swell?” Both men assent: no experiment reaches it. Thomas then draws the conclusion the whole evening had been preparing: **“The realm of God existing is in the same realm as red.”** Both exist in the realm of consciousness. Both are existent *as* consciousness. Both resist the kind of third-party interaction that would yield concrete evidence more identifiable than the experience itself. The existence of God is therefore not a defective scientific hypothesis awaiting evidence that never comes; it is a reality of the same *kind* as qualitative experience — maximally certain from the inside, constitutionally inaccessible to third-person method. John’s response — “Interesting. Okay. Very good point.” — is as close to concession as his provisionalism permits.


A lexical sub-dispute refines the demarcation. John protests the degradation of the word “myth” into “falsehood,” invoking Peterson’s dictum that fiction can be “more true than fact” — a story that never literally occurred may render a truth more accurately than any actual instance could. Thomas agrees that myth is “a dual-use word.” John then clarifies that his own use of “speculation” was never dismissive — a speculation “could very well be the truth,” lacking only agreed-in-advance reproducible evidence. Thomas offers the final distinction: a claim supported by enough data points triangulating back to it deserves the name *faith* rather than speculation, since “speculation” carries an aura of fancy that misdescribes warranted but unprovable belief. The two men thus jointly assemble, almost inadvertently, a three-tier epistemology: (1) demonstrated fact — the scientific method’s domain; (2) warranted faith — triangulated conviction beyond the method’s reach; and (3) mere speculation — conjecture without convergent support. Thomas holds that theism belongs to the second tier: the swarm of convergent witness, inner and outer, triangulates its conclusion as existent reality.


## VII. The Twelve Postulates and the Intelligibility of Atonement


Twice in the conversation Thomas gestures toward his recent theological work, the “Twelve Postulates,” a document (sent to John as an attachment) articulating his conception of *why* the Incarnation, unrighteous death, and resurrection of Jesus were not merely actual but necessary — “you couldn’t have created a universe any other way.” He frames it as the terminus of a sixty-year search that began with the question that defined his own path to belief. Confronted with the Biblical claim that Jesus died for our sins, “what kind of world are we actually living in, where that could even be true?” The question is methodologically distinctive: it treats the central Christian datum not as an article to be swallowed but as a constraint on cosmology. If the atonement is true, the universe must be the kind of place in which it *could* be true, and the theologian’s task is to reverse-engineer that universe. Within the conversation, the Postulates function as Thomas’s answer to John’s categorization of the story of the Cross as a “sales pitch.” John sees the Bible and belief in such a mythology as a pedagogical exaggeration for the simple-minded. Thomas claims a structural necessity intelligible only to the rigorous examination of the empirically observed structure of the universe and how it intersects with its Biblically/divinely revealed structure. The same event is read as being worthy of opposite ends of the intellectual dignity spectrum. The dialogue does not review or recapitulate the substance of the twelve postulates. They are noted as being consistent with:


- 1) Thomas’s physics, the fundamental construction of the universe from Conscious Points whose essence is arguably of the substance of the mind of God;

- 2) his corollary doctrine that God experiences every body because there is no substance or being that is not integral with His being/Consciousness/essence; and

- 3) his rationalization of life, unjustified death, and resurrection as the ritual required to meet the boundaries of God’s character of total purity; His desire for reciprocal love given freely; and the necessity of a justifiable and attractive “other lover” that gave men an option to love more than God.

- These elements of reality, when assembled logically, rationalize the arc of the Biblical narrative with its culmination in sacrifice (giving up of the opportunity to live in Heaven in glory, live on earth with respect and justice) to open the possibility that God’s wrath at sin could be satisfied in quantity and direction by the substitutionary death of Christ. “For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.” 2 Corinthians 5:21

- The entire Biblical ontology (the existent reality of the universe as revealed and implied by the Bible) becomes rational through understanding the structure of the universe, the nature of God, and how these factors dictated His being.


## VIII. Heidegger: The Question of Being as the Conversation’s Mirror


The dialogue’s philosophical guest arrives late and functions as its retrospective interpreter. John, who has been “listening to Heidegger” through Grok, presents him as a man of contradictions: he bought into the Nazi movement, yet was thoughtful and had interesting ideas. John’s summary conveys three Heideggerian themes.


- First, the critique of technological modernity and the romance of agrarian dwelling: Heidegger’s counsel not to treat washing dishes as an impediment to be gotten past but to “live in the actual action of what you’re doing — otherwise you’re shortchanging yourself on your life.”

- Second, authenticity: Grok’s pointed question to John — would Heidegger approve of your life? Are you true to your own motivations, or “filling the pattern of society… carried away by the mob”? — is a serviceable paraphrase of Heidegger’s distinction between authentic existence and absorption in *das Man.* John’s self-examination reported that (he worked to fund the life he wanted, enjoying the process where he could) shows the question landing.

- Third, the biographical enigma of the Arendt affair — the passionate relationship between the philosopher who joined the Nazi party and the brilliant Jewish thinker, their mutual estrangement and strange late forgiveness, “he kind of forgave her for being a Jew, and she kind of forgave him for being a Nazi” — which John offers as a case where reality outruns fiction’s capacity for invention.


Thomas looked up Heidegger mid-conversation. Heidegger’s thesis is that philosophy from Plato onward attended to *beings* — things, objects, ideas — while forgetting *Being itself*: “the fact that anything is at all”; and that traditional metaphysics and technological thinking obscure Being, issuing in cultural nihilism. Thomas’s remarked: “This really is very similar to what we’ve been talking about — what is the *being* of red?” This reflects our opening question about nature/beingness: what IS mass?  The entire dialogue has been about beingness: 1) the taboo on asking where mass comes from by the scientific community; 2) the inscrutibility of “redness” as far as its origin and it unsatisfying irreducibility of the conscious experience of red to nanometers; 3) the insufficiency of a the ego and brain as the ultimate source and basis for conscience; 4) the proposition that “God is in the same realm as red.”


This is perhaps the distinction between sustained complaint that the modern intellectual settlement answers every question about beings while forbidding the question of Being. John’s gloss completes the convergence: Western thinking, he summarizes, studied all the phenomena and “completely forgot — wait a minute, what am *I* in all this?” Two men who disagree about God discover that they share Heidegger’s question; they differ on whether the question has an Answerer.


## IX. Aesthetics, the Spirit of an Age, and Ideas That Hold Men


The conversation’s final movement translates the evening’s metaphysics into aesthetics and philosophy of history. John, planning the revival of his 1920s jazz orchestra, denies objective beauty outright: “You can’t just say that something is objectively beautiful… it’s a very personal feeling, and you either get it or you don’t.” Yet his own elaboration undermines the pure subjectivism: appreciation, he argues, is a function of formation — “pleasure really comes down to how much thought you’ve given it before… your exposure to what leads up to a particular performance.” His marveling at a perfectly tuned sectional is available only to the trained ear that knows what the musicians are doing for one another; the untrained listener “may say they like it, they really don’t know why.” He honestly confronts the resulting puzzle: is a taste requiring more training thereby *better*? “I don’t know.” Thomas resolves the tension with his central theological instrument: God is “actually the experiencer,” and each human nervous system — some pleasures native to the body (sex, sugar, thrill, peace), others requiring cultivated discrimination — is a differently trained organ through which that experiencing occurs. Aesthetic formation, on this account, is the education of God’s instruments; subjectivity of taste and objectivity of the Experiencer are reconciled by locating the universal not in the object but in the One who undergoes all appreciations.


John’s philosophy of musical history supplies the sociological counterpart. Every style of music “reflects the mentality of an age”: the ecstatic, machine-metaphor jazz of the 1920s — the banjo’s chunk echoing the flivver engine, “a well-oiled machine” as the era’s highest compliment, saxophonists on biplane wings and Charleston dancers on skyscraper ledges — expressed a generation raised on unprecedented optimism, while the blues and later rage-forms express pent-up frustration and grievance foreign to that decade. Thomas’s harsher judgment of contemporary popular forms — “primitive… guttural… one note… the note of sex… little children” — is tempered by John into the more defensible historicist claim: the music is a faithful mirror of “whatever the groove is of the age.” Into this frame John sets his most philosophically consequential borrowing from Peterson: **“You don’t hold ideas; ideas hold you.”** His illustration is Calvin Coolidge, the twenties president possessed by the idea of a freewheeling prosperous society, who candidly confessed he couldn’t even *envision* the interventionist responses the Depression era demanded — “the idea had him, and the ideas moved on.” The dictum quietly reframes the entire evening: if ideas hold men rather than the reverse, then the materialism of the physics community, John’s method-bound provisionalism, and Thomas’s theistic realism are not merely positions the two friends hold but powers that hold them — and the dialogue itself, with its real concessions on both sides, becomes the rarer thing: two men attempting, together, to see the ideas that have them.


## Conclusion


The conversation enacts, with unusual completeness, the modern encounter between methodological empiricism and theistic metaphysics — and it does so without a single moment of rancor, sustained by John’s principle that he will never argue against the reasons anyone has for being good, and by Thomas’s practice of interrogating foundations rather than demanding assent. Its philosophical yield is a set of jointly constructed distinctions of genuine value: scientific method versus scientific community; ensemble-faith versus one-off commitment; demonstrated fact, triangulated faith, and mere speculation; myth versus falsehood; the wavelength of red versus the being of red. Its theological center is Thomas’s doctrine that God lives every human body and experiences every human experience — a claim that grounds conscience, transfigures sacrifice into gift, locates God’s existence in the same epistemic realm as qualia, and answers Heidegger’s forgotten question of Being with a divine Experiencer. Its unresolved remainder is John’s honest terminus: the metaphor works, the life it recommends is the life he already lives, but “my own self-respect — that’s as deep as I can go.” Between that floor and Thomas’s foundation the dialogue does not force a decision; it does something more durable, clarifying exactly where the floor lies and exactly what would have to be true for there to be something beneath it. The final image belongs to both men equally: two friends who agree that no experiment will ever explain why the heart swells at music, disagreeing — provisionally, faithfully — about Whose heart it is.
