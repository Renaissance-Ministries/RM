### 2. DME strategy — decision reversed

**Yesterday's plan:** Apply for your own DME now while selling through third parties during the wait.

**What surfaced today**

A DME license is state-by-state, not national. This was the fact that turned the decision, and Thomas named it directly: "I was thinking it was nationwide, but it's not. That was simply wrong."

The three layers behave differently:

- **Medicare billing: travels.** DMEPOS claims go to the DME MAC for the jurisdiction where the beneficiary lives, not where the supplier sits. A California-enrolled supplier can bill for a patient in Ohio.
- **State licensure: does not travel.** Supplier Standard 1 requires compliance with all applicable state licensure requirements, and many states require out-of-state DME suppliers to register. California publishes Form CDPH 8679O specifically for out-of-state Home Medical Device Retailer registration. Illinois runs 10–12 weeks plus a $10,000–$50,000 bond for out-of-state suppliers.
- **Medicaid: fifty separate programs, fifty enrollments.** A Medi-Cal provider number does nothing in Texas.

Net effect: owning a California DME would let you serve California well and everywhere else badly.

**The facility must be real before the application, not after.** The gating item isn't CMS — it's accreditation. CMS eliminated the operational grace period effective January 1, 2026; new locations must be fully surveyed before accreditation is granted, with no temporary status. Surveys are unannounced and include records review, staff interviews, beneficiary interaction, and inventory inspection. You cannot be surveyed in an empty room.

**The real sequence:**

| Month | What must be true |
|-------|-------------------|
| 0 | Lease signed, build-out done, permanent signage up, landline installed and listed, records storage in place, policies and procedures written |
| 0–1 | Person hired and working posted hours (~30 hrs/week); California HMDR and exemptee license obtained |
| 1–2 | Apply to accrediting organization; inventory on hand |
| 3–6 | Unannounced survey, then accreditation decision |
| 6 | Now you can file CMS-855S |
| 9–12 | Enrollment completes, PTAN issued, first billable claim |

Nine to twelve months of rent and wages before one dollar comes back — roughly $45,000–$60,000 spent before the first claim, on top of about $10,000 in fees. A failed survey restarts the clock and you keep paying through the corrective action.

**Rifton doesn't own a DME.** They sell through Adaptive Mall, SpinLife, RehabMart, and Medical E Shop — four named dealers alongside an internal direct sales team — and publish LMN templates and a funding guide so local dealers and families handle the state-specific machinery. That's an existing, working proof that the model doesn't require owning one.

**Decision:** Sell through third-party DMEs. Defer owning one until there is volume and capital to justify it.

**One open idea, and why it doesn't work**

Thomas floated filing the California application now simply to hold a place in the queue, in case CMS freezes enrollment again.

There is no queue to hold. The CMS-855S comes at month six, after accreditation, and accreditation requires a surveyed, staffed, operating facility. You cannot file early and wait. Getting a place in line means signing a lease and hiring someone today — which is the whole expense you just decided to defer.

The re-freeze risk is real but it's a reason to watch, not a reason to spend. If CMS announces another moratorium, applications already pending are exempt — but "pending" requires having filed, which requires the facility. The genuinely cheap hedge is a calendar reminder to check the Federal Register quarterly.

### 3. The pricing rule that resolves the paradox

Thomas stated the bind clearly mid-call: the product is too expensive for anyone to buy at retail, and if it were priced where people would actually buy it — around $6,000 — that becomes the price you must bill. Hogtied in both directions.

**The resolution: the constraint applies only to direct-to-consumer sales.**

| Sale type | Effect on future billable rate |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Direct to a family, cash | Caps your billing near that price, permanently, until published prices change |
| Wholesale to a DME dealer | None. A trade price is not your usual and customary rate. Every manufacturer has one |
| Sales to institutions and schools | None |

So: wholesale freely; be deliberate about direct-to-consumer pricing, because that's the one that follows you.

### 4. Reimbursement research — and a combination nobody else runs

**How Rifton operates:**

- Publishes sample Letters of Medical Necessity for every product (Pacer for school, Pacer for home care, Standard, Wave, Compass Chair, HTS), plus a funding guide aimed at families and therapists
- Bills everything under E1399, miscellaneous DME — no standard fee schedule, individual consideration, LMN required each time
- Sells through named DME dealers alongside an internal direct sales team
- The engine underneath is pediatric: EPSDT requires medically necessary DME for Medicaid beneficiaries under 21 even when not covered in the state plan. In schools, equipment is requested through an IEP and funded by a mix of Medicaid, district budgets, and private insurance

**The warning worth taking seriously:**

Rifton reimburses, at $3,000–$5,000. Biodex doesn't reimburse, at $12,900. Ideomotion is proposing $12,000–$16,000 and reimbursement — a combination neither of them runs.

That's a fork in the road. It may mean the reimbursement market wants a $5,000 product and the $12,000–16,000 product belongs in the cash, facility, and VA channels. Or it may mean there's a genuine gap. Either way, Isak's Medi-Cal and state-insurance calls are the highest-value research item on the list — if E1399 individual consideration for a walker lands at a few hundred dollars, the DME channel isn't worth building at all, and the answer changes everything downstream.

**A correction to earlier advice**

In the 28 August memo the recommendation was to firewall caregiver-injury language out of the federal file entirely. Rifton does the opposite — their Pacer LMN states the device has potential to save future medical costs from caregivers injuring their backs during lifting and transfers, and it appears inside the medical necessity letter.

The refinement: caregiver cost-offset works as a supporting argument alongside a primary patient-benefit justification. What still holds is that it must not be the primary framing, and the words that map to non-covered codes — patient lift, standing frame, transfer device — stay out regardless. Follow Rifton's structure here.

### 5. Channel sequence — who to approach first

The therapist, not the DME, not the physician.

A DME won't stock an unproven product with no code and no track record — that's inventory risk plus claim risk on their side, and a bad deal for them.

The referral chain: therapist evaluates → physician signs the order → patient goes to an enrolled DME supplier → supplier obtains prior authorization → supplier delivers and bills the payer → payer pays the supplier. The supplier has already paid Ideomotion at wholesale. Their 20–40% buys the claim risk, the denial, the appeal, and a 30–90 day float from delivery to payment — longer with a miscellaneous code.

Joelle's counterpoint is also right and not in conflict: call the DMEs early anyway, to learn the process and the timeline. If onboarding a new product takes months, you don't want to have sold a patient first and left them waiting.

Resolution: therapist-first for demand generation, DME-first for channel intelligence. Both start this week.

### 6. Economics of the DME channel

Thomas's working model:

- 15 units/month × ~$3,000 margin = $45,000/month
- Less current payroll of $16,000/month = ~$29,000/month toward inventory and other expenses
- Charlie reportedly sold roughly 15/month at a previous venture, so the volume isn't hypothetical

Working capital is the constraint. DMEs pay 30–90 days from delivery. At 10 units/month and $3,500 COGS that's $35,000/month tied up; three months of float is roughly $105,000. At 15/month it's closer to $150,000. Growth increases the capital requirement — the faster you sell, the more cash is trapped in units already shipped.

Thomas's estimate of total capital before self-sustainability is closer to $1M than $100,000, with a stated preference to avoid outside investors so that margin isn't diverted to servicing an investment.

The item that makes this model contingent: 15 units/month requires 15 soft goods sets per month. Susan cannot produce that and no vendor is identified. The soft goods vendor is now the gating item for the whole plan, not just the MVP. No vendor means no volume; no volume means the DME channel math never gets tested.
