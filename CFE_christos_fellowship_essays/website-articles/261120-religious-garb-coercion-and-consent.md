# 261120 - Religious Garb, Coercion, and Consent

# The Habit and the Hijab:


### Consent Is the Difference

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 20 November 2026

*A response to a Facebook post answering the Hodgetwins meme "A nun's habit is respected as devotion. A Muslim woman's hijab is called oppression."*

## The meme and the reply

A meme has been circulating under the Hodgetwins banner. It sets a smiling nun beside a smiling woman in a hijab and says: the nun's habit is called devotion, the Muslim woman's hijab is called oppression, same religious freedom, different reactions, a double standard worth questioning.

A Catholic friend, a former seminarian, answered it on Facebook. His answer, in short: nuns choose the habit and govern their own communities; Muslim women in much of the Islamic world have the hijab imposed on them by men; Iran punishes women who remove it with brutality; and the Catholic Church gives women real authority to found, rule and reform their own religious houses. He closed with a line from Father Benedict Groeschel: *"What do you call a young priest who picks a fight with an old nun?** A casualty."*

I stand with the substance of that reply. The meme is wrong, and it is wrong for a reason that can be stated in one word: consent. A garment is not devotion or oppression in itself. The same cloth is an offering when a woman chooses it and a yoke when a man or a state forces it on her. The meme hides the one variable that decides the question.

But the reply, as written, overreaches in a few places, and an overreach gives the other side an easy way to dismiss the true point. If Catholics (and the rest of us Christians) want to answer this "succinctly and truthfully," as my friend urged, the truth has to be kept in proportion. So I will grant what the meme gets right, show where it fails, correct what the reply overstated, and offer a short answer at the end.

## What the meme gets right

The strongest case for the meme is this: a Muslim woman in Dearborn or Minneapolis who puts on her scarf each morning because she believes God asks it of her is doing exactly what the nun does. She is making her faith visible. If we honor the one and sneer at the other, we are applying a double standard.

That case has real evidence behind it. Pew Research's survey of government restrictions found that far more countries *restrict* women's religious dress than *require* it: in 2012–2013, 39 of the 50 countries that regulated women's religious attire limited it, while 12 required it. France and several other European states have banned the face veil or the headscarf in schools and public offices, and some of those laws were argued on the ground that the hijab is oppressive regardless of whether the woman chose it. That argument treats a free adult as if she had no will of her own. It is the same disrespect the meme complains of, and on that point the meme is right.

So I grant it plainly: where a Muslim woman wears the hijab freely, she deserves the same courtesy we give the nun. A Christian who mocks her is not defending freedom. He is abandoning it.

## Where the comparison breaks: compulsion

The meme's error is to treat the free hijab in America as if it were the whole story. It is not. In two countries today the hijab is the law, enforced by the state.

In **Iran**, Article 638 of the Islamic Penal Code makes it a crime for a woman to appear in public without "Islamic hijab." In 2024 the regime passed a far harsher "Chastity and Hijab" law, with escalating fines, flogging, prison terms of up to 15 years, travel bans and employment penalties; Amnesty International reported that some of its provisions carried the death penalty. Iran's Supreme National Security Council halted that law in December 2024, fearing the unrest it would cause, and in 2026, under war and economic strain, the regime has hesitated to enforce even the old rules strictly. Many Iranian women now go uncovered in open defiance. But the law remains on the books, and hardliners keep pressing to enforce it.

The history behind that defiance is grim. In 2022 Mahsa Amini died in the custody of the morality police after being arrested for an "improper" hijab. The uprising that followed, "Woman, Life, Freedom," was put down with lethal force. Amnesty International documented 45 cases of rape and other sexual violence by security forces against detained protesters, including women and children, and found that no official had been prosecuted. In 2014, men on motorcycles threw acid into the faces of women in Isfahan; witnesses reported that the attackers said they were punishing "bad hijab." The state never convicted anyone, and later paid the victims blood money.

In **Afghanistan**, the Taliban mandates covering by decree.

That is compulsion by men, backed by force. The nun has nothing to compare to it. No bishop can call the police to arrest a sister who walks out of her convent in a skirt and blouse.

## What the reply overstated

Here I must part from my friend's wording, though not his point.

**"Compelled by men in most Islamic countries."** As a matter of law, this is not accurate. Today only Iran and Afghanistan legally require the hijab nationwide. Saudi Arabia dropped its legal requirement in 2018. Indonesia has some local bylaws; Aceh enforces covering under its provincial sharia code. Turkey and Tunisia, Muslim-majority nations, for decades restricted the headscarf instead. What *is* widespread is family and community pressure: the father, brother or husband who decides for the woman. That pressure is real, but no one has counted it country by country, and a claim about "most Islamic countries" should not rest on it.

**"Hang them as Iran does."** I know of no documented case of Iran hanging a woman for removing her hijab. The executions that followed the 2022 uprising were of protesters, and the public record I have seen names men. The acid attacks were real, but they were committed by vigilantes the state failed to catch, not by court sentence. The rapes were real, committed against detainees from an uprising that began over the hijab. The 2024 law, as Amnesty read it, opened the door to death sentences, but it was suspended before it took effect.

This matters. Hyperbole is lying by proportion. The facts about Iran are terrible enough without being stretched, and a Muslim reader who catches one stretched claim will throw out the true ones with it. The accurate sentence is still devastating: *Iran's regime has jailed, flogged, beaten and fined women for uncovering their hair; a young woman died in its custody over a loose scarf; its agents raped detained protesters; and acid was thrown in women's faces for "bad hijab" while the state looked away.*

## The nun's freedom is real

On the Catholic side, my friend is substantially right, and canon law bears him out.

A women's religious institute writes its own constitutions, its fundamental rule of life, which the competent authority of the Church (the bishop or the Holy See) must approve (Canon 587). The sisters elect their own superiors in chapter (Canons 625 and 631). A sister who wishes to leave may seek an indult of departure and return to lay life (Canon 691). So "they write their own rule, elect their own leadership, and self-govern" is accurate, with the qualification that the rule needs Church approval, as a man's order's rule does.

The best proof of the nun's freedom is what happened after the Second Vatican Council. Many American congregations, by their own vote, modified or set aside the full habit. Others kept it or returned to it. Whatever one thinks of those decisions, the women made them. Nobody threw acid at the sisters who chose a short veil or none at all. That is exactly the difference the meme conceals.

And Father Groeschel's joke carries a truth. Anyone who has dealt with an experienced mother superior knows she is no one's subordinate in her own house.

## The same confusion in canon law and sharia

The meme rides on the same confusion Michigan's Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed introduced during his campaign. Asked about sharia on Fox News, he said: *"Nobody is trying to push sharia law on anyone else, just like I hope nobody is trying to push canon law on anybody else."*

His best case is this: in America, both bind only the believer who accepts them, and both are protected by the First Amendment. As far as American law goes, that is true, and I suspect many American Muslims do think of sharia as personal piety: prayer, fasting, diet, modesty.

But the two are not the same kind of thing. Canon law governs the internal life of the Church. Its heaviest penalty is excommunication. It claims no power to fine, jail, flog or execute, and no bishop can ask a Michigan court to enforce it. Sharia, in its classical form, is a complete legal order for society, including criminal penalties, and where it has become state law (Iran, Afghanistan, and in various degrees elsewhere) it is enforced by police and courts against believer and unbeliever alike. A canon-law professor at the Catholic University of America made the fair point that the comparison may illustrate the freedom to observe religious duties, but it does not establish that the two systems are equivalent.

## The question under the question

The meme asks whether Westerners who call the hijab oppressive are simply bigots. The question underneath it is this: when Christians and other Westerners grow uneasy about the hijab and sharia, what are they actually afraid of?

They are not afraid of a piece of cloth. They are afraid of the system that, in some places, stands behind it. And that concern remains plausible, for reasons that should be stated calmly:


 	- **The pattern where Islamic law gains state power.** In Iran after 1979 and Afghanistan after 2021, a voluntary practice became a compulsory one almost immediately. History has shown repeatedly what happens to the choice when the doctrine reaches the levers of government.

 	- **The doctrine itself.** Mainstream Islamic jurisprudence treats covering as an obligation, not a personal devotion like a nun's vow. An obligation backed by a legal tradition that includes penalties is a different thing from a vocation a woman freely enters and may freely leave.

 	- **Guardianship and honor.** In much of the Muslim world, a woman's dress is treated as a matter of family honor, governed by her father, brother or husband. That is where the pressure that no statute records comes from.

 	- **The intuitive sense of unnaturalness.** Most people sense that something is wrong when a girl of eight is required to cover, or when a woman's uncovered hair is treated as a crime. That instinct is not bigotry. It is conscience recognizing coercion.


None of this makes the woman in Dearborn guilty of anything. It means the question "is it freely chosen, and would it stay freely chosen?" is a fair one to ask.

## The Christian measure

I believe God lives our experience from within. He is not a distant judge watching women in Isfahan and nuns in Ohio; He feels the acid on the one face and the joy of offering in the other heart. That is why compulsion in matters of devotion offends Him. A garment forced on a woman cannot be an offering, because an offering is something given. God feels the coerced act as coercion, not as worship.

Scripture says it plainly: *"Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty"* **(2 Corinthians 3:17)**. Paul's rule for giving applies to every offering: *"Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver"* **(2 Corinthians 9:7)**.

The Qur'an itself says *"There is no compulsion in religion"* **(Qur'an 2:256)**. A Muslim who quotes that verse and means it stands with us. The regime in Tehran does not.

So my test is simple. Would I want it done to me? I would want my freely chosen devotion respected, and I would want to be protected from anyone who tried to force it. That is what I owe both women in the meme: respect for the one who chooses, and defense of the one who is compelled. *"Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them"* **(Matthew 7:12)**.

## A short answer for the comment thread

For anyone who wants to reply to the meme in a few sentences:
> The difference isn't the cloth. It's consent. A nun chooses her habit, her community votes on whether to keep it, and she can walk away with no penalty. In Iran it is a crime for a woman to uncover her hair; women have been jailed and beaten for it, and a young woman died in police custody over it. Where the hijab is freely chosen, it deserves the same respect as the habit. Where it's forced, it's oppression, and so would a forced habit be.


## Sources and their limits

My sourcing leans on the mainstream record: Pew Research, Amnesty International, the UN, Human Rights Watch, the Center for Human Rights in Iran, AP, EWTN and Catholic World Report, and the Code of Canon Law. On Iran, these sources are probably more likely to understate than overstate the regime's abuses, since the regime controls access, suppresses journalists (four were arrested in 2014 for linking the acid attacks to hijab enforcement), and rape survivors face stigma and reprisal. Amnesty itself says the true number of victims is likely much higher than what it documented.

Where curation could hide evidence: no one has systematically measured family and community pressure to veil across the Muslim world, so the size of that pressure, the heart of my friend's claim, rests on firsthand reports and regional studies rather than a count. Firsthand reports should be treated as leads. Likewise, I found no documented case of a woman executed for unveiling; that may reflect the absence of such cases or the opacity of Iran's courts.
