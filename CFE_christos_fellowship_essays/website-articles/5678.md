# 261107 - How God Got So Great

# The Glue and the Ground:


### A Reply to Manvir Singh’s “How God Got So Great”


*Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 6 November 2026*


*“For in him we live, and move, and have our being.”* **(Acts 17:28)**


**Occasion.** In March, *The New Yorker* ran a long essay by Manvir Singh, an anthropologist at the University of California, Davis, and author of *Shamanism: The Timeless Religion*, titled “How God Got So Great.” Two weeks later, Robin Schumacher, an apologist with a doctorate in New Testament, answered it in a column for *The Christian Post*. (Links to both are in the Sources section at the end.) Both pieces deserve readers. Singh’s is learned and readable, the kind of essay that will shape how many educated people think about the God of the Bible for years. Schumacher’s is a quick, faithful reply that he himself calls the short version, because a column has no room for more.


This essay takes the room. My thesis, stated plainly at the start: **Singh has measured something real and drawn the wrong conclusion from it.** The social power of belief in one God — the trust, the cooperation, the cohesion he documents — does not explain God away. It is God’s fingerprint. A belief that keeps producing those fruits across centuries and continents behaves exactly as a true belief about reality should. Schumacher is right that the decisive question is whether the faith is true. I want to go one step further and show why the answer changes the meaning of every piece of evidence Singh gathered — and why the God of the Bible is not, and never was, the watchful sky-policeman that the anthropology of religion has in view.


## I. Singh’s Case at Full Strength


Before answering a man, state his case as he would want it stated.


Singh’s argument has two halves, one historical and one functional.


The historical half runs like this. The God of Israel did not begin as the sole God of the universe. In the ancient Levant, the high god was El, the elderly king of the gods, who, with his consort Asherah, presided over a council of deities: Baal, the storm god; Yam, the sea; Mot, the lord of death. These are not inventions of modern scholars; they appear by name in the clay tablets uncovered at Ugarit (Ras Shamra, in modern Syria) beginning in 1929. On Singh’s reading, El, not Yahweh, was probably Israel’s original patron. Yahweh was a storm-and-war deity from the southern wilderness who, around the turn of the first millennium before Christ, displaced El as Israel’s chief god. Full-blown monotheism — the claim that there are no other gods at all — came later still, hardening during the Babylonian exile.


The functional half asks why this god conquered the world. Here Singh draws on a substantial literature on the cultural evolution of religion — the line of research associated with psychologist Ara Norenzayan’s Big Gods and political scientist Dominic Johnson’s *God Is Watching You*. The argument: small bands can enforce morality by gossip and reputation, because everybody knows everybody. Large societies of strangers cannot. A god who sees everything, cares about morality, and punishes cheating solves the stranger problem: if I believe that you believe you are being watched, I can trust you. Groups with such gods cooperate on a larger scale, outcompete their neighbors, and spread. On this account, the global success of Christianity and Islam is the success of a social technology.


Singh then turns to the present. In American public life, he argues, belief in one God functions as a moral credential — a badge of trustworthiness that politicians display. And cohesion built on that badge, he says, excludes: it asks Jains, Hindus, Buddhists, Taoists, agnostics, and nonbelievers to prove their decency in a moral vocabulary that is not theirs. He closes by turning Voltaire’s famous line around. Voltaire said that if God did not exist, we would need to invent him. Singh says the harder and more valuable task is to *“construct moral communities that can function without Him.”*


That is a serious argument, made by a serious scholar. It deserves a serious answer, and part of a serious answer is admitting what is true in it.


## II. What Is True in It — and What the Evidence Can and Cannot Decide


### The Bible never claims that Israel was faithful


Schumacher answers the historical half by citing the Bible’s own early declarations of one God, and by noting that the idolatry of some Israelites does not make Israel’s religion polytheistic. Both points are sound as far as they go. But I want to concede more than he did, because that concession strengthens the Christian case rather than weakening it.


The Bible itself is the loudest witness to Israel’s polytheism. It does not hide it; it indicts it, book after book:


*“And they forsook the LORD, and served Baal and Ashtaroth.”* **(Judges 2:13)**


When King Josiah cleansed the temple, he had to haul out *“all the vessels that were made for Baal, and for the grove, and for all the host of heaven”* **(2 Kings 23:4)** — and “the grove” in the King James is the translators’ rendering of *asherah*, the cult object of the very goddess Singh names. In Jeremiah’s day the people of Judah told the prophet to his face that they would go on burning incense *“unto the queen of heaven”* **(Jeremiah 44:17)**, because things had gone better for them when they did.


Archaeology adds to the picture. At Kuntillet Ajrud in the Sinai, inscriptions from around 800 B.C. bless travelers by “Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah.” A similar blessing turns up in a tomb at Khirbet el-Qom near Hebron. Here I part company with one line of Schumacher’s reply: no archaeological record shows the God of Israel ranked *beneath* El in Israelite worship, but there is a clear record that many ordinary Israelites paired Him with a consort. A Christian loses nothing by saying so. The prophets said so first.


So the evidence on the ground is not in dispute. Israel lived for centuries surrounded by, and often soaked in, Canaanite religion. The question is which direction the arrow runs.


### Same artifacts, opposite arrows


The critical reconstruction reads the evidence as an *ascent*: Israel started inside the Canaanite pantheon and slowly climbed out of it into monotheism, with the prophets as late reformers inventing a purer god than their grandparents knew.


The biblical account reads the same evidence as a *descent and a recall*: God revealed Himself to the fathers and to Moses; Israel kept sliding back into the religion of its neighbors; and God sent prophet after prophet to call His people home.


Notice that both stories predict the same potsherds. Both predict Asherah inscriptions, Baal altars, and reforming kings tearing down high places. The artifacts cannot tell the two stories apart. What tells them apart is the starting assumption a scholar brings to the dig. If revelation is ruled out from the beginning, only the ascent story remains — not because the evidence chose it but because the method did. That is Schumacher’s first and best point, and it is exactly right: the school of interpretation Singh stands in, which grew out of the late nineteenth-century “history of religions” approach, begins by assuming that religions evolve naturally and then reconstructs the past to fit. That is a legitimate research program. It is not a finding.


### El and the name


The El question deserves a closer look, because Scripture addresses it directly — more directly than either article mentions.


The patriarchs worshiped God under El names: *El Shaddai* (God Almighty), *El Elyon* (God Most High), *El Olam* (the Everlasting God). And the Bible itself tells us that the name Yahweh arrived later, with Moses:


*“And I appeared unto Abraham, unto Isaac, and unto Jacob, by the name of God Almighty, but by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them.”* **(Exodus 6:3)**


So the observation that “El came before Yahweh” is not a discovery made against the Bible. The Bible states it. The disagreement is over what it means. Singh reads two names as two gods, one displacing the other. Exodus reads two names as one God disclosing more of Himself — and Genesis has already joined them, when Abram swears by *“the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth”* **(Genesis 14:22)**, setting the covenant name and *El Elyon* side by side as one Person. Schumacher is right that *El* in Hebrew is both a name and the ordinary word for “God.” An Israelite could avoid the word El no more than an English speaker can avoid the word *God*.


### The council


The strongest card in the critical hand is the “divine council” — passages where God stands among other heavenly beings:


*“God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods.”* **(Psalm 82:1)**


In Deuteronomy 32:8, the King James reads that the Most High set the borders of the nations *“according to the number of the children of Israel.”* But the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek Septuagint preserve an older reading: according to the number of the *sons of God* — the heavenly beings. Scholars such as Mark S. Smith of Princeton Theological Seminary read that verse as El dividing the nations among his divine sons and handing Israel to Yahweh.


The textual point is real, and an honest Christian should accept it. But the conclusion does not follow. The Bible never denies that God made a host of spiritual beings; it insists on it. *“The morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy”* **(Job 38:7)** at creation. The late Old Testament scholar Michael Heiser argued at length, in *The Unseen Realm*, that the council passages describe the created spirits under God’s rule, not rival gods beside Him — that “Most High” in Deuteronomy 32 is the LORD Himself, and that the very next verse, *“For the LORD’S portion is his people”* **(Deuteronomy 32:9)**, describes Him taking Israel as His own, not receiving it from a superior. The biblical claim was never that only one spiritual being exists. The claim is that only one is uncreated, and every other answers to Him: *“the LORD he is God; there is none else beside him”* **(Deuteronomy 4:35)**.


Here is where I stand. On the historical half of Singh’s argument, the evidence is genuinely mixed, and serious scholars read it two ways. The data do not force the evolutionary story. They allow it — when revelation has already been ruled out.


## III. The Arrow of Explanation: Function as Fingerprint


Now to the functional half, the heart of Singh’s essay, where I think his argument fails at the level of logic rather than evidence.


### How a belief spread does not tell you whether it is true


Suppose an anthropologist studied the spread of germ theory. He would find that communities that believed in invisible germs washed their hands, boiled their water, quarantined the sick, and outlived their neighbors. Their belief spread because believers survived. Would anyone conclude that germ theory’s value lies in its social function rather than its truth? Of course not. The belief worked *because germs are real*. The function was the fingerprint of the fact.


Philosophers call the mistake of judging a belief by its origin the *genetic fallacy*: showing how a belief arose, or why it spread, does not show whether it is true. Singh’s data about cooperation, trust, and expansion are compatible with two hypotheses:


- Belief in a moral God is a useful fiction that happens to make societies cohere.

- A moral God actually exists, His nature actually is the moral order, and people who align with Him therefore actually flourish together.


Both hypotheses predict what Singh found. So his findings cannot choose between them. But only the second explains *why* the “fiction” works so well, so long, in so many places. A fiction that reliably outperforms the truth across three thousand years is a strange kind of fiction. A truth that produces fruit is the most ordinary thing in the world. *“Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them”* **(Matthew 7:20)**.


A note on the science itself, in fairness to the reader: Singh’s own field is still arguing about the causal story. Norenzayan’s thesis is widely respected and also widely contested. One of the largest attempts to test it with historical data — a 2019 paper in *Nature* arguing that complex societies arose *before* moralizing gods, not after — was retracted by the journal in 2021 after other researchers found serious problems with how missing data had been coded. None of this refutes Singh. It does mean that the confident story of how God “got great” rests on an unsettled scientific debate.


### Singh’s sharpest point is a prophet’s point


One part of Singh’s essay I want to accept without argument, because God said it first. Singh observes that belief in God is used in American politics as a credential — a badge displayed for advantage. He is right. It is. And the God of the Bible despises it:


*“I hate, I despise your feast days, and I will not smell in your solemn assemblies… But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.”* **(Amos 5:21, 24)**


*“This people draweth nigh unto me with their mouth, and honoureth me with their lips; but their heart is far from me.”* **(Matthew 15:8)**


When a politician waves God like a flag while his life shows contempt for God’s law, the prophets would not defend him, and neither will I. Religion used as a credential is exactly the religion God tears down. But notice what follows. If belief in God were nothing more than a social badge, there would be no standing ground from which to condemn its use as a badge. The prophets could condemn hollow religion only because they knew a God who is not hollow — whose law is the measure the counterfeit fails. Singh’s critique borrows its force from the very God his theory removes.


## IV. The Ground Beneath the Glue: What Kind of God?


Schumacher closes on the right question — is it true? — and answers it the way the historic church has always answered: God is real, He created all things, He has revealed Himself, and moral law flows from His nature rather than from social need. I affirm every word. But I want to go one level deeper, because I believe the Christian answer contains something neither article reaches, and it is the thing that most completely dissolves Singh’s picture.


### Not a watcher above the crowd


Look closely at the god of the cultural-evolution literature. He is a *watcher*: an all-seeing supervisor who monitors behavior from outside and punishes defectors. The whole mechanism depends on surveillance. People behave because they feel observed — the theory’s own researchers have shown that even a picture of watching eyes on a wall makes people more honest.


That is not the God of the Bible. The God of the Bible does not watch our lives from outside them. He lives them from inside.


*“For in him we live, and move, and have our being.”* **(Acts 17:28)**


*“And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.”* **(Colossians 1:17)**


*“Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence?”* **(Psalm 139:7)**


I take these verses as literal descriptions, not as metaphors. No place exists outside God where a creature could stand. Every human life is lived within His consciousness and sustained by it. The physics this ministry has developed over forty years—Conscious Point Physics, which holds that the very substance of reality is conscious, the sustaining thought of God—was built to show how the world is actually made, not merely how believers prefer to speak of it. But one does not need the physics to see what the Scripture says. In the plainest sense: **we are God’s experience of the world.** He feels from inside every life what that life suffers and what it inflicts.


### What that does to morality


If that is true, morality is not a social contract enforced by an imagined camera. Morality is *how God experiences what we do to one another*. When I wound my neighbor, I am not breaking a rule posted on the wall of the cosmos. I am wounding someone God is living — and wounding God in him.


Jesus said exactly this, and meant it exactly:


*“Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.”* **(Matthew 25:40)**


He said it again to Saul on the Damascus road, when Saul had never laid a hand on Jesus Himself, only on His people: *“Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?”* **(Acts 9:4)**. And Isaiah saw it centuries earlier: *“In all their affliction he was afflicted”* **(Isaiah 63:9)**.


This is the difference between the god of the anthropologists and the God of Abraham. The surveillance god produces good behavior through fear of being seen. The God who lives our lives produces good behavior through love for the One who feels it. We keep His law not because we are watched but because He is *there* — in the neighbor, in the stranger, in the enemy — and our choices land on Him. *“He that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?”* **(1 John 4:20)**.


### Why this explains the fruit


Now return to Singh’s data with this picture in hand. Why does belief in one moral God produce trust among strangers, cooperation at scale, and societies that hold together? Because every stranger *is* a place where God lives. A people who learn to treat the stranger as they would treat God align themselves with the actual structure of reality—and a society aligned with reality works the way a bridge built on true engineering does. The cohesion is not the purpose of the belief. Cohesion happens when the belief is true, and people act on it.


And this also explains what the theory cannot: why the same God who is the ground of all goodness cannot simply wave evil through. A God who feels every cruelty from inside cannot treat cruelty as nothing. His holiness — His total integrity with Himself — and His love — which requires free creatures who can genuinely refuse Him — would be in contradiction, if He had not provided a way within His own creation for the debt of evil to be paid by someone who owed nothing. That is why the Cross was not optional, and why God Himself had to come. I have set out that argument step by step in a one-page handout, “The Premise Change,” and at length in “Reconciliation of Man with the Father,” both linked below. The point here is only this: the Christian God is not a tool for social order. He is the reason social order is possible at all, and the One who paid, in His own body, for every place where we have broken it.


## V. The Pluralist’s Worry — and the Kingdom’s Answer


Singh’s closing concern deserves its own answer, because it is humane, and I share part of it. He worries that a society bound together by belief in one God will make its Jain, Hindu, Buddhist, agnostic, and atheist citizens second-class — forced to prove their decency in someone else’s language.


First, let me say what I agree with. Coercion of conscience is evil. A Christian who uses the State to compel belief, or treats a nonbeliever as a lesser citizen, has abandoned the Kingdom of God for a kingdom of men. Freedom of conscience is not a secular value that Christianity grudgingly tolerates; it is a Christian conviction from the beginning, because compelled faith is not faith at all. *“Choose you this day whom ye will serve”* **(Joshua 24:15)** assumes that a man can choose otherwise. God invites. He does not trap.


But now look at what the teaching of Section IV does to Singh’s worry. If God lives every human life, then the Jain, the Hindu, the Buddhist, the agnostic, and the atheist are each as truly God’s experience of the world as the Christian is. Their joys and their wounds land on Him. That gives a Christian the strongest possible reason to protect his neighbor’s conscience and dignity: to harm the nonbeliever is to harm God in him. The other-believer and the nonbeliever are not enemies to be defeated. They are neighbors in whom God lives, and — where they are held by something false — captives to be won by persuasion and love, never by force.


Compare the foundation Singh proposes. A moral community that functions “without Him” must still answer: *why* does the Jain’s life matter? *Why* is the atheist owed dignity? If the answer is “because we have agreed to treat each other well,” then the dignity lasts only as long as the agreement—and history has shown, more than once, how quickly majorities can agree on something else. Schumacher puts this point strongly when he says that even atheists admit absolute morals are impossible without God. That needs a fair qualification: many thoughtful atheists, including philosophers who defend objective morality without God, deny it. But the most clear-sighted unbelievers saw the problem clearly. Nietzsche understood that the death of God would eventually take Christian morality with it, and Sartre took Dostoevsky’s line — that if God does not exist, everything is permitted — as the starting point of his own philosophy.


Singh might answer that some societies already function well with little belief — the secular nations of northern Europe are the usual example. They are trusting, orderly, and kind. I grant the observation. But those societies were formed by a thousand years of Christian teaching, and the historian Tom Holland, in *Dominion*, has made a powerful case that the moral instincts of secular Western people — care for the weak, the equal dignity of every person, suspicion of the powerful — are a Christian inheritance, still spending capital that faith deposited. Whether that capital can be renewed without its source is the question the next few generations will answer. I do not think it can. I think a community cut off from the ground of goodness will keep the fruit for a while, like a cut flower that keeps its color for a few days in a vase.


One more word, offered with respect. Both articles identify Singh as a Sikh. I do not presume to know his personal convictions, and his argument must be judged on its merits, not his background. But it is worth noticing that the scripture of the Sikh tradition opens by declaring one Creator — and that Sikh teaching has long spoken of that one Creator as present in every heart. On whether God dwells within each person, that tradition stands closer to the teaching of this essay than the secular framework Singh’s article adopts.


## VI. Why God Is Great


Singh asks how God *got* so great. The question assumes that God’s greatness is a status He acquired — climbing a pantheon, winning a market, collecting worshipers. But God did not get great.


*“Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God.”* **(Psalm 90:2)**


What grew was not God. What grew was our knowledge of Him — slowly, through revelation, through prophets, through a people who kept forgetting and were kept being called back, and finally through the Son, who showed us the Father’s face. Singh has written a careful history of that slow human awakening and mistaken it for God’s history.


Here is my stake. I believe that God is the ground of everything that exists. I believe that every life — the anthropologist’s, the apologist’s, the reader’s — is lived inside Him, and that He feels it. I believe that the cooperation and trust Singh documents are what happens when human beings, however imperfectly, begin to treat each other as places where God lives. I believe that where we have failed to do that, God Himself has paid the debt in the person of Jesus Christ, and that payment is offered freely to anyone who will receive it. And I believe that a community built without Him is a house built without ground under it.


I am grateful to Manvir Singh for an essay that takes religion seriously enough to explain it, and to Robin Schumacher for refusing to let the explanation stand unanswered. Iron sharpens iron. The question both men point to is the one that matters: not what God does for us, but whether He is. He is. And because He is, He is great — not because we made Him so, but because there was never a moment when He was anything less.


*“Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised; and his greatness is unsearchable.”* **(Psalm 145:3)**
