# Introduction: The Question Every Religion Answers


## One Question, Forty-Five Answers

Every religion on earth gives an answer to the question every person will eventually face: what happens to me when I die? A popular YouTube video, [*55 Minutes of Every Religion and What Happens After Death Explained*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7_AnOUTwgQ), walks through forty-five of those answers in under an hour. It moves from the Catholic candle lit for a grandmother, to the Hindu pilgrim who travels to Varanasi to die, to the Toraja family in Indonesia who keep a deceased parent at home for months and greet him each morning.

The video is well made and generally sympathetic. It treats each tradition on its own terms and asks only what each one believes. It does not ask the question that matters most: which of these answers is true?

That is the question I want to take up in this series. I will walk through every tradition the video names. For each one I will ask three things: what it sees truly, what it gets wrong, and how a Christian can speak with its followers about the hope we have in Christ.

I do this for two reasons. First, the reader who watches a video like this one may come away thinking that all the answers are equally good guesses, and so none of them matters. I think that conclusion is mistaken. Second, every one of these traditions is held by real people, many of whom are our neighbors. If we are to love them, we need to understand what they believe and why they believe it, well enough to speak with them honestly and with respect.

A word on accuracy before I begin. The video simplifies, as any survey of forty-five religions must, and some of its names and terms are garbled. Where I describe a tradition, I have tried to state it as its own best teachers would, not as a summary on YouTube does. A Mormon, a Hindu, or a Muslim reading this should recognize his own faith in my description, even where he disagrees with my judgment of it.

## The Standard: Scripture, and the Way Paul Used It

I measure each tradition against the Bible. I do not do this because Christianity happens to be my tradition, but because I believe the Bible is God's own testimony about Himself, about us, and about what lies beyond death. Jesus is the one person in history who claimed to have come from the other side and who proved the claim by rising from the dead. His word on the afterlife therefore carries an authority no mystic's vision or philosopher's guess can match.

That standard cuts in two directions. It lets me say plainly where a tradition is wrong. It also obliges me to say plainly where a tradition is right, because Scripture itself teaches that God has not left any people without a witness. Paul told the crowd at Lystra that God *"left not himself without witness, in that he did good, and gave us rain from heaven, and fruitful seasons"* **(Acts 14:17)**. The writer of Ecclesiastes says that God *"hath set the world in their heart"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:11)**, a phrase many translators render as *eternity* set in the human heart. Every religion in this survey is, at least in part, a human response to that inward witness.

Paul's sermon on Mars Hill in Athens is my model for how to engage them. He did not open by condemning the Athenians. He opened by noticing what they had right. He had walked their streets and found an altar *"TO THE UNKNOWN GOD"* **(Acts 17:23)**, and he began there. He quoted their own poets, who had said that *"in him we live, and move, and have our being"* and *"we are also his offspring"* **(Acts 17:28)**. Only after he had honored the truth they already held did he tell them what they lacked: that God *"hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead"* **(Acts 17:31)**.

That is the pattern for this series. Start with the truth a tradition has glimpsed. Name honestly where it falls short. Then point to the risen Christ, who fills in what each of these traditions was reaching for.

## The Premise I Bring: We Live Inside God's Experience

One conviction runs beneath everything I write, and it shapes how I read every tradition in this survey. God is self-existent, and there is nothing outside Him. Paul said it to the Athenians: *"in him we live, and move, and have our being"* **(Acts 17:28)**. I take that statement literally. We do not stand outside God, looking at Him from a distance. We exist within Him, and He experiences the world from the inside of every life He has made. In a real sense, we are God's experience of the world.

This is not a new doctrine, but it is rarely emphasized in Christian teaching. Its consequences are large. If God feels every choice I make from within my own life, then His law is not an arbitrary rulebook handed down by a distant king. It is a description of what does and does not wound the One in whom I live. I keep His commandments not first out of fear of punishment, but out of love, because He feels what I do.

The same premise explains why the Cross was necessary, and I have set out that argument in one page in my [Premise Change handout](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/10/31/260725-premise-change-handout/). In brief: God is light, and He cannot admit darkness into His presence. God is also love, and love requires free creatures who can genuinely refuse it. Since there is nowhere outside God to put such creatures, the creation must be a sealed region within Him, made and held by the Son. Within that region, wrong done is never simply erased by later good; it remains on the ledger. No creature can pay his own debt, because his good works always arrive too late. Only the innocent Creator, entering His own creation and dying unjustly, could generate a payment with no matching debt, and then offer it to anyone who will receive it.

I want to be clear about what depends on this reasoning and what does not. The fact that Christ died for our sins and rose again is settled in Scripture. My account of *why* the Cross had to happen is my understanding of the mechanism. If every step of it were wrong, the Cross would still stand.

This premise gives me a single lens for all forty-five traditions. Three questions follow from it:


 	- **Does the person survive?** If God experiences the world through many distinct lives, then each life matters to Him as itself. Traditions that dissolve the person into an impersonal whole, or that say the self was never real, have seen something true about our unity with God but have lost the person God loves.

 	- **What becomes of the separation sin causes?** Isaiah says that *"your iniquities have separated between you and your God"* **(Isaiah 59:2)**. Every tradition has some account of judgment, purification, or sorting after death. The question is whether it reckons honestly with that separation.

 	- **Who pays?** Nearly every religion in this survey answers the problem of wrongdoing by asking the person to work it off: by merit, by ritual, by meditation, by many lifetimes, or by the offerings of his descendants. Scripture's answer is that we cannot, and that God Himself has paid.


## What Is Settled and What Is Open

To judge fairly, I have to separate what Scripture states plainly from what it leaves open. I hold firmly to the first and hold the second loosely.

**The core.** On these points the Bible speaks clearly, and I will call a tradition right or wrong by them:


 	- There is one personal God, the Creator, who is distinct from His creatures even as they live within Him.

 	- Every person has sinned, and sin separates us from God **(Romans 3:23; Isaiah 59:2)**.

 	- Jesus Christ, God the Son made flesh, died for our sins and rose bodily from the dead **(1 Corinthians 15:3–4)**.

 	- Salvation is God's gift, received by faith, not earned by works **(Ephesians 2:8–9)**.

 	- The dead will be raised bodily **(John 5:28–29)**.

 	- Every person will stand before God and give an account of his works **(2 Corinthians 5:10; Revelation 20:12)**.

 	- Each person lives one earthly life **(Hebrews 9:27)**.


**The open questions.** On three matters, Bible-believing Christians who love Scripture and read it carefully have reached different conclusions for centuries:


 	- Is the soul conscious between death and the resurrection, or does it sleep?

 	- Is the final punishment of the wicked eternal conscious suffering, or final destruction?

 	- Is there any purification after death?


I do not think the Bible's testimony on these questions is clear enough to declare one side right and the other wrong. Scripture contains texts that support each view, and I list them in the next section so the reader can weigh them himself. Where a tradition lands on one side of these questions, I will say that it stands within the range of honest biblical debate, not that it has erred.

These questions do not decide anyone's salvation, nor do they change the moral law by which our works will be judged. That is what matters most, and it is where the founders of this nation also stood: on the general principles of Christianity rather than on the disputes between denominations.

I should add one qualification. The open questions do not touch how we are saved, but they do touch practice in a few places. Scripture forbids attempting to contact the dead **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**, and that bears on spiritualism and on some forms of ancestor veneration whatever one believes about the soul's state. Whether we may pray to the departed, or for them, is a practical question that turns partly on these doctrines. And a confident belief that everyone will be saved in the end can quietly drain away the urgency of the Gospel. Where these come up, I will note them briefly, without reopening the debate.

## The Scriptures on Both Sides

Here are the main texts each side cites, quoted from the King James Version. I have added a short note after each list on how the other side reads the key passages. The reader can test these himself.

### 1. Between Death and Resurrection: Conscious or Asleep?

**Texts cited for conscious existence after death:**


 	- *"Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise"* **(Luke 23:43)**.

 	- *"For I am in a strait betwixt two, having a desire to depart, and to be with Christ; which is far better"* **(Philippians 1:23)**.

 	- *"We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord"* **(2 Corinthians 5:8)**.

 	- *"And in hell he lift up his eyes, being in torments, and seeth Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom"* **(Luke 16:23)**.

 	- *"I saw under the altar the souls of them that were slain for the word of God… And they cried with a loud voice, saying, How long, O Lord"* **(Revelation 6:9–10)**.

 	- *"And, behold, there appeared unto them Moses and Elias talking with him"* **(Matthew 17:3)**.

 	- *"God is not the God of the dead, but of the living"* **(Matthew 22:32)**; *"for all live unto him"* **(Luke 20:38)**.

 	- *"…and to the spirits of just men made perfect"* **(Hebrews 12:23)**.

 	- Samuel, after his death, speaks to Saul **(1 Samuel 28:11–19)**.


**Texts cited for soul sleep:**


 	- *"For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing"* **(Ecclesiastes 9:5)**; *"for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest"* **(Ecclesiastes 9:10)**.

 	- *"His breath goeth forth, he returneth to his earth; in that very day his thoughts perish"* **(Psalm 146:4)**.

 	- *"For in death there is no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?"* **(Psalm 6:5)**; *"The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence"* **(Psalm 115:17)**.

 	- *"So man lieth down, and riseth not: till the heavens be no more, they shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep"* **(Job 14:12)**.

 	- *"And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake"* **(Daniel 12:2)**.

 	- *"Our friend Lazarus sleepeth"* **(John 11:11)**, which Jesus then explains: *"Lazarus is dead"* **(John 11:14)**.

 	- *"But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep"* **(1 Thessalonians 4:13)**; *"We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed"* **(1 Corinthians 15:51)**.

 	- *"For David is not ascended into the heavens"* **(Acts 2:34)**; of Stephen's death, *"he fell asleep"* **(Acts 7:60)**.


**How each side reads the other's texts.** Those who hold to soul sleep note that the Greek of Luke 23:43 had no punctuation, so the verse can be read *"Verily I say unto thee to day, shalt thou be with me in paradise."* They read the rich man and Lazarus as a parable, not a map, and Paul's *"depart, and to be with Christ"* as the experience of the sleeper, for whom death and resurrection feel like a single moment. Those who hold to conscious existence answer that *sleep* is a gentle figure of speech for death, describing how the body looks, and that the Old Testament texts describe the grave from the vantage of the living, before Christ brought *"life and immortality to light"* **(2 Timothy 1:10)**.

### 2. The Final Punishment: Eternal Suffering or Final Destruction?

**Texts cited for eternal conscious punishment:**


 	- *"And these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal"* **(Matthew 25:46)**.

 	- *"Depart from me, ye cursed, into everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels"* **(Matthew 25:41)**.

 	- *"Where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not quenched"* **(Mark 9:44)**.

 	- *"And the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever: and they have no rest day nor night"* **(Revelation 14:11)**.

 	- *"…and shall be tormented day and night for ever and ever"* **(Revelation 20:10)**.

 	- *"…and some to shame and everlasting contempt"* **(Daniel 12:2)**.

 	- *"…to whom is reserved the blackness of darkness for ever"* **(Jude 13)**.

 	- *"I am tormented in this flame"* **(Luke 16:24)**.


**Texts cited for final destruction (conditional immortality):**


 	- *"And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell"* **(Matthew 10:28)**.

 	- *"…that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life"* **(John 3:16)**.

 	- *"For the wages of sin is death"* **(Romans 6:23)**; *"the soul that sinneth, it shall die"* **(Ezekiel 18:4)**.

 	- *"Who shall be punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord"* **(2 Thessalonians 1:9)**.

 	- *"…and the day that cometh shall burn them up… that it shall leave them neither root nor branch… for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet"* **(Malachi 4:1, 3)**.

 	- *"For yet a little while, and the wicked shall not be"* **(Psalm 37:10)**; *"into smoke shall they consume away"* **(Psalm 37:20)**.

 	- *"And death and hell were cast into the lake of fire. This is the second death"* **(Revelation 20:14)**.

 	- *"Whose end is destruction"* **(Philippians 3:19)**.

 	- Sodom and Gomorrah are *"set forth for an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire"* **(Jude 7)**, yet those cities burned to ashes and are not burning today **(2 Peter 2:6)**.

 	- God *"only hath immortality"* **(1 Timothy 6:16)**.


**How each side reads the other's texts.** Those who hold to final destruction read *everlasting punishment* as a punishment whose result is everlasting, as *eternal fire* consumed Sodom once and for all. They note that the worm and fire of Mark 9 come from Isaiah 66:24, where they consume *"carcases."* Those who hold to eternal conscious punishment answer that Matthew 25:46 sets *everlasting punishment* and *life eternal* side by side with the same word, so the one must last as long as the other, and that Revelation speaks of torment, not only of destruction.

### 3. Is There Purification After Death?

**Texts cited for a purifying fire after death:**


 	- *"For every one shall be salted with fire, and every sacrifice shall be salted with salt"* **(Mark 9:49)**.

 	- *"…the fire shall try every man's work of what sort it is… **If any man's work shall be burned, he shall suffer loss: but he himself shall be saved; yet so as by fire"* **(1 Corinthians 3:13, 15)**.

 	- *"…for he is like a refiner's fire, and like fullers' soap: And he shall sit as a refiner and purifier of silver"* **(Malachi 3:2–3)**.

 	- *"…it shall not be forgiven him, neither in this world, neither in the world to come"* **(Matthew 12:32)**, read as implying some sins may be forgiven after death.

 	- *"…Thou shalt by no means come out thence, till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing"* **(Matthew 5:26)**.

 	- Christ *"went and preached unto the spirits in prison"* **(1 Peter 3:19)**; *"For for this cause was the gospel preached also to them that are dead"* **(1 Peter 4:6)**.

 	- Judas Maccabeus offers a sacrifice for fallen soldiers *"that they might be delivered from sin"* **(2 Maccabees 12:45)**. This book appears in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles but not in Protestant ones.


**Texts cited against purification after death:**


 	- *"And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

 	- *"…between us and you there is a great gulf fixed"* **(Luke 16:26)**.

 	- *"For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified"* **(Hebrews 10:14)**.

 	- *"…the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin"* **(1 John 1:7)**.

 	- *"Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord from henceforth… that they may rest from their labours"* **(Revelation 14:13)**.

 	- The thief on the cross goes from a lifetime of crime to paradise *"to day"* **(Luke 23:43)**.


**How each side reads the other's texts.** Those who reject purification after death read 1 Corinthians 3 as the testing of a believer's *works* at the judgment, not a cleansing of the person over time, and Mark 9:49 as referring to the trials of this life or to the fire of judgment itself. Those who affirm it answer that judgment and purification may be the same event. A fire that tests works also burns away what is unworthy in the one who did them. Many who hold this view picture it as a single moment of meeting Christ, not as a long stay in a place.

### A Related Question: Will All Be Reconciled?

Some Christians go further and hold that God's purifying judgment will finally restore every person. They cite God's will that *"all men"* be saved **(1 Timothy 2:4)**; that *"as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive"* **(1 Corinthians 15:22)**; that God will *"reconcile all things unto himself"* **(Colossians 1:20)**; and that *"every knee should bow… and every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord"* **(Philippians 2:10–11)**. Others answer that the same Scriptures speak of a gulf fixed, a door shut **(Matthew 25:10)**, and a punishment called everlasting. This view comes up when I reach the Universalists, and I will treat it there in the same spirit: the texts on each side, and an honest admission of what we do not know.

 	- **The religions of India:** Hindu, Buddhist, Tibetan Buddhist, Pure Land Buddhist, Sikh, and Jain.

 	- **The religions of East Asia:** Shinto, Taoist, Confucian, Chinese folk religion, Tenrikyo, and Cao Dai.

 	- **Modern movements:** Spiritualism, Wicca, Scientology, and Rastafari.

 	- **African and indigenous traditions:** Yoruba, Haitian Vodou, Lakota, Aboriginal Australian, Hmong, Malagasy, and Toraja.

 	- **The religions of the ancient world:** Egyptian, Greek, Norse, Mesopotamian, Celtic, Aztec, and Inca.


For each living tradition I ask three questions: **What does it see truly? Where does it go wrong? How can a Christian speak with its followers?** Two groups need a different third question.

For fellow Christians, *witnessing* is the wrong word. A Catholic or Orthodox believer who trusts in the risen Christ is my brother. With him, the third question becomes: **how do we talk with one another?** That means naming where we agree, where we differ, and why the difference matters.

For the religions of the ancient world, no one remains to witness to. No one today pours water for Mesopotamian ancestors or buries a heart scarab to silence his own heart before Osiris. For these, the third question becomes: **where do their ideas live on today?** The answer is often surprising. Echoes of them run through modern movements, popular culture, and the quiet assumptions of people who would never call themselves religious.

# Part 2: The Christian Family and Its Offshoots

The first family in the survey is my own. Eight of the video's traditions grew from the Christian Scriptures. Three are the great historic branches of the Church: Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant. Five are later movements that began within Christianity and then departed from it in important ways: the Latter-day Saints, Jehovah's Witnesses, Seventh-day Adventists, Swedenborgians, and Unitarian Universalists.

These eight do not stand in the same place. The three historic branches confess the same core faith: the Trinity, the deity of Christ, His bodily resurrection, and salvation through Him alone. They differ on important questions, but they are one family. Of the five later movements, the Adventists stand largely within that core, and most of their distinctive views fall among the open questions. The Latter-day Saints, Jehovah's Witnesses, Swedenborgians, and Unitarian Universalists each depart from it on at least one point that matters for salvation, usually who Jesus is.

I have tried to treat each one as its own members would recognize it. Where I disagree, I say so plainly. Where I believe a group stands within the open questions, I say that just as plainly.

## Catholicism

**What it teaches.** The Catholic Church teaches that each soul faces a *particular judgment* at the moment of death, followed by a *general judgment* at the end of time when the body is raised. After the particular judgment, the soul goes to heaven, to hell, or to purgatory. Heaven is the vision of God face to face. Hell is the final self-exclusion of one who has refused God's love to the end. Purgatory is a purification for those who die in God's grace but are not yet wholly purified. Every soul in purgatory is already saved; none of them will go to hell. The living help them through prayer, the Mass, and indulgences, and the Church remembers them each year on All Souls' Day, November 2.

**What it sees truly.** Catholic teaching holds firmly to the core: the Trinity, the deity of Christ, His atoning death and bodily resurrection, the resurrection of the body, and the final judgment. Its teaching that no one is dragged into hell, but that hell is the fruit of a person's own final choice, agrees with Scripture's picture of people who *"loved darkness rather than light"* **(John 3:19)**. Its sense that the soul must be made wholly pure before it can stand in God's full presence takes seriously that *"there shall in no wise enter into it any thing that defileth"* **(Revelation 21:27)**. And its love for the dead, its refusal to forget them, reflects something true: in Christ, death does not break the fellowship of the saints.

In the light of my premise, the Catholic intuition about purification touches something real. If we live within God's own experience, the disorder sin leaves in us is not a legal fiction. It is real, and it must be dealt with.

**Where it goes wrong.** Purgatory itself is one of the open questions, and I do not count belief in a purification after death as error. Some of what the Church has built on that belief I do count as error.

The first is the idea that the living can shorten a soul's purification by indulgences drawn from a *treasury of merit* made up of the merits of Christ *and* the saints. Scripture names one source of merit: *"by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified"* **(Hebrews 10:14)**. The selling of indulgences in the sixteenth century was a scandal the Church itself later condemned, but indulgences are still granted today. The teaching that one believer's merit can be applied to another's debt blurs the line Scripture draws between Christ's work and ours.

The second is the danger, in practice if not in official doctrine, that a believer comes to see salvation as a lifelong balance between grace and his own effort. The Council of Trent condemned the view that we are justified *"by faith alone"* in the sense the Reformers meant it. Paul's words remain: *"For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast"* **(Ephesians 2:8–9)**.

The third is the practice of asking the departed saints to pray for us. The Church distinguishes this from worship, and the distinction is real. But Scripture gives no example of a believer addressing the dead, and it names one mediator: *"For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus"* **(1 Timothy 2:5)**.

**How we talk with one another.** A Catholic who trusts the risen Christ is my brother, and I begin there. I do not start with purgatory. I start with assurance. Many faithful Catholics have been taught that no one can know, this side of death, whether he will be saved. I ask him gently whether he knows where he will stand when he dies, and why. Then I show him what John wrote: *"These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life"* **(1 John 5:13)**. The aim is not to win a debate about purgatory but to help a brother rest his whole weight on the finished work of Christ.

## Eastern Orthodoxy

**What it teaches.** The Orthodox churches of Greece, Russia, Serbia, Romania, the Middle East, and beyond teach that at death the soul faces a particular judgment that gives it a foretaste of its final state. It then awaits the general judgment, when body and soul are reunited at the resurrection. The Church prays for the departed with great intensity, especially on the third, ninth, and fortieth days after death and on each anniversary. Families bring *koliva*, boiled wheat sweetened with honey or sugar, recalling Christ's words that a grain of wheat must die to bear fruit **(John 12:24)**. Orthodoxy rejects the Catholic doctrine of purgatory as a defined place of satisfying punishment, yet it prays for the dead without claiming to know exactly how those prayers help. Because the body will be raised, the Church generally forbids cremation, and many Orthodox churches will not give a church funeral to someone who chose it.

**What it sees truly.** Orthodoxy holds the full core of the faith. It has kept, perhaps better than any other branch, the sense that salvation is not only a legal acquittal but union with God. The Orthodox call this *theosis*, from Peter's words that we may become *"partakers of the divine nature"* **(2 Peter 1:4)**. That is close to the heart of my own premise. If we live within God and He experiences the world through us, then salvation is not a transaction at a distance. It is the restoring of a living union that sin had broken.

Orthodoxy also honors the body. Its refusal to treat the corpse as an empty shell reflects Paul's teaching that the body is sown in weakness and raised in power **(1 Corinthians 15:42–44)**. And its practice of praying without drawing a map is a kind of humility about the open questions that I respect.

**Where it goes wrong.** The weaknesses I see are less in Orthodox doctrine than in what it can become in practice. A believer can grow up surrounded by liturgy, icons, fasts, and memorial services without ever hearing plainly that he can know his sins are forgiven through faith in Christ. The richness of the tradition can become a substitute for the personal trust it was meant to express.

More specifically, some Orthodox writers teach that the soul passes through *aerial toll-houses* after death, where demons accuse it of its sins. This is not official dogma, and many Orthodox theologians reject it, but it is widely held. It has no clear footing in Scripture and can rob believers of the confidence Paul expressed: *"There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus"* **(Romans 8:1)**.

On cremation, I share the Orthodox reverence for the body but not its conclusion. The God who raises bodies from dust and scattered ashes after centuries is not hindered by fire. Scripture honors burial but nowhere forbids cremation, and the martyrs burned at the stake will not lose their resurrection.

Orthodoxy, like Catholicism, also invites the faithful to ask the departed saints for their prayers. I hold the same reservation here that I stated above.

**How we talk with one another.** I begin with *theosis*, because there we share real common ground. I ask my Orthodox brother how he understands union with God, and I tell him how I understand it: that God lives within our lives and feels them from inside. Then I ask the question that matters most. Does he trust that this union is already his, given freely in Christ, or does he hope it may be his if his life and his Church's prayers prove sufficient? Jesus answered that question for every believer: *"He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life"* **(John 5:24)**.

## Protestantism

**What it teaches.** The Protestant churches, including Lutherans, Reformed and Presbyterians, Anglicans, Baptists, Methodists, Pentecostals, and the many independent evangelical congregations, teach that after death a person is either with Christ or separated from God. There is no purgatory. A believer is saved by grace through faith alone, trusting Christ rather than his own works. Most Protestants hold that the believer's soul goes immediately into the Lord's presence at death and receives a resurrected body at Christ's return. Because nothing remains to purify, most Protestant churches do not pray for the dead.

The absence of purgatory is the reason Protestantism exists. In 1517, Martin Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses, many of which attacked the sale of indulgences. The friar Johann Tetzel was said to promise that as soon as the coin in the coffer rang, the soul from purgatory sprang. Luther's protest over that one practice grew into a recovery of the whole Gospel of grace.

**What it sees truly.** At its best, Protestantism recovered what the apostles preached: that salvation is God's free gift, received by trust in Christ alone, and that the believer can know he is saved. Paul wrote, *"Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ"* **(Romans 5:1)**. The Reformers also returned the Scriptures to ordinary people in their own languages, so that every believer could test what he was taught against the Word itself. This series stands on that principle.

The Protestant teaching that the believer goes at once to be with the Lord rests on real texts **(Philippians 1:23; 2 Corinthians 5:8)**, though, as I noted in the Introduction, other believers read those texts differently.

**Where it goes wrong.** Since this is my own family, I owe it the same honesty I give the others.

First, the doctrine of faith alone is easily cheapened into a one-time decision that asks nothing further. James warned against exactly this: *"Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone"* **(James 2:17)**. Faith that saves is living trust, and living trust changes how a man lives.

Second, popular Protestant teaching often pictures the afterlife as the soul's escape to a cloudy heaven, forgetting the resurrection of the body and the renewal of the earth **(Romans 8:21–23; Revelation 21:1–5)**. Scripture's hope is not that we leave creation behind but that God makes it new.

Third, Protestant preaching sometimes presents God as a distant judge who keeps a ledger from outside His creation and then grants a pardon from afar. My premise suggests a richer picture. God lives within every life He has made and feels each choice from inside. His law is not an external rulebook but the shape of what wounds or honors Him. When this is forgotten, obedience becomes mere rule-keeping, and the Cross becomes a legal transaction rather than the suffering love of the One in whom we live.

Finally, in rejecting prayers for the dead, many Protestants have also lost the habit of remembering them. Scripture calls the faithful departed *"so great a cloud of witnesses"* **(Hebrews 12:1)**. Honoring their memory is not a Catholic practice; it is a Christian one.

**How we talk with one another.** With a fellow Protestant, the conversation is usually not about doctrine but about life. I ask him whether his faith has changed how he lives, and whether he knows God as near or only as a judge far away. I remind him, and myself, that *"if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new"* **(2 Corinthians 5:17)**. The Gospel of grace is not only a door we walked through once. It is the house we live in.

## The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints

**What it teaches.** Latter-day Saints believe that at death the spirit enters a spirit world. The righteous rest in *paradise*, and those who did not accept the gospel wait in *spirit prison*, where the gospel is preached to them and they may still accept it. At the resurrection, nearly everyone receives a degree of glory in one of three kingdoms. The highest is the *celestial*, where those who have accepted Christ and received the temple ordinances live with God, and where the highest among them, sealed in eternal marriage, receive *exaltation*: to become like God. Below it are the *terrestrial* kingdom, for honorable people who did not fully accept the gospel, and the *telestial* kingdom, for the wicked who did not repent but are not lost. (The video's transcript garbles this name as "celestial.") Only a few, called *sons of perdition*, who knowingly deny the Holy Spirit after receiving a full witness, go to *outer darkness*.

Because the dead may accept the gospel, members are baptized in temples on behalf of their ancestors, citing Paul's mention of those *"baptized for the dead"* **(1 Corinthians 15:29)**. To identify those ancestors, the Church built FamilySearch, one of the largest free genealogical archives in the world.

**What it sees truly.** The Latter-day Saint picture of the afterlife is driven by a generous conviction: that God wants to save nearly everyone, and that no one should be lost merely because he never heard of Christ. That conviction has support in Scripture. God *"will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth"* **(1 Timothy 2:4)**, and Peter speaks of the gospel being *"preached also to them that are dead"* **(1 Peter 4:6)**. Whether the dead can still respond is one of the open questions.

Latter-day Saints also take the resurrection of the body seriously, honor the family as something meant to last, and generally live disciplined, moral lives. The labor they have poured into preserving the names of the dead has blessed millions of people of every faith who trace their families.

**Where it goes wrong.** The deepest error concerns who God is. Latter-day Saint teaching holds that God the Father has a body of flesh and bone, that He was once a man who progressed to godhood, and that faithful humans may progress to become gods as well. The Father, Son, and Holy Ghost are taught to be three separate beings, one in purpose.

This runs directly against the first premise of my theology and, more importantly, against Scripture. God says, *"before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me"* **(Isaiah 43:10)**, and *"from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God"* **(Psalm 90:2)**. God did not grow into His place within some larger universe. He is the self-existent One within whom everything else exists. Our destiny is not to become separate gods ruling our own creations, but to be united with the one God in whom we already live.

The second error concerns grace. The Book of Mormon teaches that *"it is by grace that we are saved, after all we can do"* **(2 Nephi 25:23)**. Many faithful members carry a heavy burden because of that phrase. No one has ever done all he can do. Paul's answer is that grace is grace precisely because it is not earned: *"And if by grace, then is it no more of works: otherwise grace is no more grace"* **(Romans 11:6)**.

The third concerns authority. Latter-day Saints accept the Book of Mormon, the Doctrine and Covenants, and the Pearl of Great Price as Scripture alongside the Bible, and they believe in continuing revelation through their prophets. Where these teachings contradict the Bible, as they do on the nature of God, I hold to the Bible.

As for baptism for the dead, 1 Corinthians 15:29 is one of the most obscure verses in the New Testament, and no one knows with certainty what practice Paul was describing. He mentions it once, in passing, as something *they* do, not something he commands. A whole system of temple work rests uneasily on so narrow a foundation.

**How to witness.** Latter-day Saints love Jesus, read the Bible, and live with conviction. I begin with respect, not with a list of errors. The most fruitful question I know is a personal one: *Have you done all you can do?* Many members, if they answer honestly, say no, and they feel the weight of it. That is the moment to share what Jesus offered: *"Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest"* **(Matthew 11:28)**. Once a person has tasted grace as a gift, the question of who God is can be explored together through the Bible, which he already trusts.

## Jehovah's Witnesses

**What it teaches.** Jehovah's Witnesses teach that the soul is not a separate part of a person but the whole living person, as when *"man became a living soul"* **(Genesis 2:7)**. At death, the person simply ceases to exist, like a dreamless sleep, until God raises him. There is no immortal soul and no place of eternal torment; *hell* in Scripture means the common grave. The incorrigibly wicked are destroyed forever, not tormented.

For the faithful, there are two hopes. A limited number, 144,000 **(Revelation 7:4; 14:1)**, are raised to heaven to rule with Christ. The rest, a *"great multitude"* **(Revelation 7:9)**, will be raised to live forever on a restored paradise earth. At the annual Memorial of Christ's death, the bread and wine are passed down every row, but only those who believe they belong to the 144,000 partake. In most congregations nearly everyone passes the plate untouched.

**What it sees truly.** Witnesses have recovered something many Christians have forgotten: that God's plan includes this earth. Jesus said, *"Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth"* **(Matthew 5:5)**. The Psalmist wrote, *"The righteous shall inherit the land, and dwell therein for ever"* **(Psalm 37:29)**. And John saw *"a new heaven and a new earth"* with God dwelling among men **(Revelation 21:1–3)**. A Christian hope that ends in disembodied souls floating in the clouds has lost this, and the Witnesses are right to insist on it.

Their views that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection and that the wicked are finally destroyed fall within the open questions I set out in the Introduction. Scripture supports both, and I do not count them as errors. Their belief that God raises the dead by re-creating them from His perfect memory also touches something true. If God experiences every life from inside, then nothing of a person is ever lost to Him.

Witnesses are also known for moral seriousness, mutual care, and a willingness to knock on strangers' doors that should shame many of us.

**Where it goes wrong.** The decisive error is about Jesus. Witnesses teach that Jesus was God's first creation, identified with the archangel Michael, and that He is *"a god"* but not God. Their own New World Translation renders John 1:1 that way. But John tells us that *"All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made"* **(John 1:3)**. If Jesus made everything that was made, He was not Himself made. When the apostle Thomas saw the risen Christ, he called Him *"My Lord and my God"* **(John 20:28)**, and Jesus accepted it. God commands, *"let all the angels of God worship him"* **(Hebrews 1:6)**, which He would never command toward a fellow angel. My own premise depends on this point: only the Creator, entering His own creation, could pay a debt no creature could pay.

The second error is the two-class hope. Nothing in Scripture limits the heavenly calling or the Lord's Supper to 144,000. Jesus said, *"Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you"* **(John 6:53)**. He gave the bread and cup to all His disciples, and Paul gave them to the whole church at Corinth **(1 Corinthians 11:23–26)**. Jesus spoke of *"one fold, and one shepherd"* **(John 10:16)**, not two classes of believers.

The third is the role of the organization. Witnesses are taught that the Governing Body is God's channel for understanding Scripture, and that remaining in good standing with the organization is necessary for survival at Armageddon. This places a human institution between the believer and Christ, and it makes salvation depend in practice on continued faithful service.

**How to witness.** Witnesses come to our doors well trained, and arguing verse against verse rarely helps. I would welcome them, thank them for their zeal, and agree warmly where we agree: on the restored earth, and on rejecting traditions that go beyond Scripture. Then I would ask them to read John 20:28 aloud with me and tell me whom Thomas was addressing. I would also ask a personal question: *Do you know that you will survive the judgment?* Most Witnesses will say they hope so if they remain faithful. That opens the door to Jesus' promise that whoever believes *"hath everlasting life"* **(John 5:24)**, now, as a present possession.

## Seventh-day Adventists

**What it teaches.** Seventh-day Adventists teach that death is an unconscious sleep. The dead *"know not any thing"* **(Ecclesiastes 9:5)**; no soul drifts toward the light at death, and a believer's grandmother is not watching over him from heaven. She is resting until Christ returns. At the Second Coming, the righteous dead are raised and taken to be with Christ for a thousand years. After the thousand years, the wicked are raised, judged, and destroyed by a fire that ends rather than one that burns forever. God then makes the earth new as the eternal home of the redeemed.

Because Adventists see the body as the whole person rather than a container for a soul, they take health seriously. Many are vegetarian, and most avoid alcohol and tobacco. In Loma Linda, California, home to a large Adventist community, long-term studies have found that Adventists outlive their neighbors by several years.

**What it sees truly.** Adventists hold the core of the Christian faith: the Trinity, the deity of Christ, His atoning death and bodily resurrection, and salvation by grace through faith. Their two most distinctive beliefs about the afterlife, soul sleep and the final destruction of the wicked, are both among the open questions. Serious evangelical scholars have held each of them, and I set out the texts that support them in the Introduction. I count Adventists as brothers in Christ, not as a separate religion.

Their care for the body is also deeply biblical: *"know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you… therefore glorify God in your body"* **(1 Corinthians 6:19–20)**. That fits my own premise well. If God lives our experience from within, then how we treat our bodies is not merely a private health choice. He feels it too. The Adventist life expectancy is the fruit of taking that seriously.

Their faithful keeping of a weekly day of rest is likewise a gift. Much of the modern church has forgotten that rest is a commandment.

**Where it goes wrong.** My concerns lie not with soul sleep but with two other Adventist teachings.

The first is the *investigative judgment*. Adventists teach that in 1844, Christ entered the Most Holy Place of the heavenly sanctuary and began reviewing the records of professed believers to determine who is worthy of eternal life. Whatever its intent, this teaching has left many Adventists unsure whether they will pass the review. Hebrews says Christ entered the holy place *"once"* and *"obtained eternal redemption for us"* **(Hebrews 9:12)**, and that we may enter *"with boldness"* now **(Hebrews 10:19)**. The doctrine also rests heavily on a particular reading of Daniel 8:14 that few interpreters outside Adventism share.

The second is the weight given to the writings of Ellen G. White. Adventists officially hold the Bible as their final authority, and White herself said so. In practice, her writings have often functioned as the lens through which Scripture is read, and some of the church's distinctive positions depend on her interpretations.

Some Adventists also teach that worship on Sunday will become the *mark of the beast* in the last days. Paul's counsel is gentler: *"One man esteemeth one day above another: another esteemeth every day alike. **Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind"* **(Romans 14:5)**.

**How we talk with one another.** I begin by agreeing about the body, the restored earth, and the honest reading of the texts on death. Then I turn to assurance. I ask my Adventist brother whether he knows he has eternal life now, or whether he is waiting to learn the outcome of a judgment already in progress. John wrote so that believers *"may know that ye have eternal life"* **(1 John 5:13)**. A man can keep the Sabbath gladly and still rest his whole hope on a finished work, not on a review still under way.

## Swedenborgianism (The New Church)

**What it teaches.** Emanuel Swedenborg (1688–1772) was a Swedish scientist and engineer who, in middle age, reported that the Lord had opened his spiritual sight. For the rest of his life he said he moved freely between this world and the spiritual world, and he described what he saw in many volumes, most famously *Heaven and Hell* (1758). The churches that follow his teaching call themselves the New Church.

Swedenborg taught that at death a person awakens immediately in a spiritual body in the *world of spirits*, an intermediate realm. There his outward manners fall away, and what he truly loved, his *ruling love*, comes to the surface and becomes his whole character. No one can pretend there. He is then drawn to the community of those who love what he loves. Heaven is made up of societies of people who love God and their neighbor. God sends no one to hell. People go there by their own choice, because heaven's light is painful to them and they feel at home among those like themselves. Helen Keller was among Swedenborg's devoted readers and wrote of her faith in his teaching.

**What it sees truly.** Swedenborg saw clearly that what we love shapes what we become, and that the afterlife reveals it. Jesus taught, *"For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also"* **(Matthew 6:21)**, and Paul warned, *"whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap"* **(Galatians 6:7)**. Scripture also says, *"God is love; and he that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God, and God in him"* **(1 John 4:16)**. Heaven as a society of love, and hell as the chosen home of those who cannot bear the light, echo Jesus' words that *"men loved darkness rather than light"* **(John 3:19)**.

This picture also fits my premise. If we live within God, then His presence is everywhere. What differs between heaven and hell is not where a person is, but whether he is in harmony with the One in whom he lives. Swedenborg also insisted, rightly, that Jesus Christ is fully God.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is the source. Swedenborg's teaching does not come from Scripture but from his own reported conversations with spirits and angels over nearly three decades. Scripture forbids seeking knowledge from the dead **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)** and warns that *"Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light"* **(2 Corinthians 11:14)**. Paul wrote that even if *"an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed"* **(Galatians 1:8)**.

The second is that Swedenborg rejected salvation by faith in Christ's atoning death. He taught that people are saved by becoming loving and useful, by a reformed character. But a man cannot remake his own ruling love. *"Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? **then may ye also do good, that are accustomed to do evil"* **(Jeremiah 13:23)**. A new heart is God's gift, not our achievement: *"A new heart also will I give you"* **(Ezekiel 36:26)**. If what we love simply ripens after death, then most of us are lost. The Cross is God's answer to that problem, and Swedenborg set it aside.

The third is that Swedenborg denied the bodily resurrection. He taught that we rise in a spiritual body immediately at death and that the material body is never raised. But the risen Jesus said, *"handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have"* **(Luke 24:39)**. He also taught that the Last Judgment took place in the spiritual world in 1757, a claim Scripture gives us no way to test and much reason to doubt.

Finally, Swedenborg rejected the Trinity of three persons in favor of a trinity of aspects within the one person of Jesus. I agree with him that there is only one God, but the Son prays to the Father and the Father speaks of the Son, which is hard to explain if they are not distinct persons **(John 17:1–5; Matthew 3:17)**.

**How to witness.** Readers of Swedenborg are usually thoughtful, well read, and spiritually serious. I would start with the ruling love, because there we largely agree. Then I would ask: *Can a man change what he loves by his own effort?* Most honest people know they cannot. That is where the Cross becomes good news rather than an abstraction. Christ did not only show us what love is; He paid what our disordered loves owe, and He gives the new heart we could never grow on our own.

## Unitarian Universalism

**What it teaches.** Unitarian Universalism has no required creed and no official teaching about death. In the same congregation one may find Christians, Buddhists, humanists, pagans, and atheists, each holding his own view of what comes after. Funerals center on the life the person actually lived and on what he believed, not on what the church believes. What holds a congregation together is a set of shared values, such as the worth of every person and a free and responsible search for truth.

The name records two older movements that merged in 1961. The Unitarians held that God is one person, not three, and most came to regard Jesus as a great teacher rather than as God. The Universalists held one of the boldest claims in Christian history: that every person, without exception, will finally be saved, and that no one faces eternal hell. Early American Universalists such as John Murray and Hosea Ballou preached that claim from the Bible and grounded it in the work of Christ.

**What it sees truly.** Unitarian Universalists are right that every person has worth, because every person bears God's image **(Genesis 1:27)**. They are right that truth should be sought freely and honestly, which is the spirit in which the Bereans *"searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so"* **(Acts 17:11)**. And the old Universalists saw something real in the breadth of God's love. As I noted in the Introduction, Scripture contains texts that speak of God reconciling *"all things unto himself"* **(Colossians 1:20)** and of all being *"made alive"* in Christ **(1 Corinthians 15:22)**. A Christian universalism that holds every person will finally be brought to God *through Christ*, by His Cross and His purifying judgment, is a minority view, but it is argued from Scripture by serious believers, and it belongs among the open questions.

**Where it goes wrong.** Modern Unitarian Universalism has kept the hope of the old Universalists while letting go of the Savior they preached. Without Christ, the claim that everyone will be saved has no foundation. Saved from what, and by whom? Scripture is plain that salvation comes through one name only: *"Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved"* **(Acts 4:12)**. Jesus said, *"I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me"* **(John 14:6)**. Even if God does, in the end, restore all, it will be because of the Cross, not in spite of it. My own premise explains why: no creature can repay what wrong has broken, so the payment must come from the Creator.

The Unitarian denial of Christ's deity runs into the same texts I cited for the Jehovah's Witnesses: the Word who *"was God"* and by whom *"all things were made"* **(John 1:1, 3)**.

Finally, a church that declines to say what happens at death leaves its grieving members with very little to hold. A free search for truth is good, but a search that never concludes is not freedom. Paul said that if Christ was not raised, our faith is empty; but *"now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept"* **(1 Corinthians 15:20)**. That is either true or false, and it is worth finding out.

**How to witness.** Unitarian Universalists value open inquiry, so I invite them to inquire. The resurrection of Jesus is a historical claim, made by eyewitnesses who were willing to die for it **(1 Corinthians 15:3–8)**. I ask whether they have ever examined the evidence for it with the same openness they bring to other questions. I also take their values seriously: if every person truly has worth, where does that worth come from? Christianity gives an answer: we are made by God, we live within Him, and He loved us enough to die for us. The worth they affirm is grounded in the Savior they have set aside.

The pattern is worth noticing. The questions that have divided Christians most bitterly over the centuries, about purgatory, the soul's sleep, and the duration of hell, turn out to be the ones Scripture leaves most open. The questions that truly separate a movement from the Christian faith are fewer and plainer: Who is Jesus? Is His Cross sufficient? Is salvation a gift or a wage?

In every case where a group departs from the core, the departure touches the same place. Each one, in a different way, makes the Savior smaller: a created angel, an exalted man, a moral teacher, or one guide among many. And each one, in a different way, makes our own part larger: faithful service to an organization, all we can do, a reformed character, or simply being a good person. The Gospel runs the other way. Christ is the Creator Himself, entering His own creation, and our part is to receive what He has done.

In the next essay I turn to the faiths closest to Christianity's roots: Judaism and its mystical tradition of Kabbalah, Islam, and the Persian and Middle Eastern faiths of the Baha'i, the Druze, the Yazidis, and the Zoroastrians.

# Part 3: Judaism, Islam, and the Persian Faiths

The second family in this survey shares Christianity's roots. Judaism is the faith of the people through whom God gave the Scriptures and the Messiah. Kabbalah is its mystical tradition. Islam claims Abraham as its father and honors Jesus as a prophet. The Baha'i faith grew out of Shia Islam in nineteenth-century Persia, and the Druze grew out of it in eleventh-century Egypt. The Yazidis of northern Iraq and the Zoroastrians of ancient Persia stand a little farther off, but both belong to the same region and have exchanged ideas with these faiths for centuries.

Every one of these traditions believes in one God, or in one supreme God above lesser beings. Most believe in a final judgment and a resurrection. In that sense they stand closer to the Bible than any family I will consider after this one. That closeness makes the remaining differences all the more important to see clearly. For each of them, the question is the same one Jesus asked His disciples: *"But whom say ye that I am?"* **(Matthew 16:15)**

## Judaism

**What it teaches.** Judaism says surprisingly little about what happens after death, and that reticence is deliberate. The Hebrew Scriptures speak mostly of *Sheol*, the shadowy place of the dead. Later Jewish teaching added more: *Olam Ha-Ba*, the world to come, and *techiyat ha-metim*, the resurrection of the dead. Maimonides made belief in the resurrection one of his thirteen principles of faith. The Mishnah teaches that *all Israel has a share in the world to come*, and the rabbis taught that the righteous of the nations share in it too.

Rabbinic tradition describes two destinations after death. *Gan Eden*, the Garden of Eden, is a paradise for the righteous. *Gehinnom* is a place where the soul is cleansed, and the Mishnah limits that cleansing for most souls to twelve months. That is why a child says the Mourner's Kaddish for a parent for only eleven months: to say it for twelve could imply that the parent needed the full cleansing. Mourning itself follows a fixed pattern: *shiva*, a week at home receiving visitors, and then a year of mourning for a parent.

Yet the emphasis stays on this life. What counts is keeping the commandments and doing right by one's neighbor, not drawing maps of what comes after.

**What it sees truly.** Christianity is not a different religion from the faith of Moses and the prophets; it is that faith fulfilled. Everything true in Jewish teaching about God is the foundation on which I stand: that God is one **(Deuteronomy 6:4)**, that He is holy and just, that He made man in His image, and that He keeps His covenants. Daniel promised that *"many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt"* **(Daniel 12:2)**. Job said, *"yet in my flesh shall I see God"* **(Job 19:26)**. The Pharisees held to the resurrection against the Sadducees who denied it **(Acts 23:8)**, and Jesus sided with the Pharisees on that point **(Matthew 22:29–32)**.

The Jewish insistence that this life matters, that obedience is how one loves God, fits my premise closely. If God lives within our lives, then how we treat our neighbor today is not a preparation for the real thing. It *is* the real thing, and God feels it. The Jewish idea of a limited, cleansing Gehinnom also belongs to the open question of purification after death.

**Where it goes wrong.** The decisive difference is the Messiah. I believe that Jesus of Nazareth is the Messiah promised in the Hebrew Scriptures, and that through Him God made the atonement the Law always pointed toward. The Law itself taught that *"it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul"* **(Leviticus 17:11)**. Since the destruction of the Temple in AD 70, Judaism has had no sacrifice. The rabbis taught that repentance, prayer, and charity take its place, and those are good things. But the Law never said they were enough. Isaiah described a Servant who would bear what we could not: *"he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed"* **(Isaiah 53:5)**. I believe that Servant has come.

This is, in the end, the question of who pays. Without a sacrifice, a man is left to work off his own debt, and my premise holds that no creature can do that, because his good works always arrive too late.

**How to witness.** Any Christian speaking with a Jewish friend must begin with humility. For centuries, people calling themselves Christians persecuted the Jews in Jesus' name, and that history is a real obstacle. Paul, himself a Jew, wrote that God *"hath not cast away his people which he foreknew"* **(Romans 11:2)**, and that the Gentile believer is a wild branch grafted into Israel's olive tree, not its replacement **(Romans 11:17–18)**.

The best place to start is in the Hebrew Scriptures themselves. I would invite a Jewish friend to read Isaiah 52:13 through 53:12 with me and ask him whom he thinks it describes. I might also tell him a story from the Talmud. When the great Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai lay dying, he wept. His students asked why. He answered that two roads lay before him, one to Gan Eden and one to Gehinnom, and he did not know on which he would be led **(Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 28b)**. If the greatest teacher of his generation could not be sure, who can? Jesus offers what Rabbi Yochanan longed for: *"He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life"* **(John 3:36)**.

## Kabbalah

**What it teaches.** Kabbalah is the mystical tradition within Judaism. Its central text, the *Zohar*, appeared in thirteenth-century Spain. Its most influential teacher, Rabbi Isaac Luria (1534–1572), taught in the town of Safed in the land of Israel.

Most people associate reincarnation with Hinduism and Buddhism, but Jewish mystics have taught it for centuries. Kabbalah calls it *gilgul*, the rolling or cycling of souls. Luria taught that a soul may return to this world to complete its *tikkun*, its repair: a commandment left unfulfilled, a wrong never made right. The belief is widely held today among Hasidic and many other Orthodox Jews. Many Jewish prayer books include, in the bedtime prayer, a promise to forgive anyone who has wronged you, *whether in this incarnation or in another*.

Behind this teaching lies a larger picture of creation. Luria taught that God, the *Ein Sof* or Infinite One, filled all things. To make room for a world, He withdrew Himself to open a space within His own being, a contraction called *tzimtzum*. Into that space He sent His light, but the vessels meant to hold it shattered, scattering sparks of holiness throughout creation. The work of every Jew, through prayer and the commandments, is to gather those sparks and repair the world.

**What it sees truly.** Of all the traditions in this survey, Luria's picture of creation comes closest to my own premise. God is infinite, and there is nothing outside Him. Creation therefore cannot be placed somewhere apart from God; it has to be a region opened *within* Him. I hold that the Son made and holds that region, *"upholding all things by the word of his power"* **(Hebrews 1:3)**. Luria reached his conclusion by a different road, but he saw the same structure. He also saw that creation is broken and waiting to be restored, as Paul wrote: *"the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now"* **(Romans 8:22)**.

The Kabbalist bedtime prayer of forgiveness also reflects the spirit of Jesus' teaching: *"if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses"* **(Matthew 6:15)**. To go to sleep each night having released every grudge is a discipline any Christian could learn from.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is reincarnation. Scripture says that *"it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**. Each person lives one earthly life, and that is part of the core I set out in the Introduction, not one of the open questions.

The second, and more important, is who does the repair. In Lurianic Kabbalah, the world is mended by human effort, one commandment at a time, across many lifetimes if necessary. The Bible teaches that the repair is God's own work, accomplished by the Messiah: *"And, having made peace through the blood of his cross, by him to reconcile all things unto himself"* **(Colossians 1:20)**. My premise explains why it has to be so. Disorder does not fade, and later good cannot reach back to cancel earlier wrong. A soul could return a thousand times and never finish its own tikkun. Only the Creator, entering His own creation, could generate a repair that has no debt of its own to pay.

The third is the method. Kabbalah builds elaborate systems from hidden meanings in the letters and numbers of the Torah. Moses warned that *"The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us"* **(Deuteronomy 29:29)**. Speculation beyond what God has revealed easily becomes a substitute for trusting what He has.

**How to witness.** A Jewish friend drawn to Kabbalah is usually a serious seeker who wants more than ritual. I would begin with *tzimtzum* and *tikkun*, because there we share real ground: creation exists within God, it is broken, and it must be repaired. Then I would ask: *Who finishes the tikkun?* Kabbalah itself says the final repair comes with the Messiah. I would then turn to Isaiah 53 and to the One who said, as He died, *"It is finished"* **(John 19:30)**.

## Islam

**What it teaches.** Islam teaches that at death the soul enters the *barzakh*, a barrier or intermediate state between death and the Day of Judgment **(Qur'an 23:100)**. On the first night in the grave, two angels, Munkar and Nakir, ask the dead three questions: Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet? Tradition holds that the grave then widens into comfort for the faithful or closes in on the unbeliever.

On the Day of Judgment, all the dead are raised bodily. Each person's deeds are weighed on a scale; those whose good deeds are heavy are saved, and those whose scales are light are lost **(Qur'an 101:6–9)**. Then souls cross the *Sirat*, a bridge over hell. The righteous cross into *Jannah*, the garden of paradise. Many Muslims believe that sinful believers may suffer in hell for a time before being brought out, especially through the Prophet's intercession.

Burial is quick, usually within a day. The body is washed, wrapped in plain white cloth, and laid in the grave facing Mecca. A well-known hadith says that when a person dies, his deeds come to an end except for three: ongoing charity, knowledge that continues to benefit others, and a righteous child who prays for him **(Sahih Muslim 1631)**. That is why many Muslims fund wells, plant trees, or endow schools in the name of someone who has died.

**What it sees truly.** Islam affirms a great deal of what the Bible teaches. There is one God, the Creator of all, who is merciful and just. Every person will be raised bodily and judged for what he has done. Heaven and hell are real. And Islam honors Jesus more than any other religion outside Christianity: the Qur'an calls Him the Messiah, teaches His virgin birth, records His miracles, and says He will return **(Qur'an 3:45–49; 4:158)**.

The hadith about deeds that continue after death also touches something true. If God lives within every life, then the good we set in motion keeps reaching others after we are gone, and He continues to feel it. Paul said something similar of the believer's work: *"if any man's work abide which he hath built thereupon, he shall receive a reward"* **(1 Corinthians 3:14)**.

Islam's reverence for the body in burial also honors the resurrection, which Muslims share with Christians.

**Where it goes wrong.** The deepest difference is about Jesus. The Qur'an denies that Jesus was crucified, saying that it only appeared so **(Qur'an 4:157)**, and it denies that He is the Son of God **(Qur'an 4:171)**. These are not small differences. The crucifixion is among the most firmly established facts of ancient history, attested by Roman and Jewish sources as well as Christian ones. And if Jesus did not die, then no one has paid for sin.

That leads to the second difference: the scale. In Islam, a person's eternity depends on whether his good deeds outweigh his bad ones, subject to God's mercy. But my premise holds that moral disorder does not fade, and that later good cannot reach back to cancel earlier wrong. Good deeds placed on a scale do not erase the bad ones; they simply sit beside them. Scripture says, *"by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight"* **(Romans 3:20)**.

The practical result is that a faithful Muslim cannot know where he will stand. The Qur'an itself records the Prophet saying that he did not know what would be done with him or with his followers **(Qur'an 46:9)**.

**How to witness.** Muslims value hospitality, sincerity, and respect for God. A Christian who prays, takes Scripture seriously, and lives modestly will be heard far more readily than one who argues. I would begin with what we share: one God, the coming judgment, and honor for Jesus.

I would then point to two bridges the Qur'an itself provides. First, it tells Muslims that God sent down the Torah and the Gospel as guidance and light **(Qur'an 5:46)**, and it tells them to consult those who read the earlier Scriptures **(Qur'an 10:94)**. I would invite a Muslim friend to read the Gospel of Luke with me. Second, every year at Eid al-Adha, Muslims remember how God spared Abraham's son by providing a ram in his place, which the Qur'an calls *a great sacrifice* **(Qur'an 37:107)**. I would ask: *What was the great sacrifice pointing to?* Then I would tell him what John the Baptist said when he saw Jesus: *"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world"* **(John 1:29)**.

Finally, I would ask gently whether he knows he will cross the bridge. Jesus' answer is the one every Muslim longs to hear: *"And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand"* **(John 10:28)**.

## The Baha'i Faith

**What it teaches.** The Baha'i faith began in nineteenth-century Persia with two figures: the Bab, who announced a new age in 1844, and Baha'u'llah (1817–1892), whom Baha'is regard as the latest in a line of *Manifestations of God*. That line includes Abraham, Moses, Krishna, Zoroaster, the Buddha, Jesus, and Muhammad, each bringing the same divine truth in a form suited to his age. This teaching is called *progressive revelation*.

Baha'is teach that the soul begins at conception, survives death, and continues to grow toward God forever. There is no finish line. Baha'u'llah's son and successor, 'Abdu'l-Baha, compared this life to the womb. A baby grows eyes, lungs, and limbs it cannot use until it is born. In the same way, we develop love, kindness, and spiritual insight in this world for use in the next. Heaven and hell are not places but states: nearness to God and distance from Him. The prayers of the living can help the souls of the departed.

Baha'i law directs that the body be buried no more than an hour's journey from the place of death. Cremation is not permitted, and embalming is avoided unless the law requires it.

**What it sees truly.** The Baha'i teaching that life begins at conception agrees with Scripture: *"Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee"* **(Jeremiah 1:5)**; *"thou hast covered me in my mother's womb"* **(Psalm 139:13)**.

The womb analogy is beautiful and, I think, true as far as it goes. Paul wrote that *"now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face"* **(1 Corinthians 13:12)**, and that *"Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard… the things which God hath prepared for them that love him"* **(1 Corinthians 2:9)**. What we become here is preparation for a life we cannot yet picture. The Baha'i idea of endless growth toward God echoes Paul's words that we are *"changed into the same image from glory to glory"* **(2 Corinthians 3:18)**.

The description of heaven and hell as nearness to God and distance from Him fits my premise closely. If everything exists within God, then the difference between heaven and hell cannot be a matter of geography. It is a matter of harmony or separation within the One in whom all things exist. The Baha'i practice of prayer for the departed belongs to the open questions. And its insistence on burial honors the body that will be raised.

**Where it goes wrong.** The central error is progressive revelation. It is attractive because it seems to honor every religion, but it cannot be true, because the Manifestations contradict one another on the most basic questions. Krishna taught that the soul is reborn many times; Jesus taught that each person lives once and then faces judgment. The Buddha taught that there is no permanent self; the Bible teaches that each soul will stand before God as himself. Baha'is accept that Jesus was crucified, but most Muslims read the Qur'an as saying He was not. Baha'i scholars work hard to harmonize these teachings, but the harmonizing usually requires reinterpreting what each founder plainly said. These are not different expressions of one truth. They are different claims, and they cannot all be right.

Scripture, moreover, does not present Jesus as one messenger in a series, but as God's final word: *"God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son"* **(Hebrews 1:1–2)**. Jesus warned that after Him many would come claiming to bring the next revelation **(Matthew 24:23–27)**, and Paul said that even an angel bringing another gospel should not be believed **(Galatians 1:8)**.

The Baha'i faith also interprets Jesus' resurrection spiritually rather than bodily. But the apostles were clear that the tomb was empty, and that Jesus ate with them and invited them to touch Him **(Luke 24:39–43)**. Without a bodily resurrection, there is no firstfruits of our own resurrection, and the Cross loses its vindication.

Finally, in the Baha'i view, spiritual progress comes through obedience to the teachings of the Manifestations and the development of virtues. The question of who pays for the wrong already done is never fully answered.

**How to witness.** Baha'is are usually gracious, well read, and committed to the unity of humanity, all of which I share. I would honor that commitment and then ask a simple question: *If the Manifestations all teach the same truth, does each soul live once or many times?* *And did Jesus' body leave the tomb, as His apostles said?* Truth cannot contradict itself, so someone must be mistaken. That opens the way to examining the evidence for the resurrection together. I would also point out that the unity Baha'is long for is exactly what Christ offers: *"There is neither Jew nor Greek… for ye are all one in Christ Jesus"* **(Galatians 3:28)**. True unity comes not from merging every religion into one, but from every person being reconciled to God through the One who died for all.

## The Druze

**What it teaches.** The Druze are a close-knit faith community of about a million people, living mainly in Lebanon, Syria, Israel, and Jordan. Their faith arose in eleventh-century Egypt from Ismaili Shia Islam, but it has long been distinct from Islam. The Druze call themselves *al-Muwahhidun*, the people of God's unity. Their scriptures, the *Epistles of Wisdom*, are known in full only to initiated members called the *uqqal*. The community closed to converts in the eleventh century; one can only be born Druze.

The Druze believe in immediate reincarnation. At the moment of death, the soul passes at once into a newborn human body. It is always reborn as a human, never as an animal. The number of souls was fixed at creation and never changes. When a young child speaks of another family, another house, or another name, the Druze call it *nutq*, speaking, and relatives take it seriously, sometimes visiting the family the child describes. Mourning is real, but it is held alongside the belief that the loved one has already begun a new life. At the end of time, the Druze believe, there will be a final judgment.

**What it sees truly.** The Druze hold firmly to the unity of God, which is the first article of biblical faith: *"Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD"* **(Deuteronomy 6:4)**. They also hold a high view of the human soul. Their refusal to believe that a person could ever be reborn as an animal reflects the biblical truth that man alone was made in God's image **(Genesis 1:27)**.

The Druze conviction that the person does not simply vanish at death, that the one we loved still exists, is a true intuition, and it fits my premise. If God experiences every life from within, then no person is ever lost to Him.

The Druze also honor Jethro, the father-in-law of Moses, as a great prophet, and his tomb near the Sea of Galilee is their most important shrine. Scripture records that Jethro rejoiced at what God had done for Israel and confessed, *"Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods"* **(Exodus 18:11)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is reincarnation, which Scripture rules out: *"it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

The second is a consequence of the first. If the soul of the person I lost is now a child in another family, then he is no longer the one I knew. The Christian hope is different and, I think, far greater: the same person, raised in a body, reunited with those who loved him. Paul comforted the grieving with exactly that promise: *"For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him"* **(1 Thessalonians 4:14)**.

The third is that salvation in the Druze faith comes through knowledge of the hidden truth and faithfulness to the community's moral law, across many lifetimes. That knowledge is reserved for an initiated few. The Gospel is the opposite of a secret. Jesus said, *"I spake openly to the world… and in secret have I said nothing"* **(John 18:20)**. And it is offered to everyone, not only to those born into a particular people.

**How to witness.** Druze communities are tightly bound together, and leaving the faith can cost a person his family and community. A Christian must approach with patience and real friendship, not with pressure. Jethro offers a natural place to begin. I would ask a Druze friend about Jethro and then read Exodus 18 with him. From there I would turn to the Prophet whom Moses promised: *"The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken"* **(Deuteronomy 18:15)**, a promise the apostles applied to Jesus **(Acts 3:22)**. And I would share the hope that the one we lose at death is not reborn as a stranger, but will be raised, as himself, in Christ.

## The Yazidis

**What it teaches.** The Yazidis are a Kurdish-speaking people whose homeland lies in northern Iraq, around the Sinjar Mountains and the holy valley of Lalish. Their ancient faith teaches that God created the world and then entrusted it to seven holy beings, chief among them *Tawusi Melek*, the Peacock Angel. Outsiders have long slandered the Yazidis as devil-worshippers by identifying Tawusi Melek with Satan, an identification the Yazidis themselves reject. That slander helped fuel centuries of persecution, culminating in the genocide carried out against them by the Islamic State in 2014. Like the Druze, the Yazidis do not accept converts.

The Yazidis have a gentle name for death: *changing the shirt*. The soul leaves one body and passes into another, wearing each body for a while, as a person wears a shirt, until it wears out. Every Yazidi also chooses, during his lifetime, a *brother or sister of the hereafter*, a spiritual sibling from one of the religious families. That companion has real duties when he dies and is believed to stand beside him in the next world.

**What it sees truly.** The Yazidi image of the body as a garment has a striking parallel in Paul, who called the body a tent and wrote of our longing *"to be clothed upon with our house which is from heaven"* **(2 Corinthians 5:2)**. The Yazidi belief that God works through holy angels also has biblical support. The angels are *"ministering spirits, sent forth to minister for them who shall be heirs of salvation"* **(Hebrews 1:14)**.

Most moving of all is the brother of the hereafter. The Yazidis have understood that no one should face death alone, and that we need a companion we chose and trusted long before the hour came. That is a deep and true instinct. David wrote, *"Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me"* **(Psalm 23:4)**.

The Yazidis have also kept their faith through persecution that few peoples have endured. That faithfulness deserves honor.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is transmigration. Scripture teaches that each person lives once and then faces judgment **(Hebrews 9:27)**. And Paul's point about the garment is the opposite of the Yazidi one. He did not long to trade one shirt for another, but to be *"clothed upon, that mortality might be swallowed up of life"* **(2 Corinthians 5:4)**: the same person, raised and made new.

The second concerns God's nearness. In Yazidi belief, God created the world and handed its running to the seven angels. My premise holds the reverse. God is not distant from His world; He lives within every life He has made. Paul told the Athenians that God is *"not far from every one of us: For in him we live, and move, and have our being"* **(Acts 17:27–28)**. Angels serve Him, but they do not stand in His place, and Scripture warns against honoring them as mediators **(Colossians 2:18)**.

The third is that the Yazidi faith offers no answer to the question of who pays for the wrong we have done. The soul simply moves on to the next shirt.

**How to witness.** Many Yazidis today are refugees who survived unspeakable suffering, and there are now Yazidi communities in Germany and in the United States, notably in Lincoln, Nebraska. The first witness to a Yazidi family is practical love: help with resettlement, language, work, and grief. Words come later and mean more for it.

When the time comes for words, the brother of the hereafter is the natural place to begin. I would tell a Yazidi friend that the companion he longs for has already come. Jesus *"is not ashamed to call them brethren"* **(Hebrews 2:11)**. He went through death Himself so that He could walk beside us through it, and He promised, *"I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee"* **(Hebrews 13:5)**. He is the brother of the hereafter who does not only stand beside us at the judgment but has already paid what we owe.

## Zoroastrianism

**What it teaches.** Zoroastrianism is one of the oldest living religions, the ancient faith of Persia founded by the prophet Zarathustra (Zoroaster in Greek). Scholars disagree on when he lived; estimates range from about 1500 to 600 BC. Today perhaps one or two hundred thousand Zoroastrians remain, most of them Parsis in India and smaller communities in Iran and abroad.

Zoroastrians worship *Ahura Mazda*, the Wise Lord, who is wholly good. Opposing him is a destructive spirit, *Angra Mainyu*, and the whole of history is a struggle between them. Every person takes part in that struggle through his choices. The heart of the faith is a three-part rule: good thoughts, good words, good deeds.

After death, the soul stays near the body for three nights. At dawn on the fourth day it must cross the *Chinvat Bridge*, the Bridge of the Separator. For the righteous, the bridge is wide, and the soul is met by its own conscience in the form of a beautiful maiden who leads it to the House of Song. For the wicked, the bridge narrows to a blade's edge, and the soul falls into the House of Lies.

But hell is not forever. At the end of time comes *Frashokereti*, the renewal of the world. A savior, the *Saoshyant*, will appear. The dead will be raised, and every person will pass through a river of molten metal. To the righteous it will feel like warm milk; to the wicked it will burn. When it is over, every soul is purified, evil is destroyed, and the world is made new.

**What it sees truly.** Zoroastrianism saw, perhaps earlier than any other faith outside Israel, much of what the Bible teaches about the end. There is a real war between good and evil, and every choice matters in it. Paul wrote that *"we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers"* **(Ephesians 6:12)**. There will be a resurrection of the dead, a judgment, a coming Savior, and a renewal of the world: *"Behold, I make all things new"* **(Revelation 21:5)**.

The image of the soul meeting its own conscience at the bridge is also profound. What we have become meets us at death. And the vision of fire that is gentle to the righteous and painful to the wicked touches the open question of purification after death. Malachi described the Lord as *"like a refiner's fire"* **(Malachi 3:2)**, and Paul wrote that the fire *"shall try every man's work"* **(1 Corinthians 3:13)**. The Zoroastrian hope that all will finally be purified belongs to the related question of universal reconciliation that I set out in the Introduction.

I would add one more point that fits my premise. The Zoroastrian conviction that a kind word spoken this morning is a real blow struck in a cosmic war is not exaggeration. If God lives within our lives and feels every choice, then no act is small.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is dualism. In Zoroastrian teaching, the evil spirit is an independent power opposing God, not a creature in rebellion against Him. Scripture leaves no room for a rival to God. Through Isaiah, God spoke to the Persian king Cyrus: *"I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me…** I form the light, and create darkness"* **(Isaiah 45:5, 7)**. My premise points the same way: there is nothing outside God, so evil cannot be a second power beside Him. It is a sealed-off region within His creation, permitted for the sake of free love, and destined to end.

The second is the path to the wide bridge. In Zoroastrianism, what decides a person's crossing is the balance of his thoughts, words, and deeds. But no one's balance is good enough, and later good cannot cancel earlier wrong. The Zoroastrian story has a Savior at the end of history, but no one who pays for sin in the middle of it.

**How to witness.** Zoroastrians are a small community, and many traditional Parsis do not accept converts or encourage marriage outside the faith. Respect for that history matters.

The richest bridge I know is in the Gospel of Matthew. When Jesus was born, *"there came wise men from the east to Jerusalem, Saying, Where is he that is born King of the Jews?** for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him"* **(Matthew 2:1–2)**. The Greek word for those wise men is *magoi*, the title of the Persian priestly class, very likely Zoroastrian priests. I would tell a Zoroastrian friend that his own spiritual ancestors were among the first people on earth to worship Jesus. They were watching for a Savior, and they found Him. Then I would invite him to consider whether the Saoshyant his tradition awaits has already come, and whether the One the Magi worshiped is the One who carries us across the bridge.

# Part 4: The Religions of India

With the religions of India the question changes. The faiths of the previous two essays asked how a soul reaches heaven. Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, and Jainism start from a different picture. Each assumes that the soul, or something like it, passes through an endless cycle of death and rebirth, called *samsara*, driven by *karma*, the moral weight of one's own actions. The goal is not to reach heaven, which in these traditions is only another temporary stop in the cycle. The goal is to escape the cycle altogether. Hindus call that escape *moksha*, Buddhists *nirvana*, Sikhs *mukti*, and Jains *kevala* and liberation.

These traditions have thought more deeply than almost any others about the problem of moral cause and effect. They have also, at their best, reached toward grace, and in one case, Pure Land Buddhism, come startlingly close to it. For each of them, the questions from my Introduction become sharp: Does the person survive? And who pays?

## Hinduism

**What it teaches.** Hinduism is not one system but a family of traditions with a shared outlook. Its basic teaching is that you do not *have* a soul; you *are* one. The true self, the *atman*, is eternal, and the body is a temporary garment. The atman is born again and again in samsara, and karma determines whether the next life is higher or lower, human or otherwise. Even the heavens are temporary. The *Bhagavad Gita* teaches that souls who enjoy heaven return to the mortal world when their merit is spent **(Bhagavad Gita 9:21)**. Only *moksha*, release from the cycle, is permanent.

What moksha means depends on the school. In *Advaita Vedanta*, the school best known in the West, the atman is identical with *Brahman*, the one ultimate reality, and liberation is the realization that the individual self was never truly separate. In the schools of Ramanuja and Madhva, the soul remains distinct, living forever in loving relationship with a personal God. Many Hindus walk the path of *bhakti*, devotion to a personal God such as Vishnu or Krishna, and trust His grace rather than their own effort.

At death, the body is cremated to free the soul, and the ashes are often scattered in a sacred river, ideally the Ganges. Some Hindus travel to the holy city of Varanasi to die, believing that death there grants moksha.

**What it sees truly.** Hinduism sees that the moral order is real. Karma is close to Paul's warning that *"whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap"* **(Galatians 6:7)**. It sees that the self is more than the body, and that earthly reward, even heavenly reward, is not the final goal. Jesus said the same: *"For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?"* **(Matthew 16:26)**.

Above all, Hinduism has seen something close to the center of my premise. The Upanishads teach that the divine dwells within every living being. I believe God does experience the world from inside every life He has made. But my premise draws a line that Advaita erases. God experiences the world *through* many distinct persons, and He loves each one as himself. Ramanuja's school comes closer to the biblical picture. Ramanuja taught that souls are like the body of God, wholly dependent on Him, living within Him, yet forever distinct. That is near to Paul's *"in him we live, and move, and have our being"* **(Acts 17:28)**.

The bhakti tradition has also reached toward grace. In the Gita's climactic verse, Krishna tells Arjuna to abandon all other duties and take refuge in him alone, promising to free him from all sins **(Bhagavad Gita 18:66)**. That longing for a God who forgives freely is a true longing.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is reincarnation. Each of us lives once, and then faces judgment **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

The second is that karma, as Hinduism conceives it, has no one to pay it. It is an impersonal law that grinds on across lifetimes. My premise agrees that moral disorder is conserved: it does not fade, and later good cannot cancel earlier wrong. But that is exactly why karma, left to itself, can never be exhausted. A soul could live a million lives and never balance the account. Only a payment from outside the cycle, by one who owes nothing, can end it.

The third is the loss of the person. In Advaita, liberation means recognizing that the individual self was an illusion. But Scripture's hope is personal: *"I shall see God: Whom I shall see for myself, and mine eyes shall behold, and not another"* **(Job 19:26–27)**.

The fourth concerns incarnation. Hinduism teaches that Vishnu descends again and again as *avatars* to restore order. Christianity teaches one incarnation, unrepeatable, whose purpose was not only to restore order but to die for sin: *"now once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself"* **(Hebrews 9:26)**.

**How to witness.** Hindus are often deeply spiritual and open to Jesus as a holy teacher, even as one avatar among many. The challenge is to show that He is not one among many.

I would begin with bhakti. I would ask a Hindu friend whether he has ever longed for the forgiveness the Gita promises, and whether he has found it. Then I would tell him about the God who did not merely appear, but died, to free us from our sins. The great Indian Christian Sadhu Sundar Singh, raised as a Sikh in the Punjab, described the peace he found in Christ in terms any devout Hindu would understand: not escape from the self, but the self at last at rest in God. Jesus' invitation speaks to every soul weary of the wheel: *"Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest"* **(Matthew 11:28)**.

## Buddhism

**What it teaches.** Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, lived in northern India around the fifth century BC. He taught *Four Noble Truths*: that life is marked by suffering and dissatisfaction; that suffering arises from craving; that suffering ends when craving ends; and that the way to end it is the Eightfold Path of right understanding, conduct, and meditation.

Buddhism teaches rebirth. At death, the momentum of a person's karma carries into a new life, in one of six realms: gods, demigods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, and hell beings. None of these is permanent; even a god's life eventually ends. The cycle continues until craving and ignorance are fully extinguished. That extinguishing is *nirvana*, a word that means the blowing out of a flame.

What sets Buddhism apart is the teaching of *anatta*, no self. There is no permanent soul at the core of a person, only a changing stream of body, feelings, perceptions, thoughts, and consciousness. So what is reborn is not *you*. An early Buddhist text, *The Questions of King Milinda*, compares rebirth to lighting one lamp from another: the flame carries on, but it is not the same flame. Something of what you did goes on, but it will not answer to your name.

**What it sees truly.** The Buddha saw clearly that life in this world, apart from God, is marked by dissatisfaction. The Preacher said the same: *"all is vanity and vexation of spirit"* **(Ecclesiastes 1:14)**. He saw that craving lies at the root of much of our misery, as James also saw: *"every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed.** Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin"* **(James 1:14–15)**. He saw that everything visible is passing: *"the things which are seen are temporal"* **(2 Corinthians 4:18)**. And Buddhism has produced remarkable traditions of compassion, discipline, and self-examination.

Even the teaching of no self contains a truth my premise affirms. Only God is self-existent; He *"only hath immortality"* **(1 Timothy 6:16)**. No creature holds its own existence in itself. If we examine ourselves looking for a self that stands on its own, we will not find one, because there is none. But the conclusion the Buddha drew is not the only one. My identity does not rest on some hidden substance at my core. It rests on God, who experiences my life from within and holds it as mine. The person is real because God knows him by name: *"I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine"* **(Isaiah 43:1)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is the loss of the person. If there is no self, then there is no one to love, no one to forgive, and no one to be raised. The Christian hope is that the very person who died will rise. Jesus said of the dead patriarchs that God *"is not the God of the dead, but of the living"* **(Matthew 22:32)**. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob still answer to their names.

The second is the absence of a Creator. The Buddha set aside the question of God as unprofitable. But without a personal God, there is no one to whom we owe our wrongs, and no one who can forgive them. Karma becomes a law with no lawgiver and no pardon.

The third is the method. Buddhism calls each person to work out his own liberation by his own effort. Tradition records the Buddha's final counsel to his followers to be lamps unto themselves. But no one has ever extinguished his own craving. Paul, who tried harder than most, confessed, *"For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do"* **(Romans 7:19)**. His answer was not greater effort but a Deliverer: *"I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord"* **(Romans 7:25)**.

The fourth is the goal. Nirvana is the extinction of craving, and with it the end of the cycle. Scripture's goal is not the blowing out of a flame but fullness of life: *"I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly"* **(John 10:10)**. Desire is not destroyed but redeemed: *"Delight thyself also in the LORD; and he shall give thee the desires of thine heart"* **(Psalm 37:4)**.

**How to witness.** Buddhists are often thoughtful, honest about human nature, and wary of dogma. I would begin where the Buddha began: with suffering and craving. Here we agree more than most Christians realize. Then I would ask honestly: *Has your practice extinguished your craving?* Most sincere practitioners will say they are still on the path, perhaps for many lifetimes more. That opens the door to grace. The Buddha said he showed the way. Jesus said, *"I am the way, the truth, and the life"* **(John 14:6)**. He did not only point toward freedom from craving; He gives a new heart that desires God, and He pays for what our old cravings have done.

## Tibetan Buddhism

**What it teaches.** Tibetan Buddhism belongs to the *Vajrayana* branch of Buddhism, which teaches that liberation can be reached swiftly through advanced meditation and ritual under a qualified teacher. It shares the general Buddhist teaching of rebirth, but it gives special attention to the moment of death and the period that follows.

Death opens the *bardo*, an intermediate state that can last up to forty-nine days, between the life just ended and the next birth. To guide the dead through it, a lama or family member reads aloud from a text known in the West as *The Tibetan Book of the Dead*. Its Tibetan title means *Liberation Through Hearing in the Intermediate State*. The text tells the departed not to be afraid. First comes a brilliant *clear light*; then come visions of peaceful and terrifying deities. The instruction is simple: recognize them all as projections of your own mind, and you will be liberated. If the dead fail to recognize them, they are drawn toward another birth.

Because death is the greatest opportunity for liberation, practitioners train for it through meditation their whole lives. The body, by contrast, receives little attention. In a traditional *sky burial*, it is carried to a mountainside and offered to vultures, a final act of generosity that feeds living things with a body the mind no longer needs.

**What it sees truly.** Tibetan Buddhism takes death more seriously than almost any other tradition. It treats the moment of death as the most important moment of a life, and it prepares for it every day. Moses prayed, *"So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom"* **(Psalm 90:12)**. Most modern people, including many Christians, avoid thinking about death until it arrives. The Tibetans are wiser.

The *Book of the Dead* also speaks a word every dying person needs: do not be afraid. And it describes meeting a brilliant light at death. That fits my premise closely. I hold that God is light, and that *"in him is no darkness at all"* **(1 John 1:5)**. If the soul at death comes nearer to the One in whom it lives, it is no surprise that it would meet light.

**Where it goes wrong.** The decisive difference concerns what the light is. The *Book of the Dead* tells the departed that the clear light is the nature of his own mind. Scripture says the light is a Person: *"In him was life; and the life was the light of men"* **(John 1:4)**. Jesus said, *"I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life"* **(John 8:12)**. If the light we meet at death is God Himself, then recognizing it as our own mind is the one mistake we must not make. The light does not ask to be recognized as ourselves. It asks to be received as Lord.

The second problem is that liberation depends on recognition, on knowing the right thing at the right moment, which in turn depends on a lifetime of disciplined practice. But a man's standing before God does not depend on whether he remembers the right instruction while dying. It depends on whether his sins have been paid for.

The third is rebirth itself. Scripture gives no forty-nine days of passage toward another birth. *"It is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** Tibetan Buddhists, and the many Westerners drawn to their tradition, value preparation for death and take spiritual practice seriously. I would honor that and then ask: *What is the light?* If the light at death is only the mind, why does every account of it describe something so much greater than the self? I would read John 1:1–9 together and suggest that the light is not something to be recognized as oneself, but Someone who knows you by name and has already made a way for you. A Christian can also read Scripture to the dying, as the Tibetans read their text. Psalm 23 has carried countless souls through the valley without fear.

## Pure Land Buddhism

**What it teaches.** For millions of Buddhists in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, liberation does not depend on a lifetime of meditation. It depends on trust. Pure Land Buddhism centers on *Amitabha* Buddha, called *Amida* in Japan. According to the Pure Land sutras, long ages ago a monk named Dharmakara made a series of vows, the most important of which promised that anyone who sincerely calls on his name would be born into his Pure Land, a realm of bliss in the West. Dharmakara fulfilled his vows and became Amitabha. Followers therefore recite his name. In Japan the phrase is *Namu Amida Butsu*, "I take refuge in Amida Buddha."

The Pure Land is not the final goal. It is a place with perfect conditions for completing the path to enlightenment, free of distraction and temptation.

In thirteenth-century Japan, the monk Shinran took this teaching further than anyone before him. He founded *Jodo Shinshu*, the True Pure Land school, which remains the largest Buddhist school in Japan. Shinran taught that salvation comes entirely through *other-power*, Amida's vow, and not at all through *self-power*, one's own efforts. Even faith itself is Amida's gift. His most famous saying, recorded by a disciple, runs: if even a good person can be born in the Pure Land, how much more an evil one. The person who knows he cannot save himself relies on Amida most completely.

**What it sees truly.** Of every religion outside the biblical family, Pure Land Buddhism, and Shinran's form of it above all, comes closest to the Gospel of grace. When Christian missionaries first met it in Japan in the sixteenth century, they were startled to find what looked like Martin Luther's teaching on the other side of the world. In the twentieth century the Swiss theologian Karl Barth studied it and judged it the closest parallel to Reformation Christianity in any religion outside the Bible.

Shinran saw what the Bible teaches: that we cannot save ourselves, and that the attempt to do so is itself a kind of pride. *"For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God"* **(Ephesians 2:8)**. He saw that those who know their own evil are closer to salvation than those who trust their own goodness, as Jesus taught: *"I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance"* **(Luke 5:32)**. And the practice of calling on a saving name has a striking echo in Scripture: *"For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved"* **(Romans 10:13)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** Barth, after noting the parallel, named the one difference that decides everything: the name of Jesus Christ. That is my judgment as well.

Amitabha's story is told in sutras about events in a distant past age, in no place on earth, witnessed by no one. Jesus lived in a particular town, under a particular Roman governor, and died and rose before witnesses. Peter wrote, *"For we have not followed cunningly devised fables, when we made known unto you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but were eyewitnesses of his majesty"* **(2 Peter 1:16)**. Grace that rests on a vow in a story is a beautiful hope. Grace that rests on an empty tomb is a fact.

The second difference concerns justice. Amida's vow promises mercy, but it does not explain how the wrong already done is set right. My premise holds that moral disorder is conserved and must be answered exactly, not merely overlooked. A promise of mercy cannot cancel a debt; only a payment can. Jesus did not only vow to save. He *"gave himself a ransom for all"* **(1 Timothy 2:6)**. In the Cross, God's mercy and His justice meet, so that He is *"just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus"* **(Romans 3:26)**.

The third is the destination. The Pure Land is a stopping place on the way to enlightenment, not eternal communion with a personal God. The Christian hope is not better conditions for completing our own path. It is to be with Him: *"that where I am, there ye may be also"* **(John 14:3)**.

**How to witness.** With a Pure Land Buddhist, I would begin with deep agreement. We both believe that we cannot save ourselves and that salvation is a gift received by trust. I would tell him how much his tradition resembles the Christian Gospel, and that one of the twentieth century's greatest Christian theologians said so. Then I would ask the question that matters: *What makes Amida's vow effective? Who paid for the evil it forgives?* I would tell him about the One who did not merely vow but came, in history, and paid. He is the evil person's Savior Shinran was looking for: *"Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief"* **(1 Timothy 1:15)**.

## Sikhism

**What it teaches.** Sikhism was founded in the Punjab by Guru Nanak (1469–1539) and shaped by nine Gurus who followed him. Its scripture, the *Guru Granth Sahib*, is honored as the eternal living Guru. Sikhs believe in one God, *Waheguru*, the Creator, who is formless, eternal, and present in all things. The Sikh scripture opens with the words *Ik Onkar*: there is One.

Sikhs believe the soul is reborn according to its karma through as many as 8.4 million forms of life. Among all of these, a human birth is rare and precious, because only as a human can the soul escape the cycle and merge with God. That liberation is called *mukti*. It does not come through empty ritual, pilgrimage, or asceticism, but through three everyday disciplines: remembering God's name, earning an honest living, and sharing with others. The great obstacle is *haumai*, the ego, the self-centeredness that separates the soul from God. Liberation is also a gift; the opening hymn of the Sikh scripture closes with the words *Gur Prasad*, by the Guru's grace. Sikh teaching holds that a person can be liberated while still alive.

Death is accepted as God's will, so loud mourning is discouraged. Mourners sing hymns and recite the evening prayer, *Kirtan Sohila*. The body is cremated, and Sikh teaching discourages monuments to the dead.

**What it sees truly.** Sikhism holds a strong faith in one Creator God, which Guru Nanak proclaimed against both idolatry and empty ritual. It teaches that ego is the root of our separation from God, which agrees with Scripture: *"Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall"* **(Proverbs 16:18)**. Its three disciplines are deeply biblical. The Psalmist delights in remembering God's name **(Psalm 145:1–2)**; Paul commands, *"if any would not work, neither should he eat"* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**; and the early church *"had all things common"* **(Acts 2:44)**. Sikh *langar*, the free communal kitchen at every gurdwara, where anyone of any caste or faith may eat, is one of the great works of hospitality in the world.

Sikhism also teaches that this human life is the decisive chance, which is close to the biblical urgency: *"behold, now is the day of salvation"* **(2 Corinthians 6:2)**. And it recognizes that liberation comes by grace. Both of those insights reach toward the Gospel.

My premise finds a further point of contact. Sikh scripture teaches that God pervades every heart. I agree that God lives within every life He has made.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is reincarnation. A human birth is not one rare chance among millions of lives. It is the only one: *"It is appointed unto men once to die"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

The second concerns grace. Sikhism teaches that liberation comes by grace, but it does not say how God's grace deals with the wrong already done. My premise holds that wrong is conserved and must be answered, not merely overlooked. Remembering God's name, however faithfully, does not undo what the ego has already done. Grace in the Bible is free to us because it was costly to God. *"Ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold… But with the precious blood of Christ"* **(1 Peter 1:18–19)**.

The third concerns the goal. Sikh writers differ on whether merging with God means the soul keeps its own identity. Scripture is clear that it does. We will be like Christ, but we will still be ourselves, and *"we shall see him as he is"* **(1 John 3:2)**.

**How to witness.** Sikhs value courage, honesty, and service, and a Christian who lives those things earns a hearing. I would begin with haumai. I would ask a Sikh friend whether his disciplines have freed him from ego, and whether he knows he will merge with God at death. Then I would tell him the story of Sadhu Sundar Singh. Born into a devout Sikh family in the Punjab in 1889, Sundar Singh was so hostile to Christianity as a boy that he publicly burned a Bible. Weeks later, in despair, he prayed that God would reveal Himself before morning. He later said that Jesus appeared to him in his room, and he became one of the best-loved Christian teachers in Indian history, wearing the saffron robe of a holy man and preaching Christ across India and Tibet. The grace the Gurus spoke of has a name and a face, and He paid its price.

## Jainism

**What it teaches.** Jainism took its present form under Mahavira, a contemporary of the Buddha in the sixth or fifth century BC, whom Jains honor as the last of twenty-four *Tirthankaras*, or ford-makers, teachers who crossed the river of rebirth and showed the way across. Jainism teaches that every living being, from humans and animals to plants and even tiny organisms in earth and water, has a soul, a *jiva*.

What makes Jainism distinctive is its teaching that karma is not a metaphor but a literal substance: fine particles of matter that cling to the soul through its actions, especially through harming other living things. That is why Jains practice *ahimsa*, nonviolence, with extraordinary rigor. Many avoid root vegetables, because pulling them up kills the whole plant and the small creatures in the soil. Monks sweep the ground before them as they walk.

At death, the soul is reborn as a god, a human, an animal or plant, or a hell being, according to the karma it carries. Here lies the surprise: good deeds bind karma too. Kindness produces good karma, but it still sticks to the soul and keeps it in the cycle. Good karma is valuable because it leads to births where liberation is possible, but the final goal is to shed all karma, good and bad alike. When the last particle falls away, the soul rises to the top of the universe and remains there forever in perfect knowledge and bliss. Jainism has no Creator God; the universe is eternal, and each soul must liberate itself.

**What it sees truly.** Jainism sees that every living creature matters. Scripture agrees: *"A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast"* **(Proverbs 12:10)**, and not one sparrow *"shall fall on the ground without your Father"* **(Matthew 10:29)**.

More striking still, Jainism sees what most religions overlook: that wrongdoing is not a passing mood but a real weight that clings and does not simply wear off. That is very close to my premise that moral disorder is conserved. It does not fade, and later good cannot reach back to cancel earlier wrong. The writer of Hebrews uses the same image: *"let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which doth so easily beset us"* **(Hebrews 12:1)**.

Jainism also sees that good works cannot be the final goal, that even our virtues, offered as merit, become one more thing binding us. Isaiah said the same: *"all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags"* **(Isaiah 64:6)**. The Jain is right that being a good person is not the finish line.

**Where it goes wrong.** The Jain diagnosis is profound; the cure is impossible. If karma is a real weight that clings to the soul, and every action adds more, then no amount of austerity can shed it. Even the rigorous discipline meant to burn karma away is itself an action. Jain tradition acknowledges how rare full liberation is. Paul warned against exactly this kind of severe self-discipline as a path to holiness: such rules *"have indeed a shew of wisdom in will worship, and humility, and neglecting of the body"* but are of no value *"against the indulging of the flesh"* **(Colossians 2:23)**.

The second problem is the absence of a Creator. Without a God to whom we owe our wrongs, there is no one who can lift the weight from us. Each soul is left alone with its load.

The third is the goal itself. The liberated Jain soul rests forever in perfect knowledge, but alone, sealed in its own completeness. Scripture's goal is not isolation but communion: *"Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people"* **(Revelation 21:3)**.

And the fourth is rebirth, which Scripture rules out **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** Jains are often gentle, disciplined, generous, and successful in business, and they deserve respect for the seriousness of their moral life. I would begin with the weight. I would agree that sin is real and clings to us, and ask honestly: *Is the weight getting lighter?* Most sincere Jains will admit that liberation lies many lives away. Then I would tell them about the One who did not shed the weight by austerity but carried it for us: *"the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all"* **(Isaiah 53:6)**. He was also the most nonviolent man who ever lived, who would not resist even those who killed Him. And He offers the weightlessness the Jain soul longs for, not at the end of countless lives, but now: *"For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light"* **(Matthew 11:30)**.

The great insight is that moral cause and effect is real. What we do does not simply disappear. It clings, it accumulates, and it shapes what comes next. That is the doctrine of karma, and in its Jain form it is almost exactly my premise that moral disorder is conserved: it does not fade, and later good cannot cancel earlier wrong.

The great problem is that, once that insight is taken seriously, there is no way out from inside. If every action adds to the load, and even good deeds bind, then a soul could cycle through millions of lives and never balance the account. These traditions have answered the problem in different ways: by dissolving the self that carries the debt, by extinguishing desire, by recognizing the light at death, by disciplined austerity, or by trusting a Buddha's vow. The deepest of them, Pure Land and the bhakti tradition, have concluded that only grace can save. They are right. What they lack is the event that makes grace just: a payment made from outside the cycle by the One who owed nothing.

That event happened once, in history. *"For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God"* **(1 Peter 3:18)**. The wheel of samsara is broken not by our escape from it, but by God's entry into it.

These traditions say surprisingly little about the soul's destiny and a great deal about our duties to the dead.

# Part 5: The Religions of East Asia

The religions of China and Japan approach death differently from everything we have seen so far. They say surprisingly little about where the soul goes, and a great deal about what the living owe the dead. Their center of gravity is the family: ancestors honored at home altars, graves tended every spring, food set out for those who have gone before.

They also overlap freely. Many Japanese families keep both a Shinto shelf and a Buddhist altar in the same room. Many Chinese households blend Confucian duty, Taoist ritual, and Buddhist teaching without any sense of contradiction. A person may belong to several of these traditions at once, each handling the part of life it handles best. Tenrikyo and Cao Dai, two newer faiths, grew out of this same blending.

Scripture has much to say to a culture centered on honoring parents. The fifth commandment is *"the first commandment with promise"* **(Ephesians 6:2)**. The question for each of these traditions is whether honoring the dead leads the living to the God who gives life to the dead.

## Shinto

**What it teaches.** Shinto is Japan's native religion. It centers on the *kami*, the sacred powers present in mountains, rivers, trees, great people, and ancestors, and on purity in this life. Shinto says very little about the afterlife, and the silence is deliberate.

Its oldest myths, in the *Kojiki*, describe *Yomi*, a dark land of the dead. When the goddess Izanami died, her husband Izanagi followed her there to bring her back. He found her body decaying, fled in horror, and on his return washed himself in a river to remove the defilement. From that story comes Shinto's central concern: death brings *kegare*, impurity, and the sacred must be kept clean of it. Shrines keep death at a distance, and mourners may avoid visiting shrines for a time after a loss.

The dead are not forgotten. They are honored at home, and over generations they are believed to become protective kami of the family. But because Shinto keeps death at arm's length, most Japanese funerals are Buddhist. A common saying sums up the arrangement: born Shinto, married Christian, die Buddhist.

**What it sees truly.** Shinto sees that purity matters, and that we cannot approach what is holy while defiled. *"Who shall ascend into the hill of the LORD? or who shall stand in his holy place? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart"* **(Psalm 24:3–4)**. The connection between death and defilement is also biblical. Under the Law of Moses, anyone who touched a dead body was unclean for seven days and had to be purified with water **(Numbers 19:11–12)**. Shinto and the Law agree that death is not natural to the holy. It is an intrusion.

Shinto also sees the sacredness of creation, as the Psalmist did: *"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork"* **(Psalm 19:1)**. And its reverence for ancestors honors the commandment to honor our fathers and mothers **(Exodus 20:12)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that the kami take the place of the God who made them. The beauty of the mountain points beyond itself to its Maker. Paul described exactly this error: people who *"worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator, who is blessed for ever"* **(Romans 1:25)**.

The second is that ritual purification cleans only the outside. Jesus taught that defilement comes from within: *"There is nothing from without a man, that entering into him can defile him: but the things which come out of him, those are they that defile the man"* **(Mark 7:15)**. Even the Law's purifications, Hebrews explains, sanctified only *"to the purifying of the flesh"*. Only Christ's blood can *"purge your conscience from dead works to serve the living God"* **(Hebrews 9:13–14)**.

The third is the silence about death. Shinto offers the dead an honored place in family memory and a gradual transformation into guardian spirits, but no account of judgment, forgiveness, or resurrection. The living are left without a hope to hold.

**How to witness.** Christians are about one percent of Japan's population, and many Japanese see Christianity as a foreign religion that would require them to abandon their families. A Christian must show that honoring parents is part of following Christ, not opposed to it.

The story of Izanagi offers a powerful bridge. Izanagi went down into the land of the dead to bring back the one he loved, and he could not. He fled from death's corruption and had to wash himself clean. Jesus went down into death and came back victorious: *"I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death"* **(Revelation 1:18)**. Death did not defile Him; He overcame it. And He can do what Izanagi could not. He can raise those we love, and wash us clean, not on the outside, but within.

## Taoism

**What it teaches.** Taoism takes its name from the *Tao*, the Way, the nameless source and order of all things, described in the *Tao Te Ching* attributed to Laozi. Its opening line warns that the Tao that can be spoken of is not the eternal Tao. The wise person lives in harmony with the Way: humble, yielding like water, free of grasping.

In Taoist and wider Chinese belief, a person has more than one soul. Two matter most at death. The *hun*, the light, spiritual soul, rises after death. The *po*, the heavier soul, stays with the body and sinks into the earth. The po needs care from the living; proper burial and offerings keep it at peace, while a neglected po may become restless.

Taoism holds two very different attitudes toward death. One strand sought to escape death altogether, pursuing the state of the *immortals* through meditation, breathing practices, diet, and alchemy. Several Chinese emperors died from drinking elixirs of mercury and other substances meant to make them immortal. The other strand accepts death as part of the natural flow of change. The book of *Zhuangzi* tells how, after his wife died, a friend found Zhuangzi drumming on a basin and singing. He explained that he had grieved, but then saw that she had simply returned to nature's cycle, like the turning of the seasons.

**What it sees truly.** Taoism sees that there is a Way beneath all things, an order and source that holds the world together. Scripture says the same of Christ: *"he is before all things, and by him all things consist"* **(Colossians 1:17)**. It sees that humility and gentleness are stronger than force, as Jesus taught: *"Blessed are the meek"* **(Matthew 5:5)**. And the Taoist search for immortality reflects the longing God placed in every heart: *"he hath set the world in their heart"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:11)**.

The Chinese picture of two souls, one rising and one staying with the body, also touches a biblical truth. At death, *"the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it"* **(Ecclesiastes 12:7)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that the Tao is impersonal. It is a way, not a Person; it does not know us, love us, or forgive us. My premise holds that the source of all things is not merely a principle of order, but a living God who experiences every life from within.

The second is the acceptance of death as natural. Zhuangzi's peace is admirable, but Scripture does not treat death as part of the harmony of things. It is an intruder that came through sin **(Romans 5:12)**, and *"The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death"* **(1 Corinthians 15:26)**. We do not make peace with death; Christ defeats it.

The third is the quest for immortality by technique. The emperors who drank mercury are a sad picture of human effort to escape death. Eternal life is not something we can manufacture; it is a gift: *"the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord"* **(Romans 6:23)**.

**How to witness.** There is no better bridge to a Taoist friend than the Chinese Bible itself. In the standard Chinese translation, the Gospel of John begins: *In the beginning was the Tao, and the Tao was with God, and the Tao was God.* The translators chose the word *Tao* for the Greek *Logos*, the Word **(John 1:1)**. I would show a Taoist friend that verse and then read on: *"And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us"* **(John 1:14)**. The nameless Way has a name. He walked the earth, He wept at a grave, He died and rose. And He said, *"I am the way"* **(John 14:6)**: the very Tao the sages sought, now come to find us.

## Confucianism

**What it teaches.** Confucianism rests on the teachings of Confucius (551–479 BC), who taught ethics, family duty, and social harmony. It is less a religion of the afterlife than a way of life. When a disciple asked him about serving the spirits of the dead, Confucius replied, in substance, that if we are not yet able to serve the living, we cannot serve the dead. When the disciple pressed him about death, he answered that if we do not yet understand life, we cannot understand death **(Analects 11:12)**.

What Confucius cared about was *filial piety*, the respect and care children owe their parents. That duty does not end at the funeral. Traditional mourning for a parent lasted about three years. The Confucian tradition, which refused to describe the afterlife, produced some of the most detailed rituals for the dead anywhere. In Korea, at the ancestral rite called *jesa*, food for the ancestors is laid out on a table in a fixed order on holidays and death anniversaries. No one needs to know where the ancestors are to know exactly where the rice goes.

**What it sees truly.** Confucius saw that righteousness begins at home, in the way we treat our parents, and Scripture agrees. *"Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee"* **(Exodus 20:12)**. Paul wrote that a man who will not provide for his own family *"hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel"* **(1 Timothy 5:8)**. Confucius's teaching that we should not do to others what we would not want done to ourselves **(Analects 15:24)** is close to Jesus' golden rule **(Matthew 7:12)**.

His reticence about the afterlife also has a kind of wisdom. He refused to speculate about what he did not know. That is better than inventing detailed maps, and it keeps the focus on how we live now, which matters to God because He experiences our lives from within.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that the question Confucius set aside does not go away. Every person will die, and every grieving family wants to know where their loved one has gone. Confucius was honest that he did not know. But silence is not an answer.

The second is that Confucian virtue, however admirable, cannot make a person righteous before God. No son has honored his parents perfectly, and no ruler has governed with perfect benevolence. *"For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God"* **(Romans 3:23)**.

The third concerns the rites. Honoring the memory of parents is good. But when the rites involve offering food to the spirits and bowing before them as if they could receive worship or bless the living, they cross from honor into worship that belongs to God alone **(Exodus 20:3–5)**.

**How to witness.** Confucius's own words offer the bridge. He said that if we do not understand life, we cannot understand death. Jesus is the one person who understood both, because He came from God and passed through death Himself. He said, *"I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live"* **(John 11:25)**. Where Confucius was honestly silent, Jesus spoke with authority, and He proved His word by rising.

I would also show a Confucian friend that Jesus was the most filial son who ever lived. He obeyed His Father perfectly, even to death **(Philippians 2:8)**, and while dying on the cross He made sure His mother would be cared for **(John 19:26–27)**. Korean Christians have shown one way to honor ancestors faithfully: many hold memorial services on the anniversary of a parent's death, gathering the family to remember, give thanks, and pray to God, without offering food or bowing to the spirits. Filial piety does not have to be abandoned to follow Christ. In Him it finds its fulfillment.

## Chinese Folk Religion

**What it teaches.** Chinese folk religion is the practical, everyday faith of hundreds of millions of people in China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia, and the Chinese diaspora. It blends Taoist, Buddhist, and Confucian beliefs with local traditions into a single practical faith, with no founder or creed.

After death, the soul is judged in an underworld organized like the imperial government, with ten courts presided over by ten kings. Each court examines particular sins and assigns punishments. When the soul has passed through, it drinks a broth of forgetting and is reborn.

Dying does not end a person's needs. The dead still want money, food, clothing, and shelter, so families burn paper offerings to send them over: paper money printed with enormous denominations, called *hell bank notes*, along with paper houses, cars, and these days even paper phones. At *Qingming*, the tomb-sweeping festival each spring, families visit, clean, and tend their ancestors' graves.

A spirit with no descendants has no one to feed it. Such spirits become *hungry ghosts*, believed to roam during the seventh lunar month, Ghost Month. So families feed not only their own dead but set out food and burn offerings for strangers' spirits as well. Travelers in that month learn not to step on or kick the offerings left by the roadside.

**What it sees truly.** Chinese folk religion sees that there will be a judgment, and that it will be thorough and exact. Every wrong will be examined. Scripture agrees: *"For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil"* **(Ecclesiastes 12:14)**. This fits my premise that the moral law is exact, that wrongs are not erased by time but must be answered.

It also sees our duty to the family, which the Bible commands. And its custom of feeding the spirits of strangers who have no family left reflects a true and beautiful instinct: that no one should be forgotten. God Himself is *"A father of the fatherless, and a judge of the widows"* who *"setteth the solitary in families"* **(Psalm 68:5–6)**. Pure religion, James wrote, is *"To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction"* **(James 1:27)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that the afterlife becomes a marketplace. If the dead need money, and the courts of judgment can be eased with offerings, then salvation is something that can be bought, at least in part. Scripture says the opposite: *"Ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold"* **(1 Peter 1:18)**. And Paul reminds us that *"we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out"* **(1 Timothy 6:7)**.

The second is fear. Much of Chinese folk practice is driven by the fear of restless or angry spirits, and by the burden of keeping them satisfied. Jesus came to *"deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage"* **(Hebrews 2:15)**.

The third is the attempt to sustain relationships with the dead through offerings and communication, which Scripture forbids **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**. And the fourth is rebirth, which Scripture rules out **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** Christians in Chinese communities have long faced the charge that following Jesus means abandoning one's ancestors. I would show that this is not so. Christians can and should keep Qingming, visiting graves, cleaning them, remembering, and giving thanks, without burning offerings to the spirits.

The hungry ghosts offer a moving bridge. In Chinese belief, the most pitiable fate is to die with no descendants to remember you. Through Isaiah, God made a promise to those who had no children: *"Even unto them will I give in mine house and within my walls a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters: I will give them an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off"* **(Isaiah 56:5)**. In Christ, no one is a hungry ghost. Everyone who believes is adopted into God's own family **(Galatians 4:5)**, with a place at His table that no one can take away. And Jesus said, *"I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger"* **(John 6:35)**.

## Tenrikyo

**What it teaches.** Tenrikyo is a Japanese religion founded in 1838 by Nakayama Miki, whom followers call *Oyasama*, "the Parent." It teaches that there is one God, *God the Parent*, who created human beings in order to see them live the *Joyous Life* and to share in their joy.

Tenrikyo teaches that the human body is *a thing lent, a thing borrowed* from God the Parent. The only thing truly our own is the mind. Death, therefore, is not an ending. Tenrikyo calls it *denaoshi*, setting out anew: the soul returns the borrowed body, and later God lends it a new one. Illness and misfortune are understood as signs that God is urging a person to sweep away the *dust* from his mind. Tenrikyo names eight such dusts, among them miserliness, covetousness, hatred, self-love, anger, greed, and arrogance.

Followers believe that Oyasama, who died in 1887, is *ever-living* and still at work. At her sanctuary in Tenri, Japan, three meals a day are prepared and served to her, and a bath is drawn for her each evening.

**What it sees truly.** Tenrikyo sees God as a loving Parent who made us for joy. Jesus taught us to pray, *"Our Father which art in heaven"* **(Matthew 6:9)**, and said, *"These things have I spoken unto you, that my joy might remain in you, and that your joy might be full"* **(John 15:11)**.

Its teaching that the body is borrowed is close to Paul's: *"know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?* *For ye are bought with a price"* **(1 Corinthians 6:19–20)**. Its list of the mind's dusts reads much like Jesus' list of the things that come out of the heart: *"evil thoughts… covetousness, wickedness, deceit… an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, foolishness"* **(Mark 7:21–22)**.

Tenrikyo also fits my premise in an unusual way. It teaches that God made us to see us live joyfully and to share our joy. I believe God lives our lives from within and feels them, both our joy and our pain. That is why our choices matter to Him.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is rebirth. Scripture gives each person one life **(Hebrews 9:27)**, and the body we return is not exchanged for another but raised and made new **(1 Corinthians 15:42–44)**.

The second is the link between illness and the mind's dust. Sometimes sin does bring suffering, but Jesus refused to make that a rule. Asked whether a man born blind had sinned or his parents, He answered, *"Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him"* **(John 9:3)**. Teaching the sick that their illness reveals the dust in their minds can add guilt to suffering.

The third is the method. Tenrikyo asks each person to sweep his own mind. But the dust always returns, and sweeping does not undo what the dust has already caused. Only God can make a heart clean: *"If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness"* **(1 John 1:9)**.

The fourth is the claim that the foundress is ever-living. Her followers serve her meals, but she does not eat them. Jesus, after He rose, ate before His disciples to show them that He was truly alive **(Luke 24:41–43)**, and He *"ever liveth to make intercession"* for us **(Hebrews 7:25)**.

**How to witness.** Tenrikyo followers are known for joyful, voluntary service, which they call *hinokishin*. A Christian can honor that and share it. I would begin with God the Parent, and tell a Tenrikyo friend Jesus' parable of the father who ran to meet his returning son **(Luke 15:20)**. Then I would ask: *Has sweeping removed the dust for good?* Most sincere followers will say the work is never finished. That opens the way to the One who does not ask us to sweep alone, but washes us Himself. And I would tell him about the one Founder who truly is ever-living, who rose from the dead, ate with His friends, and is still at work today.

## Cao Dai

**What it teaches.** Cao Dai is a Vietnamese religion formally founded in 1926, with its Holy See in the city of Tay Ninh. Its name means "High Tower" or "Supreme Palace," a title for God, whose symbol is the *Divine Eye*, an eye within a triangle. Cao Dai teaches that God revealed Himself in two earlier ages through the founders of the world's religions, including Moses, the Buddha, Laozi, Confucius, and Jesus. Now, in a third age of grace, He speaks directly to unite them all.

Cao Dai's early teachings came through séances, as messages received from the spirit world. Some of the spirits it honors carry famous names. A painting at the Holy See shows the French novelist Victor Hugo, the Chinese revolutionary Sun Yat-sen, and the sixteenth-century Vietnamese poet Nguyen Binh Khiem writing a covenant between God and humanity, with the words *Love and Justice*. To followers, these men's deaths did not end their work.

Like Buddhism, Cao Dai teaches karma and rebirth. The goal is to escape the cycle and return to God. Followers live by prayer, vegetarian days, and ethical conduct.

**What it sees truly.** Cao Dai believes in one Supreme God who sees all things. Scripture agrees: *"The eyes of the LORD are in every place, beholding the evil and the good"* **(Proverbs 15:3)**. That image of God's all-seeing eye fits my premise especially well. God does not only watch our lives from outside; He sees them from within.

Cao Dai's covenant motto, love and justice, names two attributes that Scripture holds together in God: *"Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne: mercy and truth shall go before thy face"* **(Psalm 89:14)**. And its longing to unite all peoples under one God reflects God's own purpose, *"That in the dispensation of the fulness of times he might gather together in one all things in Christ"* **(Ephesians 1:10)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is the source. Cao Dai's scriptures were received through mediums from spirits of the dead. Scripture forbids this practice **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)** and asks a pointed question: *"should not a people seek unto their God? for the living to the dead?"* **(Isaiah 8:19)**. Whatever spoke through those séances, it was not the God who forbade them.

The second is the blending of all religions into one. As I noted with the Baha'i faith, the founders Cao Dai honors contradict one another on the most basic questions, about God, the self, death, and salvation. They cannot all be speaking for the same God.

The third is that Jesus is placed among many teachers and saints rather than above them all. Scripture gives Him *"a name which is above every name"* **(Philippians 2:9)**. And the fourth is rebirth, which Scripture rules out **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** Cao Dai's own motto offers the bridge. *Love and justice* are the two things every religion struggles to hold together. If God is just, how can He forgive? If He forgives, how is He just? I would ask a Cao Dai friend where love and justice finally meet. The answer is the Cross, where God's justice was satisfied and His love was poured out in the same act. The Psalmist foresaw it: *"Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other"* **(Psalm 85:10)**.

Victor Hugo himself offers another bridge, through his own great novel. At the beginning of *Les Misérables*, the bishop forgives the thief Jean Valjean, who has stolen his silver, and gives him the candlesticks as well. That unearned mercy remakes Valjean's whole life. Hugo understood grace. I would tell a Cao Dai friend that the story Hugo wrote points to a greater one: the God who, when we had stolen from Him, gave Himself.

What stands out in these traditions is love for family that does not end at the grave. Graves are swept, rice is set out, anniversaries are kept. Even strangers' spirits are fed. That love is good, and Christianity does not ask anyone to give it up.

# Part 6: Modern Movements

The four movements in this essay are young. Spiritualism began in 1848, Rastafari in the 1930s, Wicca and Scientology in the 1950s. Yet their influence reaches far beyond their membership. The Ouija board at a sleepover, the candle lit on Halloween for a grandmother, the conviction that we have all lived before, the reggae song about Zion: each of these has roots in one of these movements.

All four arose in cultures shaped by Christianity, and all four borrowed from it while rejecting its center. Each offers what it presents as a fuller or freer answer than the Church gave: contact with the dead, a gentler cycle of nature, scientific mastery of the spirit, or a life that does not end. For each, the question is whether its answer is better than the one it replaced.

## Spiritualism

**What it teaches.** Spiritualism began in 1848 in Hydesville, New York, when the young sisters Kate and Maggie Fox reported mysterious rapping sounds in their family home and said the knocks came from a spirit who answered their questions. Word spread, and a movement grew around the belief that the dead live on in a spirit world and can communicate with the living through *mediums*. Spiritualist churches still hold services in which a medium passes on messages from the other side.

In Spiritualism, death is neither an ending nor a verdict. There is no hell. The dead continue to progress upward through higher and higher spheres, and the door to progress is open to every soul. The Ouija board grew out of this movement as a parlor game in the 1890s.

The movement's most famous champion was Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes. He declared himself a Spiritualist in 1916. Two years later his son Kingsley died of pneumonia, weakened by wounds from the Battle of the Somme. Doyle spent his last years traveling the world to promote the faith. The author of fiction's most rigorous skeptic became the best-known believer of his age.

**What it sees truly.** Spiritualism sees that the person survives death. That is true, and it is the faith of the Bible: God *"is not the God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him"* **(Luke 20:38)**. Spiritualism also teaches personal responsibility and holds that our actions have consequences after death, which Scripture affirms **(Galatians 6:7)**.

Above all, Spiritualism speaks to the grief of the bereaved, and that grief is real and honorable. Doyle turned to the séance after losing his son. Many have done the same. Their longing to know that their loved ones still exist, and are well, is a longing God placed in the human heart.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first and decisive problem is that Scripture forbids exactly what Spiritualism practices. *"There shall not be found among you… a consulter with familiar spirits, or a wizard, or a necromancer. For all that do these things are an abomination unto the LORD"* **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**. *"Regard not them that have familiar spirits, neither seek after wizards, to be defiled by them"* **(Leviticus 19:31)**. King Saul consulted a medium at Endor, and Scripture records that he died partly *"for asking counsel of one that had a familiar spirit"* **(1 Chronicles 10:13)**. This prohibition stands whatever one believes about the open questions of the soul's state after death.

The second is deception. The movement's own founders illustrate it. In 1888, Maggie Fox publicly confessed that the rappings had been a trick, made by cracking the joints of her toes. She later recanted the confession. The escape artist Harry Houdini, once Doyle's friend, spent years exposing fraudulent mediums. Where the messages are not fraud, Scripture warns of a darker possibility: *"Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light"* **(2 Corinthians 11:14)**. A voice that claims to be a loved one and tells the living that there is no judgment is not to be trusted.

The third is the promise of endless upward progress for every soul with no judgment and no need of a Savior. Scripture's picture is different: *"it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

Jesus Himself spoke to this question. In His parable, the rich man in torment begs Abraham to send Lazarus back from the dead to warn his brothers. Abraham refuses: *"If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead"* **(Luke 16:31)**. God has not chosen to speak to us through messages from the dead. He has spoken through His Word, and through One who truly did rise.

**How to witness.** Most people who visit a medium are grieving. A Christian should come to them with compassion first, not condemnation. I would listen to the story of the person they lost, and then offer them something better than a message relayed by a stranger. There is one person who came back from death, not as a voice in a darkened room, but in a body that ate and could be touched, seen by hundreds of witnesses **(1 Corinthians 15:6)**. He came back to tell us what lies beyond, and to promise that those who die in Him will be raised. Paul wrote to grieving believers, *"I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope"* **(1 Thessalonians 4:13)**. That hope does not require a medium. It requires only trust in the One who has the keys of death.

## Wicca

**What it teaches.** Wicca is a modern pagan religion that took shape in England in the mid-twentieth century, largely through the work of Gerald Gardner, who published *Witchcraft Today* in 1954. It honors a Goddess and a God, often pictured as the Moon and the Horned God of the forest, and it celebrates the cycles of nature through eight seasonal festivals called the *Wheel of the Year*. Its central ethical principle, the *Wiccan Rede*, is usually summarized as: if it harms none, do what you will. Many Wiccans practice magic, which they understand as directing natural energies through ritual and will.

Many Wiccans believe that after death the soul goes to the *Summerland*, a peaceful place where it rests, reflects on the life just lived, and meets loved ones again before being reborn. The name was borrowed from Spiritualism. There is no heaven or hell, and no one is punished forever. Life, death, and rebirth turn like the seasons.

For many Wiccans, *Samhain*, on October 31, is the most sacred night of the year. It marks the turning of the year, when the boundary between the worlds is believed to be thinnest and the dead are honored. Some Wiccans set out a silent meal for loved ones who have died.

**What it sees truly.** Wicca sees that creation is sacred and that its rhythms speak of something beyond themselves. Paul wrote that God's *"eternal power and Godhead"* are *"clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made"* **(Romans 1:20)**. The seasons are God's own promise: *"While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease"* **(Genesis 8:22)**. The Preacher saw the same rhythm: *"a time to be born, and a time to die"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:2)**.

The hope of rest after death and reunion with loved ones is a true hope. And the principle of harming none is a fragment of the law of love: *"Love worketh no ill to his neighbour"* **(Romans 13:10)**. The tenderness of Samhain toward the dead also reflects a love that should not be despised.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is the object of worship. Wicca honors the Goddess and God of nature, and in so doing turns the creation into the deity. Scripture names this error directly **(Romans 1:25)**. The beauty of the moon and forest is real, but it points to the One who made them.

The second is magic. Scripture places witchcraft among the practices God forbids **(Deuteronomy 18:10)** and lists it among the works of the flesh **(Galatians 5:20)**. Whatever Wiccans intend by it, the attempt to bend spiritual powers to one's own will is the opposite of the posture Scripture asks: *"Not my will, but thine, be done"* **(Luke 22:42)**.

The third is the ethic. Harming none is a good beginning but a thin law. It asks only that we avoid injury, not that we lay down our lives for others. And it offers no answer for when we have already done harm, as all of us have. Without sin, there is no need of forgiveness, and without judgment, no need of a Savior.

The fourth concerns the cycle itself. In Wicca, life, death, and rebirth turn forever like the seasons. In Scripture, history is not a wheel but a story with a beginning, a turning point, and an end. The cycle of seasons is not the final word but a picture of something that happens once: the grain that dies and rises **(John 12:24)**, the resurrection that leads into a new creation where there will be *"no more death"* **(Revelation 21:4)**. And rebirth itself is ruled out by Scripture **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** Many Wiccans came to their faith after leaving a church where they felt that nature was despised, the body was shamed, or women were diminished. A Christian should listen to that story before answering it. Then I would show a Wiccan friend that the God of the Bible made the moon and the forest and called them good **(Genesis 1:31)**, and that He describes His own love in tender images: *"As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you"* **(Isaiah 66:13)**.

The Wheel of the Year itself is a bridge. Every spring, the earth appears to die and rise again. Christians celebrate the event that spring was always pointing toward: on Easter morning, a dead Man walked out of His tomb. Jesus used the image Himself: *"Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit"* **(John 12:24)**. The seasons are not the god. They are the parable.

## Scientology

**What it teaches.** Scientology was founded by the science-fiction writer L. Ron Hubbard, who published *Dianetics* in 1950 and established the Church of Scientology a few years later. It teaches that you are not a body that has a soul. You are a *thetan*, an immortal spiritual being, and the body is something you have. The thetan has already lived countless lives before this one, and when the body dies, the thetan leaves it behind and later takes up a new one.

Over those many lives, the thetan has accumulated painful mental images called *engrams*, stored in what Hubbard called the reactive mind. Through *auditing*, a form of spiritual counseling often using an electrical device called an E-meter, members work to clear these traumas. The aim is to rise through ascending levels, called *the Bridge to Total Freedom*, first to the state of *Clear* and then to the levels of *Operating Thetan*, with ever greater spiritual ability and freedom. These courses are expensive, and advancing through them commonly costs tens of thousands of dollars or more.

The Sea Org, Scientology's most dedicated order, asks its members to sign a contract for one billion years of service, pledging to return and serve lifetime after lifetime.

**What it sees truly.** Scientology sees that a person is more than his body, and that the spirit is primary. Jesus said, *"fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul"* **(Matthew 10:28)**, and Paul wrote that *"though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day"* **(2 Corinthians 4:16)**.

Scientology also sees that past wounds shape the present, and that people need healing from them. God promises exactly that: *"He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds"* **(Psalm 147:3)**. And its longing for freedom is a true longing. Jesus came *"to preach deliverance to the captives… to set at liberty them that are bruised"* **(Luke 4:18)**. Even the billion-year contract reflects a true instinct: a life of service to what one believes is meant to last forever. Scripture says that in the age to come *"his servants shall serve him"* **(Revelation 22:3)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that Scientology has no clear place for a personal God. Its teaching speaks of an infinite spiritual dimension, but the center of attention is the thetan himself and his recovery of his own abilities. The thetan is presented as essentially divine, a being who needs to remember his power rather than one who needs to be forgiven. My premise points in the opposite direction. We are not self-existent beings; we live within God, and our life is His gift.

The second is that Scientology treats our deepest problem as trauma rather than guilt. Engrams are wounds done to us. But our deepest problem is the wrong we have done, which no amount of clearing can undo. Auditing may relieve memories, but it cannot pay a debt.

The third is the price. In Scientology, spiritual progress is purchased course by course. Scripture says salvation cannot be bought. When Simon the sorcerer tried to buy the power of the Holy Spirit, Peter answered, *"Thy money perish with thee, because thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money"* **(Acts 8:20)**. God's invitation is the opposite: *"Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price"* **(Isaiah 55:1)**.

The fourth is rebirth, which Scripture rules out **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

I should add that many former members, as well as journalists and some courts, have described serious abuses within the organization, including high-pressure fundraising and the practice of requiring members to cut off contact with critics, even family. The church disputes these accounts, and former members are themselves interested witnesses. But the accounts are numerous and consistent enough to be taken seriously.

**How to witness.** Many people inside Scientology joined out of a sincere desire to be free of pain and to help others. Many who leave carry deep wounds, financial loss, and broken family ties. A Christian should come to both with compassion.

The word Scientology uses most, *freedom*, is the bridge. I would ask a Scientologist: *How much has your freedom cost, and how far up the Bridge are you?* Then I would tell him about the freedom that cannot be bought because it has already been paid for: *"If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed"* **(John 8:36)**. That freedom does not depend on how many courses a person can afford. It is given to anyone who asks, and it begins not with the recovery of our own power, but with the forgiveness of our sins.

## Rastafari

**What it teaches.** Rastafari began in Jamaica in the 1930s, after Ras Tafari Makonnen was crowned Emperor Haile Selassie I of Ethiopia in 1930. Among his many titles was *Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah*. Many poor Black Jamaicans, inspired by Marcus Garvey's call to look to Africa, saw in his coronation the fulfillment of biblical prophecy. Most Rastafari regard Haile Selassie as *Jah*, God incarnate, or as the returned Messiah.

Rastafari reads the Bible, especially the King James Version, through the experience of slavery and exile. *Babylon* names the oppressive system of the West that enslaved Africans and still holds them captive. *Zion* is the promised homeland, identified with Ethiopia and Africa. It is a real place on earth, not somewhere above the clouds.

Rastafari is not built around life after death but around life that does not end. Its idea of *everliving* holds that the righteous can live on in the body itself. Rastafari emphasize *livity*, a natural, wholesome way of life, caring for the body and spirit together, often with a natural diet. Many avoid dwelling on death at all, and some avoid funerals. When Bob Marley died in 1981 without a will, many linked the omission to this outlook, and his estate was tangled in legal battles for years.

**What it sees truly.** Rastafari takes the Bible seriously and reads it as a book about real oppression and real deliverance, which it is. God said to Moses, *"I have surely seen the affliction of my people… and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters"* **(Exodus 3:7)**. The exiles' lament, *"By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion"* **(Psalm 137:1)**, became a Rastafari anthem for good reason.

Rastafari also sees that God's final kingdom is not a cloudy escape from the earth but a restored creation with real ground underfoot. That is the hope of the new earth **(Revelation 21:1)**. Its care for the body honors the temple God made **(1 Corinthians 6:19)**. And its longing for a life that does not end is the longing God promises to fulfill: *"whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die"* **(John 11:26)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is the deity of Haile Selassie. The emperor himself did not claim to be divine. He was a devout member of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church who worshiped Jesus Christ, and he reportedly told Rastafari visitors that he was a man. He died in 1975. Many Rastafari believe he did not truly die or that he lives on in another way, but his death is a matter of record.

The second concerns the meaning of everliving. Jesus' promise that believers *"shall never die"* does not mean the body will escape death; it means that death will not hold them. *"It is appointed unto men once to die"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**, but the believer will be raised. Avoiding the thought of death does not prevent it, and refusing to prepare for it can leave a family in confusion and grief.

The third concerns Zion. Rastafari is right that the promised home is a real place, but the Zion of Scripture is not finally a nation on the present earth. It is *"the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem"* **(Hebrews 12:22)**, which will one day come down to a renewed earth **(Revelation 21:2)**.

Finally, many Rastafari use cannabis as a sacrament, which Scripture nowhere commends. Paul's counsel is to be filled not with intoxicants but with the Spirit **(Ephesians 5:18)**.

**How to witness.** Rastafari know the Bible, often better than many Christians, and they take seriously its cry for justice. I would begin there, honoring the history of suffering that gave rise to the movement and agreeing that God hears the cry of the oppressed.

The richest bridge is Haile Selassie's own title. *Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah* comes from the Bible, where it describes Jesus: *"Weep not: behold, the Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David, hath prevailed"* **(Revelation 5:5)**. In the very next verse, the Lion appears as *"a Lamb as it had been slain"* **(Revelation 5:6)**. I would point out to a Rastafari friend that the emperor himself bowed to that Lion. Haile Selassie worshiped Christ. The Lion of Judah who truly conquered did so by dying and rising, and He offers the everliving life every Rastafari longs for: not a body that never dies, but a body that death cannot keep.

# Part 7: African and Indigenous Traditions

The seven traditions in this essay come from West Africa, the Caribbean, the American plains, Australia, the mountains of Southeast Asia, Madagascar, and Indonesia. They have no common founder or scripture, and most were passed down orally for centuries. Each varies from village to village, so any short description simplifies. What they share is a conviction that the dead remain close to the living, as members of the family who still need care and still give help.

Many of the peoples who hold these traditions are now largely Christian. Most Yoruba are Christian or Muslim; most Malagasy and Toraja are Christian; many Hmong and Aboriginal Australians are Christian. For them, the question is often not whether to follow Christ, but how to follow Him faithfully within a culture shaped by these older beliefs. And Christians from outside must remember that missionaries have sometimes treated these cultures with contempt, destroying what was good along with what was false. Paul's approach at Athens, honoring what is true before naming what is missing, is especially needed here.

## Yoruba Religion

**What it teaches.** The traditional religion of the Yoruba people of Nigeria, Benin, and Togo, which also lives on in the Americas as Santería, Candomblé, and related faiths, teaches that there is one supreme God, *Olodumare*, the Creator. Olodumare is exalted and distant. Day-to-day dealings with the spiritual world go through the *orishas*, divine spirits associated with forces of nature and human life, and through the ancestors.

The Yoruba distinguish between *Aye*, the visible world, and *Orun*, the spiritual realm. People who live good lives become honored ancestors in Orun, and they can be reborn into their own families. Becoming an ancestor requires two things: a good life and a good death, which means dying at an old age, surrounded by one's children. Those who achieve both become guides and protectors for the family they left behind. At *Egungun* festivals, masked dancers in full costume embody the ancestors returning to visit the living.

The belief lives in names. *Babatunde* means "father has returned"; *Yetunde* means "mother has returned." Families give these names to children born after a grandparent has died.

**What it sees truly.** The Yoruba have always known that there is one supreme Creator above all other spiritual beings. That is the truth Paul said God had left as a witness among all peoples **(Acts 14:17)**. They also hold a deep sense of family continuity across generations, as Scripture does. When Abraham died, he *"died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people"* **(Genesis 25:8)**. The Yoruba ideal of a good death in old age, surrounded by children, is the same blessing.

The Yoruba sense that the dead remain connected to the living reflects the biblical truth that the faithful departed are *"so great a cloud of witnesses"* **(Hebrews 12:1)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that Olodumare is too distant, and the orishas and ancestors stand between God and the people. Scripture insists that there is *"one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus"* **(1 Timothy 2:5)**. And God is not distant: He lives within every life He has made.

The second is that ancestorhood must be earned by a good life and a good death. That excludes the child who dies young, the childless, and the one whose life went wrong. The Gospel opens the family of God to all of them, by grace.

The third is the attempt to commune with the ancestors through ritual and possession, which Scripture forbids **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**. And the fourth is rebirth into the family, which Scripture rules out **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** The Yoruba have a long Christian history of their own. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba man rescued from a slave ship as a boy, became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864 and translated the Bible into Yoruba. Christians among the Yoruba can be honored as the bearers of this tradition, not as foreigners.

I would begin with Olodumare. The Yoruba have always known the supreme Creator, but have felt Him to be far away. I would tell a Yoruba friend that this distant God came near: *"the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us"* **(John 1:14)**. And I would tell him about a family no good death is needed to join. Christ is *"the firstborn among many brethren"* **(Romans 8:29)**, and in Him even those who die young or childless have a place, a name, and an inheritance.

## Haitian Vodou

**What it teaches.** Haitian Vodou grew from the West African religions brought to Haiti by enslaved people, blended with Catholic elements. It teaches that there is one supreme God, *Bondye* (from the French *Bon Dieu*, Good God), who is distant from human affairs. Practitioners serve the *lwa*, spirits who act as intermediaries and who may possess worshipers during ceremonies.

Vodou teaches that the soul has two parts. The *gwo bonanj*, the big good angel, carries a person's memory and personality. The *ti bonanj*, the little good angel, holds his conscience. At death, the gwo bonanj goes to *Ginen*, the land of the ancestors, often pictured beneath the waters of a lake, a river, or the sea. A year and a day after death, a ritual called *wete mò nan dlo*, "taking the dead from the water," can call the spirit back up. It is then housed in a clay jar and becomes an ancestor spirit who guides the family.

Among the lwa are the *Gede*, the spirits of the dead, led by Baron Samedi in his black top hat. During Fèt Gede in early November, they are honored in cemeteries with dancing, purple and black clothes, and rum. They are known for their humor and for watching over children. In much of the world the dead are spoken of in hushed voices. In Vodou, they get music and laughter.

**What it sees truly.** Vodou knows that there is one supreme God above all spirits. Its distinction between the personality and the conscience reflects the biblical truth that God has written His law on every heart, so that the conscience bears witness within us **(Romans 2:15)**.

Its refusal to let death have the last word, meeting it with laughter and dance, has a strong echo in Scripture. Paul taunted death itself: *"O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?"* **(1 Corinthians 15:55)**. And its sense that the dead are not abandoned, that the family remains bound together, reflects a true love.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that Bondye is treated as too distant to approach, so that worship goes to the lwa instead. But God is not far away. Jesus invites every person to come to the Father directly through Him **(John 14:6)**, and Scripture forbids serving other spirits **(Exodus 20:3)**.

The second is the practice of possession and of calling the dead back from the waters. Scripture forbids every attempt to summon or consult the dead **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**.

The third is the fear and obligation that serving the spirits creates. The lwa must be fed, honored, and kept satisfied, or they may withdraw their protection or cause harm. Many former practitioners describe this as a heavy burden. Christ came to free us from such fear: *"ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father"* **(Romans 8:15)**.

The fourth concerns Baron Samedi as the lord of the dead. Scripture gives that place to another: Christ, who holds *"the keys of hell and of death"* **(Revelation 1:18)**.

**How to witness.** Haiti is largely Christian, and many Haitian believers, Catholic and Protestant, came out of Vodou or have family members who serve the lwa. They are the best witnesses to their own people. Christians from outside should come in humility, aware of Haiti's suffering and of the times foreigners have looked down on it.

The ritual of taking the dead from the water offers a striking bridge. In Christian baptism, a person goes down into the water as into a grave and is raised up to new life: *"we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life"* **(Romans 6:4)**. Christ does not call the dead up from the waters into a clay jar. He raises the living from the waters into His own life now, and He will raise the dead, whole, at the last day. And the laughter of Fèt Gede finds its true reason in Him. We can mock death, as Paul did, not because death is a joker, but because it has been defeated.

## Lakota Spirituality

**What it teaches.** The Lakota, one of the Sioux peoples of the northern Great Plains, honor *Wakan Tanka*, often translated as the Great Mystery or Great Spirit, the sacred power behind all things. A familiar Lakota prayer ends with the words *mitakuye oyasin*, "all my relatives," expressing the kinship of all creation.

In Lakota tradition, the spirit of the dead travels the *spirit road* to the spirit world. That road is the Milky Way. On any clear night, one can look up and see the path itself. Some accounts tell of an old woman at a fork in the road who examines each spirit and directs it onward or turns it back.

A family does not have to say goodbye at once. The *Keeping of the Soul* is one of the seven sacred rites of the Lakota. In it, a family keeps a loved one's spirit close, often with a lock of hair kept in a sacred bundle, for a period that is often a year. During that time they live carefully and honor the spirit. Then, in a ceremony, the spirit is released to continue its journey.

At the end, the grieving family holds a *giveaway*, handing out their possessions to the community, sometimes nearly everything they own. In most places people bring gifts to a grieving family. Among the Lakota, the grieving family gives, and the whole community is left stronger.

**What it sees truly.** The Lakota know that a sacred Creator stands behind all things, and that creation itself speaks of Him. *"The heavens declare the glory of God"* **(Psalm 19:1)**. Their sense of kinship with all creation reflects God's own care for every creature **(Psalm 145:9)**.

The Keeping of the Soul shows wisdom about grief. Mourning needs time, and then it needs an end. *"To every thing there is a season… **A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:1, 4)**. When King David's child died, he grieved, and then he rose, washed, and ate, saying, *"I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me"* **(2 Samuel 12:23)**.

The giveaway is one of the most Christlike customs in this entire survey. Jesus said, *"It is more blessed to give than to receive"* **(Acts 20:35)**. A grieving family that answers loss with generosity bears witness to a truth many Christians have forgotten.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first concern is the attempt to hold the spirit of the dead. Remembering a loved one and honoring his memory are good. But Scripture forbids every effort to keep, consult, or commune with the spirits of the dead **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**. Our dead are not ours to keep. They belong to God.

The second is that the spirit's journey depends on how it has lived, with no one to pay for the wrongs it carries. And the third is that Wakan Tanka remains a Mystery. The Lakota have known that the Creator is real but not that He has made Himself known in a Person who walked among us.

**How to witness.** No Christian can speak to the Lakota without first acknowledging the history. For generations, government and church-run boarding schools took Lakota children from their families and tried to strip away their language and culture, often harshly. Many Lakota rightly associate that history with the Church. Christians must come in repentance and humility, not as conquerors.

The Lakota also have Christian witnesses of their own. Nicholas Black Elk, the holy man whose visions became famous through the book *Black Elk Speaks*, was baptized as a Catholic in 1904 and spent decades as a catechist teaching the Gospel among his people. His life showed that a Lakota could follow Christ without ceasing to be Lakota.

The giveaway offers a beautiful bridge. I would tell a Lakota friend that God Himself held the greatest giveaway in history. *"For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich"* **(2 Corinthians 8:9)**. And the spirit road has a guide. Jesus said, *"I am the way"* **(John 14:6)**, and promised that He Himself would come to take His own home **(John 14:3)**.

## Aboriginal Australian Spirituality

**What it teaches.** Australia is home to hundreds of distinct Aboriginal nations, each with its own language, law, and ceremony, so there is no single rulebook. Many share a spirituality rooted in what is called in English *the Dreaming*: the time when ancestral beings moved across the land, shaping its features and establishing the law by which people live. The Dreaming is not only past; it remains present in the land itself.

In many traditions, dying is a homecoming. The spirit returns to *country*, the ancestral land to which a person belongs. In some traditions, it may later be born again. Death is not leaving home; it is going back.

Mourning has its own name. *Sorry Business* is the period of grief and ceremony after a death, and it can last for weeks. Many communities stop saying the name of someone who has died, sometimes for years, and anyone who shares that name may use a substitute. That is why Australian television programs, films, and museums often open with a warning that what follows may contain images or voices of deceased Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. The warning is not legal small print. It is respect for a spirit allowed to rest.

**What it sees truly.** Aboriginal spirituality sees that we belong to the land and that the land is a gift held in trust, not a mere possession. God told Israel, *"the land is mine; for ye are strangers and sojourners with me"* **(Leviticus 25:23)**, and the Psalmist sang, *"The earth is the LORD's, and the fulness thereof"* **(Psalm 24:1)**. It sees that our bodies return to the earth from which they came: *"for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return"* **(Genesis 3:19)**.

The picture of death as a homecoming is deeply biblical. Paul longed *"to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord"* **(2 Corinthians 5:8)**, and the patriarchs died looking for *"a better country, that is, an heavenly"* **(Hebrews 11:16)**. And Sorry Business, a whole community grieving together for weeks, fulfills Paul's command to *"weep with them that weep"* **(Romans 12:15)** better than the hurried funerals of most modern cultures.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that the spirit belongs to the land and its ancestral beings rather than to the Creator who made the land. Country is a gift, but it is not the Giver. The second is that, in the traditions that teach it, rebirth is ruled out by Scripture **(Hebrews 9:27)**. And the third is that the Dreaming has law but no Savior. It tells people how to live, but not what is to be done when the law is broken.

**How to witness.** Christians must begin with honesty about the past. For much of the twentieth century, government policies, sometimes carried out through church missions, removed Aboriginal children from their families. Those children are now called the Stolen Generations, and in 2008 the Prime Minister of Australia formally apologized to them in Parliament. Many Aboriginal Australians are also faithful Christians, and they are the best witnesses to their own people.

The longing for country offers a moving bridge. The Christian hope is not that we will leave the land behind forever, but that God will renew it. Paul wrote that *"the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God"* **(Romans 8:21)**. I would tell an Aboriginal friend that Jesus is preparing a place **(John 14:2)**, and that the homecoming He promises includes a restored creation, a new earth **(Revelation 21:1)**. The country is not lost. It will be made new.

## Hmong Traditional Religion

**What it teaches.** The Hmong are a people from the mountains of southern China, Laos, Vietnam, and Thailand. After the Vietnam War, in which many Hmong fought alongside American forces, tens of thousands resettled in the United States, especially in California, Minnesota, and Wisconsin. Traditional Hmong religion is a world of spirits, ancestors, and shamans, who heal illness by journeying in the spirit world to recover lost souls.

The Hmong teach that a person has several souls. After death, one of them must travel back to the ancestors before it can be reborn, and it cannot make that journey without one thing: the placenta, which the Hmong call a *jacket*. Traditionally, a newborn's placenta was buried under the floor of the family home.

At the funeral, a chant called *Showing the Way* guides the soul back through every place the person lived, stop by stop, retracing a whole life until it reaches the birthplace. There, the soul puts on its jacket again. The *qeej*, a bamboo reed pipe, plays to guide it home. Only once the soul is wearing that jacket can it join the ancestors.

This belief collides with modern life. Hospitals usually dispose of placentas as medical waste. For Hmong families in America, that could leave a soul without its jacket. Some U.S. hospitals, including some in California, now allow families to take the placenta home on request.

**What it sees truly.** The Hmong see that a human life is a journey with a shape, from birth to death, and that it matters where it began. The Psalmist marveled that God saw him from the beginning: *"My substance was not hid from thee, when I was made in secret…* *Thine eyes did see my substance, yet being unperfect; and in thy book all my members were written"* **(Psalm 139:15–16)**.

The chant of Showing the Way also reflects a true instinct. At death, a soul needs a guide, someone who knows the whole road. And the image of the soul needing a garment to enter the presence of the ancestors has a deep echo in Scripture, as I will show below.

The Hmong commitment to care for the dead, and the respect of families for each stage of life, are also worthy of honor.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that a soul's entire destiny depends on a physical object and on rituals performed correctly by the family. A soul whose placenta was lost, or whose funeral was done wrongly, is left stranded through no fault of its own. That is a heavy burden, and it is not how God deals with His children.

The second is the world of spirits and shamans. Scripture forbids seeking help from spirits **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**, and it teaches that Christ has triumphed over every spiritual power **(Colossians 2:15)**. Many Hmong who have become Christians describe their conversion as freedom from fear of the spirits.

The third is the return to the ancestors and rebirth. Scripture's hope is not a return to where we began, but a journey forward to God, and each person lives once **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** A substantial share of Hmong Americans are now Christians, and Hmong churches are among the best places for the Gospel to reach Hmong families. A Christian from outside should be patient, because becoming a Christian can strain relationships with family members who still keep the old rites, especially at funerals.

The jacket offers a striking bridge. In Scripture, too, the soul needs a garment to stand before God, but it is not one we bring with us from birth. It is one God gives. Isaiah rejoiced, *"he hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, he hath covered me with the robe of righteousness"* **(Isaiah 61:10)**. Paul wrote that *"as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ"* **(Galatians 3:27)**. That garment cannot be lost or thrown away by a hospital. And the One who gives it does not need a chant to show Him the way: He knows every place we have ever been, and He has gone before us. *"I go to prepare a place for you… that where I am, there ye may be also"* **(John 14:2–3)**.

## Malagasy Traditional Religion

**What it teaches.** Traditional religion on the island of Madagascar centers on the *razana*, the ancestors. They watch over the living and connect them to God, the Creator. Dying does not take a person out of the family; it moves him to a more honored seat within it. Many customs, called *fady*, are taboos believed to have been set by the ancestors, and breaking them is thought to bring misfortune.

The best-known ritual is *famadihana*, the turning of the bones, practiced mainly in the central highlands. Every several years, often every five to seven, a family opens its tomb and brings out the remains of its ancestors. Each is wrapped in a fresh silk shroud. Then the music starts, and relatives lift the bundles and dance with them. It is not a somber event but a joyful family reunion, and it costs a great deal to hold. Many families who practice it are also Christian.

In many Malagasy communities, family tombs are built of stone and more lavishly than homes. The reasoning is simple: you live in a house for a lifetime, but you stay in the tomb forever. The house is temporary; the tomb is the family's true address.

**What it sees truly.** The Malagasy see that the family is bound together across generations, and that love for the dead does not end at burial. They also honor the body of the dead, and Scripture does too. Joseph, dying in Egypt, made the Israelites swear to carry his bones to the Promised Land, and four hundred years later Moses kept that oath **(Genesis 50:25; Exodus 13:19)**. Hebrews counts it as an act of faith: *"By faith Joseph, when he died… gave commandment concerning his bones"* **(Hebrews 11:22)**.

The joy of famadihana also reflects something true. The dead are not lost forever, and their memory is cause for celebration as well as grief. And the Malagasy conviction that our true dwelling is not the house we live in now agrees with Scripture: *"For here have we no continuing city, but we seek one to come"* **(Hebrews 13:14)**.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that the ancestors are treated as mediators who can bless or curse the living, so that the family's welfare depends on keeping them honored and keeping their taboos. That breeds fear, and it places the dead in a role Scripture gives to Christ alone **(1 Timothy 2:5)**.

The second is the cost. Famadihana and the building of tombs can place heavy financial burdens on families who have little, sometimes drawing resources away from the needs of the living.

The third is that the tomb is not the family's true address. In the Christian hope, the tomb is the temporary place, and the permanent home is still to come.

**How to witness.** Madagascar is largely Christian, and Malagasy churches have long debated how to relate to famadihana. Some tolerate it as a family custom of remembrance; others discourage it as ancestor veneration. A Christian from outside should listen to Malagasy believers before taking sides.

Joseph's bones offer the richest bridge. I would tell a Malagasy friend that the Bible honors the bones of the dead too, but for a particular reason. Joseph wanted his bones carried to the Promised Land because he believed God would keep His promise, and Israel carried them there in hope. The Christian hope goes further: the bones of the dead will not only be honored and carried, but raised. And the family's true address is not the tomb at all. *"For we know that if our earthly house of this tabernacle were dissolved, we have a building of God, an house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens"* **(2 Corinthians 5:1)**. The one tomb in history that truly mattered is the one that was found empty.

## Aluk To Dolo (The Toraja)

**What it teaches.** In the highlands of Sulawesi, Indonesia, a person whose heart has stopped is not yet dead, at least not to his family. *Aluk To Dolo*, "the way of the ancestors," is the traditional religion of the Toraja people. Until the funeral, the person who has died is called *to makula*, a sick person. The body is preserved and kept at home for weeks, months, sometimes years. Family members bring food, talk to him, and greet him in the morning, as they would any relative who is unwell.

When the funeral finally comes, it is a huge communal event lasting days. Water buffalo are sacrificed, and they are believed to carry the soul to *Puya*, the land of souls. Only then does the sick person become someone who has died. A prized buffalo can cost as much as a car, and relatives travel from far away, so families need time to save and to gather. In Toraja, death is not the moment the body stops; it is the day the family is ready to let go.

**What it sees truly.** The Toraja see that death is not merely a biological event but a passage the whole community must walk through together. Their patient, unhurried grief is wiser than the rush of most modern funerals. And their care for the body as still a member of the household honors the person who lived in it.

Most striking is their conviction that a sacrifice is needed to carry the soul to the land of the dead. That is a true intuition, older than the Toraja and found throughout the world. Scripture confirms it: *"without shedding of blood is no remission"* **(Hebrews 9:22)**. Something must be given to open the way.

**Where it goes wrong.** The first problem is that no animal sacrifice can carry a soul to God. Hebrews says plainly, *"it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins"* **(Hebrews 10:4)**. The sacrifices of the Old Testament, like the buffalo of Toraja, were pointers to the one sacrifice that could.

The second is that the soul's passage depends on what the family can afford. A poor family may wait years, or fall into debt, to give a loved one the funeral the tradition requires. Funerals can also become contests of status. God's way is not for sale, and it does not depend on the wealth of one's relatives.

The third is that the family decides when death has come. But it is God who appoints the day: *"it is appointed unto men once to die"* **(Hebrews 9:27)**.

**How to witness.** Most Toraja today are Christians, many of them Protestants, though many Christian families still hold the great funerals of their ancestors. Toraja believers are the best guides to how faith and custom fit together.

The buffalo offers the clearest bridge in this entire essay. The Toraja believe a costly sacrifice must carry the soul to the land of souls. The Gospel agrees that a sacrifice is needed, and announces that it has already been made, at a cost no family could pay. John the Baptist, seeing Jesus, said, *"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world"* **(John 1:29)**. *"Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us"* **(Hebrews 9:12)**. No Toraja family needs to save for years. The price has been paid, and the way is open.

# Part 8: The Religions of the Ancient World

The last seven traditions in the video are religions of the ancient world. No one practices them today in their original form. Small modern revivals exist, especially of Norse and Celtic paganism, but they are reconstructions, not unbroken traditions. For these religions, then, the third question changes. I will not ask how to witness to their followers, but *where their ideas live on today*.

The answer is often surprising. These ancient pictures of death still shape how modern people think about it, usually without their knowing. The man who says he is a good person and has never hurt anyone is reciting an Egyptian confession. The person who says death is just a gray nothing is echoing the Greeks. The one who says we live on only in our children's memory is speaking the creed of Mesopotamia. Seeing where these ideas came from can help us answer them.

Several of these religions also touch the biblical story directly. Israel was enslaved in Egypt and carried away to Babylon. Paul preached in Greek cities to people raised on the old myths. Their gods were the gods the prophets confronted, and their questions are the questions Scripture answers.

## Ancient Egypt

**What it taught.** Ancient Egyptians believed that after death a person faced judgment in the Hall of the Two Truths. The dead person's heart was placed on a scale and weighed against the feather of *Ma'at*, the goddess of truth, justice, and cosmic order. Before the weighing, the dead made a long declaration, sometimes called the *Negative Confession*, swearing that he had not committed each of a long list of wrongs: I have not stolen, I have not killed, I have not lied, and so on, dozens of times.

If the heart balanced the feather, the person went on to the *Field of Reeds*, a perfect Egypt with endless harvests. If it was heavy with wrongdoing, it was devoured by *Ammit*, a monster part crocodile, part lion, part hippopotamus, and that was the end. Meanwhile, back in the tomb, mummification preserved the body so the soul could return to it.

There was a loophole. People were buried with a *heart scarab*, an amulet carved with a spell from the *Book of the Dead*. The spell speaks directly to the heart and begs it not to stand up as a witness against its owner. So a person swore he was innocent, and then had a quiet word with the only witness who knew the truth.

**What it saw truly.** Egypt saw that there will be a judgment, and that it will be by a standard of truth and justice outside ourselves. It saw that the heart is the place where our true character is revealed: *"for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart"* **(1 Samuel 16:7)**. And its care for the body expressed a hope that the person would return to it, a dim echo of the resurrection. Scripture records that both Jacob and Joseph were embalmed in Egypt **(Genesis 50:2, 26)**.

**Where it went wrong.** The Negative Confession asks every person to claim innocence. But no one is innocent: *"If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us"* **(1 John 1:8)**. And the heart scarab shows that the Egyptians knew it. Even the most careful of them feared that their own hearts would testify against them. Their answer was to silence the witness with a charm. That is self-deception, not salvation.

**Where it lives on.** The Negative Confession is alive and well. It is the creed of the modern person who says, "I'm a good person. I've never killed anyone or stolen anything." And the heart scarab lives on in every rationalization we use to quiet our own conscience.

The Gospel offers something far better than a charm. It does not ask us to claim innocence or to silence our hearts. It tells us that our hearts are right to accuse us, and that God has answered the accusation Himself: *"For if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things"* **(1 John 3:20)**. *"Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again"* **(Romans 8:33–34)**. The Christian does not need his heart to stay silent at the judgment. He has an Advocate who speaks for him.

## Ancient Greece

**What it taught.** In ancient Greek religion, *Hades* was both the god of the underworld and the name of the place itself, where nearly everyone went after death: good people, bad people, and ordinary people alike. It was not the Greek version of hell. Getting there took a guide and a boat. The god Hermes led souls down, and the ferryman Charon carried them across the river. Families buried the dead with a coin to pay the fare.

Once across, most souls drifted through the *Asphodel Meadows*. It was not fire or torture, but a dim gray existence, closer to fading out than to suffering. A few heroes and favorites of the gods reached *Elysium*. The worst offenders went to *Tartarus*. Everyone else drifted through the gray.

The greatest hero of all was not impressed. In Homer's *Odyssey*, Odysseus meets the spirit of Achilles in the underworld. Achilles tells him that he would rather be a hired hand for a poor farmer on earth than rule over all the dead. The most celebrated warrior in Greek legend would trade all his glory below for the lowest job above.

Later Greek philosophers, above all Plato, developed a different picture: an immortal soul, trapped in the body, which is released at death to return to a higher realm.

**What it saw truly.** The Greek picture of a shadowy realm where all the dead go is close to the Hebrew *Sheol* of the Old Testament, and the New Testament itself uses the word *Hades* for the realm of the dead **(Revelation 1:18)**. Peter even uses a form of the word *Tartarus* for the place where rebellious angels are held **(2 Peter 2:4)**. The Greeks saw that the dead do not simply cease to exist, and that some moral distinction is made among them.

Achilles also saw something true. Apart from God, death is not an improvement on life. Life is a gift, and even its humblest form is precious: *"a living dog is better than a dead lion"* **(Ecclesiastes 9:4)**.

**Where it went wrong.** The Greek afterlife had no hope in it. For most people, death meant fading into gray. Heaven, in the form of Elysium, was for heroes and divine favorites, not for ordinary people, and there was no way for the ordinary to reach it. When Paul preached the resurrection in Athens, some of the philosophers mocked him **(Acts 17:32)**. To a Greek mind, the body was something to escape, not something to be raised.

**Where it lives on.** The gray Asphodel Meadows live on in the modern belief that death is simply nothing, a fading out. Charon's coin lives on in the old custom of placing coins on the eyes of the dead. And Plato's picture of the soul escaping the body lives on, surprisingly, in much popular Christianity, which pictures heaven as a realm of disembodied spirits floating among the clouds. That picture is more Greek than biblical. The Bible's hope is the resurrection of the body and a renewed earth **(1 Corinthians 15:42–44; Revelation 21:1)**.

The Gospel answers the Greek picture point by point. We do not need Hermes to lead us down; we have a Shepherd who walks with us *"through the valley of the shadow of death"* **(Psalm 23:4)**. We do not need a coin for the ferryman; our passage has been paid, *"not with corruptible things, as silver and gold…** But with the precious blood of Christ"* **(1 Peter 1:18–19)**. And the destination is not a gray meadow, nor a paradise for heroes only, but life itself, open to everyone who believes. Achilles would rather have been a poor man's servant on earth than a king among the dead. In Christ, the least servant will reign: *"if we suffer, we shall also reign with him"* **(2 Timothy 2:12)**.

## Norse Religion

**What it taught.** In Norse religion, warriors who died in battle could be chosen by the *Valkyries* to enter *Valhalla*, Odin's great hall. Life there followed a strict routine. Every day the warriors fought; every evening their wounds healed, and they feasted on meat and mead. Half of the battle dead went instead to *Folkvangr*, the field of the goddess Freyja. Most people never saw either place. Those who died of sickness or old age went to *Hel*, a cold realm ruled by a figure of the same name.

Valhalla was not a retirement. It was training. All that fighting was preparation for *Ragnarok*, the final battle at the end of the world, and its outcome was already foretold. Odin would be killed by the wolf Fenrir. The god gathering the bravest dead was building an army for a fight he was fated to lose. Yet the old poems also say that after Ragnarok a new earth would rise green from the sea, and that Baldr, the beautiful and innocent god who had been slain, would return.

Most of what we know of Norse belief comes from Icelandic writings of the thirteenth century, set down by Christians two centuries after Iceland's conversion, so it is hard to know how much of the picture is purely pagan.

**What it saw truly.** The Norse saw that the world is a battlefield between good and evil, and that courage matters. Paul used the same image: *"Fight the good fight of faith, lay hold on eternal life"* **(1 Timothy 6:12)**. They saw that history is heading toward a final conflict, and beyond it, a world made new **(Revelation 21:1)**. And the story of Baldr, the innocent one slain and destined to return, is a remarkable echo of the Gospel.

**Where it went wrong.** The Norse afterlife rewarded the strong. The warrior who died with sword in hand feasted with Odin, while the farmer who died in bed went to the cold of Hel. Scripture turns that order upside down: *"the last shall be first, and the first last"* **(Matthew 20:16)**. And the Norse gods themselves were bound by fate. Even Odin could not escape his death. A god who cannot save himself cannot save anyone else.

**Where it lives on.** The Norse gods are still in our calendar: Wednesday is Odin's day, Thursday is Thor's, and Friday is named for Frigg or Freyja. They live on in films and novels, and in small modern revivals of Norse paganism. More deeply, they live on in an attitude: the heroic resolve to fight on even when defeat is certain, the courage that expects no victory. Many admirable people hold that view of life. Some extremist groups have also tried to claim Norse symbols, a misuse that most modern pagans reject.

The Gospel takes that heroic courage seriously and answers it with something greater. The Norse world ends with a god who goes into the last battle and loses. The Christian story centers on a God who went into the last battle and won by losing. Christ went to the cross, died, and rose, and *"having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it"* **(Colossians 2:15)**.

C. S. Lewis, who loved the Norse myths from boyhood, came to faith partly through them. His friend J. R. R. Tolkien persuaded him that the story of the dying and returning god, which had always stirred him in the myths, had in Christ actually happened. The longing the Norse poems awaken is not mistaken. It is fulfilled. Baldr was a hope; Christ is the fact.

## Ancient Mesopotamia

**What it taught.** In ancient Mesopotamia, the land of Sumer, Babylon, and Assyria, every person went at death to the underworld, a gloomy *land of no return* ruled by the goddess Ereshkigal. The old texts do not soften it. There, they say, dust is the food of the dead and clay their bread. There was no reward for a good life and no special punishment for a bad one. Kind or cruel, everyone went to the same place.

The one comfort came from the living. Descendants poured water and left food for the dead, and that care made their stay more bearable. In a Sumerian poem, the hero Gilgamesh's friend Enkidu reports what he saw in the underworld: the man with one son weeps bitterly, but the man with seven sons sits beside the gods. Comfort after death depended not on what one had done, but on how many children one had left to care for him.

Even the greatest hero could not escape. In the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the king, grief-stricken at Enkidu's death, sets out to find immortality. He obtains a plant that restores youth, but while he bathes, a serpent steals it and slips away. Gilgamesh returns home empty-handed. In one version, a wise woman advises him to give up the search, eat and drink, and enjoy the days he has.

**What it saw truly.** Mesopotamia saw death honestly. It did not pretend that death is natural, beautiful, or a mere passage. It saw it as the enemy it is **(1 Corinthians 15:26)**. Gilgamesh's search for life, and his loss of it to a serpent, is a haunting echo of Eden, where a serpent's deception cost humanity access to the tree of life **(Genesis 3:22–24)**. And the Mesopotamian sense that the dead need the care of the living reflects a true instinct: no one can secure his own life beyond death.

**Where it went wrong.** The Mesopotamian underworld was without hope or justice. The good and the wicked shared the same dust. And the only comfort was one the dead could not secure for themselves, depending entirely on descendants who might forget or fail.

**Where it lives on.** The creed of Mesopotamia is common today: that the only immortality we have is to live on in our children and in the memory of those who loved us. So is the counsel given to Gilgamesh: since death is final, eat, drink, and enjoy what you can. Paul saw exactly where that logic leads: *"If after the manner of men I have fought with beasts at Ephesus, what advantageth it me, if the dead rise not?** let us eat and drink; for to morrow we die"* **(1 Corinthians 15:32)**. If there is no resurrection, the Mesopotamians were right.

But there is a resurrection, and it was in Babylon itself that God revealed it most clearly. During Israel's exile there, Daniel received the promise: *"many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life"* **(Daniel 12:2)**. The dust that Mesopotamia saw as the food of the dead became, in God's word, the bed from which they will rise. And the plant of life that the serpent stole from Gilgamesh is restored in Christ, who gives *"to eat of the tree of life, which is in the midst of the paradise of God"* **(Revelation 2:7)**. No one needs seven sons to be comforted beyond the grave. One Son is enough.

## Celtic Religion

**What it taught.** Roman writers said that the Celts of Gaul and Britain charged into battle with no fear of death, and they believed they knew why. Julius Caesar wrote that the *druids*, the Celtic priestly class, taught that souls do not die but pass after death from one body to another, and that this belief made Celtic warriors fearless **(Caesar, *Gallic War* 6.14)**. Another Roman writer, Valerius Maximus, reported that the Gauls lent each other money to be repaid in the next life.

The Celts left almost no writings of their own, so much of what we know comes from their Roman enemies and from Irish and Welsh tales written down centuries later by Christian monks. Those tales describe the *Otherworld*. One of its best-known regions is *Tír na nÓg*, the Land of Youth, where no one ages and the feasting never ends. It could be reached through burial mounds, lakes, and mist.

**What it saw truly.** The Celts saw that the person survives death, and that this belief changes how one lives. Paul knew the same courage: *"For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain"* **(Philippians 1:21)**. The Land of Youth also reflects a true hope. In God's kingdom, *"there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying"* **(Revelation 21:4)**, and those who wait on the Lord *"shall renew their strength"* **(Isaiah 40:31)**.

Even the Gaulish custom of loans repaid in the next life saw something real: our obligations do not vanish at death. What we owe follows us.

**Where it went wrong.** The Celtic hope rested on transmigration of souls, which Scripture rules out **(Hebrews 9:27)**. The Otherworld was reached by chance, enchantment, or heroism, not by any reckoning with right and wrong, and there was no Savior. Caesar also reported that the druids practiced human sacrifice, though his account was written by an enemy and should be read with care.

**Where it lives on.** The Celtic festival of *Samhain*, marking the turn of the year when the boundary between the worlds was thought thinnest, lives on as Halloween. The Land of Youth lives on in countless fantasy stories. And the Celtic sense that some places are *thin*, where heaven and earth seem close, has been taken up in Christian spirituality, especially in Ireland and Scotland.

The Celts also left one of the most beautiful stories of how a pagan culture received the Gospel. When Patrick brought the faith to Ireland in the fifth century, he did not despise the Irish love of nature or their courage. He called them to the Creator of the nature they loved. The prayer traditionally attributed to him, known as Saint Patrick's Breastplate, asks Christ to be with the believer on every side: before him, behind him, beneath him, above him.

The Gaulish loan offers the clearest bridge. The Gauls believed debts could be carried into the next life and repaid there. Scripture says our greatest debt, the one we owe God, cannot be repaid in any life. But it has been paid already. Christ cancelled it, *"Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us… and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross"* **(Colossians 2:14)**. No one needs to carry that debt into the next world. The Land of Youth is real, and the way into it is not through a burial mound, but through the empty tomb.

## Aztec Religion

**What it taught.** The Aztecs believed that most of the dead traveled to *Mictlan*, the underworld ruled by the Lord of the Dead, a journey through nine levels that took four years. It was a hard road. The soul crossed mountains, passed through winds as sharp as knives, and finally reached a great river, where a dog helped it across.

Not everyone took that road. Warriors who died in battle and people who were sacrificed went to accompany the sun across the sky, and after four years they returned to earth as hummingbirds and butterflies. Women who died giving birth were honored like warriors and also accompanied the sun. People who died by drowning, by lightning, or from illnesses associated with water went to *Tlalocan*, the lush paradise of the rain god.

None of this depended on how good a person had been. How you died, not how you lived, chose your destination. A gentle person who died peacefully of old age still made the long walk to Mictlan, while someone struck by lightning went straight to paradise.

Aztec religion also taught that the sun and the world depended on human blood. The gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world, and humans owed them a debt that had to be repaid in hearts and blood. The Aztecs practiced human sacrifice on a vast scale.

**What it saw truly.** The Aztecs saw that death is not the end and that the soul has a journey to make. They honored mothers who died in childbirth as heroes, a recognition of the cost of bringing life into the world that our culture would do well to remember.

Most striking, they saw, in a terribly distorted form, a true principle: that life comes through sacrifice, and that the world is held together by a debt owed to the divine. Scripture agrees that *"without shedding of blood is no remission"* **(Hebrews 9:22)**. My premise holds that the moral order is exact, and that the debt wrongdoing incurs must be paid in full.

**Where it went wrong.** The Aztecs drew the most terrible possible conclusion from that truth. They believed humanity must pay the gods with human lives, endlessly, every day. Scripture teaches the opposite: the debt is real, but God Himself pays it. The Aztec gods demanded the blood of men. The God of the Bible gave His own. And because His sacrifice was perfect, it never needs to be repeated: *"this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God"* **(Hebrews 10:12)**.

The Aztec afterlife was also arbitrary. Destinations were assigned by the manner of death, with no regard for justice, mercy, or faith.

**Where it lives on.** The Aztec reverence for the dead lives on in Mexico's *Día de los Muertos*, the Day of the Dead, which blends Aztec remembrance with the Christian feasts of All Saints and All Souls. The dog that guided souls across the river lives on in the hairless *xoloitzcuintli*, Mexico's ancient breed, which many viewers met in the animated film *Coco*.

It also lives on in a more troubling way. The idea that the world requires human sacrifice to keep running has never fully disappeared. Every age finds new reasons to spend human lives for the sake of some greater cause.

The Aztecs believed that the sun needed human hearts to rise each morning. The Gospel tells of a different Sun. *"But unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings"* **(Malachi 4:2)**. He did not demand the hearts of others to rise. He gave His own heart, and rose on the third day by His own power. In 1531, only a decade after the fall of the Aztec capital, the reported appearance of the Virgin of Guadalupe to an Aztec convert named Juan Diego became a turning point in the conversion of Mexico, and to this day she is depicted standing in front of the sun. Whatever one makes of that account, the message it carried to the Aztecs was the Gospel's own: the sacrifices are over. One has been made for all.

## Inca Religion

**What it taught.** In the Inca Empire, a dead emperor still owned his palace. Being dead was no reason to move out. Inca religion treated the dead, especially rulers, as still present and still powerful. Royal mummies, called *mallqui*, were cared for as living members of the family. Servants fed them and changed their clothes. They were carried to festivals in Cusco, where people consulted them for advice.

Each dead ruler also had his *panaqa*, his family line, who ran his estates and spoke for him. So the emperor was gone, but his household, his lands, and his voice were all still at work. A new emperor received the title, but not the treasure. Many historians think this helped drive Inca expansion: each new ruler had to conquer new lands of his own, because his predecessors' wealth stayed with the dead. That respect for the dead may have helped build the largest empire in the Americas before Columbus.

The Inca also practiced sacrifice, including, on rare and solemn occasions, the sacrifice of children on high mountain peaks, some of whose preserved bodies have been found in modern times.

**What it saw truly.** The Inca saw that the dead are not simply gone, that a person's identity and household continue in some way beyond death. They honored their ancestors and took seriously the obligations between generations.

Their reverence for a dead king's household also reflects a true instinct about kingship: that a great king's reign should not end with his death. Scripture promises exactly such a King: *"of his kingdom there shall be no end"* **(Luke 1:33)**.

**Where it went wrong.** The Inca kept their dead kings in the world of the living, and the living paid the price. The dead held land the living needed, and their households demanded new conquests to support each new ruler. Consulting mummies for counsel is a form of the necromancy Scripture forbids **(Deuteronomy 18:11)**. And the sacrifice of children was a grievous wrong, the very practice the prophets condemned among Israel's neighbors **(Jeremiah 7:31)**.

**Where it lives on.** The Inca habit of keeping the dead in charge has modern echoes. English law has an old word for property controlled by the dead: *mortmain*, the dead hand. More strikingly, the twentieth century revived the Inca practice almost exactly. The bodies of Lenin, Mao Zedong, and Ho Chi Minh were embalmed and placed on public display, so that the dead founder could continue to preside over the state he founded. In parts of the Andes today, families still keep and honor the skulls of their ancestors.

The Gospel tells of a King whose reign also did not end with His death, but in a very different way. He did not keep His wealth from His heirs. He *"made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant"* **(Philippians 2:7)**, and He shares His whole inheritance with those who belong to Him. We are *"heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ"* **(Romans 8:17)**, receiving *"an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away"* **(1 Peter 1:4)**. And His body is not on display. The Inca carried their dead kings through the streets of Cusco. The disciples went to the tomb of their King and found it empty.

The Gospel answers each: a heart that need not be silenced, a body that will be raised, a battle already won, a plant of life restored, a debt cancelled, a sacrifice made once for all, and a King whose tomb is empty.

# Conclusion: Forty-Five Answers and One Who Came Back

The video ends by observing that each of these forty-five traditions began with the same question you will one day ask yourself. That is true. Having walked through all of them, I want to close with what they show us together.

**Every tradition saw something true.** God has not left any people without a witness **(Acts 14:17)**. The Egyptians knew there would be a judgment. The Hindus knew the moral order is real. The Jains knew that sin clings to us like a weight. Shinran knew that only grace can save the wicked. The Yoruba knew the supreme Creator. The Toraja knew a sacrifice is needed to open the way. The Celts knew the person survives. Scattered across forty-five traditions are fragments of the truth God *"hath set… in their heart"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:11)**.

**Every tradition faced the same three questions.** In the Introduction I asked three questions of each tradition, drawn from my premise that we live within God's own experience.

The first was *Does the person survive?* Most traditions say yes, but many lose the person along the way: dissolved into Brahman, denied by the doctrine of no self, reborn as a stranger, faded into a gray meadow, or remembered only by his descendants. Scripture says the person survives as himself, known by name, because God, who lives within every life He has made, loves each one as itself.

The second was *What becomes of the separation sin causes?* Every tradition has some answer: a scale, a bridge, a court, a fire, a cycle of rebirth. Every one of them knows, in its own way, that something is wrong between us and the source of life.

The third was *Who pays?* Here, nearly every tradition gives the same answer: you do. You pay by your merit, by your ritual, by your meditation, by your many lifetimes, by your austerity, by your family's offerings, or by the blood of animals or even of other human beings. And nearly every tradition, faced with the impossibility of that payment, finds some way to soften it: a merciful scale, another life to try again, a fire that purifies everyone in the end with no one paying the debt, or a charm to quiet the heart.

**Only one answer says that God pays.** My premise explains why this answer is not merely one option among many, but the only one that can work. Moral disorder is conserved. It does not fade, and later good cannot cancel earlier wrong. No creature can pay his own debt, because his good works always arrive too late. Only the Creator, entering His own creation as an innocent man and dying unjustly, could produce a payment with no matching debt of His own, and then offer it to everyone who will receive it.

That is what the Gospel announces. *"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life"* **(John 3:16)**.

**And only one answer comes from someone who returned.** Every other tradition in this survey is a report from this side of death, whether a philosopher's reasoning, a mystic's vision, a medium's message, or an ancient myth. Jesus is the one person in history who claimed to speak from the other side and proved it by coming back. He did not return as a spirit in a darkened room, but in a body His friends could touch, which ate with them by the lake. Paul staked everything on that fact: *"if Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain…** But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept"* **(1 Corinthians 15:14, 20)**.

Forty-five religions, forty-five answers. I have tried to treat each with respect, to honor what each has seen, and to name honestly where each falls short. I hold my own understanding of the open questions loosely, and my reasoning about the mechanism of the Cross is offered as my best understanding, not as the Gospel itself. But on the central point I take my stand. The question every religion asks has been answered, not by a guess from this side, but by the One who came back.

*"I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: And whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?"* **(John 11:25–26)**
