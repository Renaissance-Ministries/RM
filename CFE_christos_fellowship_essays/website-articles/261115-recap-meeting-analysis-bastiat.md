# 261115 - Recap - Meeting Analysis - Bastiat

# The Playground and the Sword:


# Examination of the15 November Discussion

Nov 16, 2026 · Thomas Lee Abshier, ND

After Michael left the Christos Fellowship meeting of 15 November 2026, the conversation turned from apologetics to the life of believers: how to read a skeptic, who God is in Leonard's Latter-day Saint understanding, how to heal offense in a congregation, how Christians should relate to a government that has become, in Bastiat's term, an instrument of legal plunder, and whether God will rule His people before Christ returns. Thomas, Leonard, Susan, Charlie, and Armond carried the discussion.

This essay examines those threads as arguments, with commentary on each, in the manner of the companion essay on the exchange with Michael. Its overall judgment is that the group's practical conclusions are sound and mutually reinforcing: offense must be faced rather than suppressed, God's ways are verified by doing them, and a just order must be built by changing hearts and then laws, gradually rather than abruptly. Where the discussion was weakest was in its readings of other people's motives, and those places are marked below.

## 1. Reading Michael


### What was happening in his head

Asked how the exchange went, Thomas judged that Michael is defending a 71-year-old worldview, a physical universe arising from randomness. Armond identified the structural difficulty: every rebuttal Michael offers rests on physical experience, which can be demonstrated, while Thomas had just admitted that he has never seen God. Charlie answered that if God is everything, we see Him all the time.

**Commentary.** Armond named the real obstacle. A person who accepts only what can be pointed to will not find God among the things pointed to, because God is not one more object in the world. But Charlie's reply is more than a quip. Paul argues exactly this: *"For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made"* **(Romans 1:20)**. The evidence is not hidden; it is everywhere and so easily overlooked. The task with Michael is less to show him something new than to help him read what he already marvels at.

Thomas's reading of Michael's inner state is plausible, given fifty years of friendship, but it should be held loosely. Michael said explicitly that he was learning the rules of an unfamiliar game, and a man who asks good questions cheerfully for an hour is not obviously clinging to a raft. The more charitable reading, that he is genuinely curious and genuinely unconvinced, is also the more useful one, because it keeps the conversation open.

### Aloneness and hell

Susan argued that Michael's claim to need no other is an untested abstraction; real lifelong aloneness would feel lonely. Thomas agreed and connected it to God's motive for creating. He thought Michael would nonetheless claim he could be happy as a castaway, and recalled Michael's account of five days of unrelenting, maximal pain that he eventually surrendered into. From that Thomas speculated that hell may be a place of accommodation, where people settle into the level they chose. Susan disagreed in part: even thieves and gang members have some love in this life, and since God is love, hell would have none at all.

**Commentary.** Susan's first point has the weight of Scripture behind it: *"It is not good that the man should be alone"* **(Genesis 2:18)**. Her second point is the stronger of the two answers about hell. Thomas's idea of accommodation is psychologically realistic, since people do adapt to almost anything, but it sits uneasily with Jesus' own images of outer darkness and weeping. Susan's argument explains why those images fit. If every good gift comes from God **(James 1:17)**, then a place without God is a place without any of the goods that make even a hard life bearable: friendship, beauty, a brother's loyalty. What remains is not a lower setting of the same life but the removal of everything good in it. Both participants rightly admitted that no one knows what hell is like in detail.

### A moral life without acknowledging God

Susan observed that Michael already lives largely in accordance with God's ways and is blessed for it, without recognizing the source. She included his long-term, unmarried partnership, arguing that its genuine commitment is the essence of marriage, and that formal vows and witnesses exist because so many people lack such commitment. Thomas agreed with all of it.

**Commentary.** The first part of Susan's observation is sound and theologically important. Christians call it common grace: God *"maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good"* **(Matthew 5:45)**, and a person who keeps faith, tells the truth, and treats others well will reap much of the good those practices produce, whether or not he credits God.

The second part deserves a dissent, though both Thomas and Susan affirmed it. Commitment is indeed the heart of marriage, but Scripture treats marriage as a public covenant, not merely a private bond: the prophet calls a wife *"the wife of thy covenant"* **(Malachi 2:14)**, and Genesis describes a man leaving his father and mother, a public act. Witnesses and vows are not only a crutch for the uncommitted. They make the promise accountable to the community, give the weaker party standing, protect children, and declare before God what the couple intends. Michael's faithfulness is real and admirable, and it may well be blessed; that is the common-grace point. But its being admirable does not make the public covenant unnecessary.

## 2. Leonard: the Father, the Son, and the playground

Leonard, speaking from his Latter-day Saint understanding, read John 5:19–22, where Jesus says the Son does nothing of Himself but what He sees the Father do. From it he drew a familial picture of heaven: a Father and a Son, the Father wanting the best for His children, and the Son doing what He literally saw the Father do in some earlier creation. He disagreed with Thomas's premise that God created for His own enjoyment. God already is the fullness of joy; He created so that His children could learn, grow, and find joy in His presence. Leonard believes God is still progressing, and that we contribute to His progress by glorifying His name. His summary image was a playground: God's, not ours, where children choose to share the swings or shove each other off them.

Thomas answered with the verses behind his own premise and acknowledged that the two views are at odds. He then offered a notable concession: much of his account is his own revelation, "the story I made up," and he cannot claim it reflects truth any more than he can confirm Joseph Smith's. That realm is opaque, and neither backstory seems primary to him. Leonard replied that the two corroborate each other: God made it all, and it is His.

**Commentary.** The two men were arguing past a translation issue that, once seen, brings them closer. Thomas's key text reads in the King James Version, *"for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created"* **(Revelation 4:11)**. The Greek word rendered "pleasure" is *thelema*, which means will. Most modern translations read "by your will they existed and were created." The verse therefore says creation exists because God willed it, not that God made it in order to be entertained. It is weaker support for Thomas's thesis than the King James wording suggests, and Thomas should know that before relying on it in print.

That does not leave Leonard simply right, however. Scripture also says plainly that God delights in His creatures: *"he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing"* **(Zephaniah 3:17)**. Leonard's suggestion that the idea of God's pleasure in creation entered Christianity through pagan influence does not survive that verse. The biblical balance is that God needs nothing (Paul says He is not served *"as though he needed any thing"* **(Acts 17:25)**) and yet freely rejoices in what He made. Thomas's "God experiences His creation from within" and Leonard's "God made it for our joy" are both partial statements of that balance.

Leonard's belief that God is still progressing is a different matter. It is a distinctive Latter-day Saint teaching and runs directly against texts such as *"For I am the LORD, I change not"* **(Malachi 3:6)**. Thomas was right to name it as being at odds with his view.

Thomas's humility about his own backstory is admirable, but it should not be stretched into equivalence. A speculative model offered as a model, and a claim of prophetic revelation, are different kinds of statements. Both, however, answer to the same test, which Leonard himself supplied: Scripture, read with care. Thomas's remark that the two backstories may have "downstream" consequences is also more important than he let on. Whether God is complete or still growing changes what worship is, and that is not a small effect.

### The Lawgiver among His people

Leonard traced Christ's story as the God of the Old Testament, Jehovah, come in the flesh to a people so bound to the law that they sought to kill the Lawgiver for breaking His own Sabbath. He sees the present age as the playground left to grow filthy before Christ returns to cleanse it, and Malachi's turning of the hearts of fathers and children as the preparation required first. He insisted he was not proselytizing, only stating his belief, and that he loves those who disagree, as Christ healed the man whose ear Peter cut off.

**Commentary.** The irony Leonard noted is the irony of the Gospels themselves, and his identification of Jesus with the God of Israel is orthodox: *"Before Abraham was, I am"* **(John 8:58)**. His posture toward disagreement is a model for this fellowship. The healed man, incidentally, was not a soldier but Malchus, the high priest's servant **(John 18:10)**.

### The wealth of the church

Leonard lamented that the LDS Church is among the world's wealthiest institutions, holding its assets, it says, to give to the Lord when He returns. He asked who the Church thinks owns them already.

**Commentary.** His theological point is exactly right: *"The earth is the LORD's, and the fulness thereof"* **(Psalm 24:1)**, and wealth held in reserve for God is already His. His figure was too high. Outside analysts estimate the Church's total assets at roughly $265–293 billion; a trillion dollars is a projection for around 2044. The concern about stewardship, and Leonard's broader observation that all churches tend toward accumulation, stands without the inflated number.

## 3. Why argue theology with someone who does not believe

Thomas explained why he engages Michael with his own model rather than with Scripture or Joseph Smith: it is a story he can defend on common ground. To an unbeliever, debates about doctrine sound like counting angels on a pinhead when one believes in neither angels nor pins. Yet the whole moral order rests on those angels and pins, so there is no shared language for persuasion until their existence is established. He added that the Old Testament prophecies and John the Baptist served this purpose for Jesus: without that prelude, a man claiming to be God would be taken away in a straitjacket. From his years as a yogi he recalled chanting "God and me, me and God, are one" for hours; no one came to know it by saying it. That, he said, is what spiritual talk sounds like to the man of flesh. He suspects the new birth may be the awakening of a sense of connection with God, though he said he does not know.

**Commentary.** The order Thomas describes is the right order. Scripture itself puts it first: *"he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him"* **(Hebrews 11:6)**. Arguing over doctrine with someone who does not believe in God is building the second floor before the first. Thomas's model is valuable precisely because it starts from things Michael grants, such as consciousness, order, and the experience of love, and reasons toward God from there.

His point about prophecy as evidence is sound and deserves emphasis. The claim of Jesus did not arrive unannounced; it arrived after centuries of public expectation, which is what made it testable. The yogi memory makes a complementary point: repetition does not produce knowledge. Assent to a slogan is not the same as insight, and the New Age claim that one becomes one with God by saying so is the same error discussed earlier as the Shirley MacLaine fallacy.

On the new birth, Thomas's tentativeness is honest, and Scripture supports his guess more than he claimed. Jesus says that unless a man is born again he *"cannot see the kingdom of God"* **(John 3:3)**, which is a statement about perception, and Paul says the natural man cannot receive the things of the Spirit *"because they are spiritually discerned"* **(1 Corinthians 2:14)**. A new sense of the reality and nearness of God is close to what both texts describe.

One caution is in order about Thomas's remark that the world calls Michael enlightened while he talks only of "houses and dirt." The critique is fair as a description of a ceiling: wonder at how the world works, without asking who made it. But Thomas also noted that he and Michael shared exactly this outlook in college and called their insights "enlightenments." The memory is an argument for patience. Thomas himself went beyond the ceiling; there is no reason Michael cannot.

## 4. True and false prophets

Leonard read from Joseph Smith's 1842 *Times and Seasons* editorial on trying the spirits: the world has always mistaken false prophets for true and true for false, persecuting the one and honoring the other. He added that discerning spirits is a gift of Christ, and that one need not accept Smith as a prophet to learn from the article. Thomas offered his own test: the true prophets, such as Jonah and Isaiah, called people to turn from sins they loved, and that is why they were hated.

**Commentary.** Thomas's test captures something real. A message that flatters its hearers' desires should be suspect, and one that costs the messenger his comfort has a claim to be heard. But it is not sufficient on its own, because false prophets can also be stern, and Scripture gives further tests. A prophet whose predictions fail has not spoken for God **(Deuteronomy 18:22)**. One who works signs but leads people to other gods is false regardless of the signs **(Deuteronomy 13:1–3)**. And John gives the test that matters most for the New Testament: *"Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world"* **(1 John 4:1)**, with the confession of Jesus come in the flesh as the measure. Fairness requires applying these tests evenly, including to the author of the article Leonard read, and Leonard's own remark that one need not accept Smith as a prophet to learn from him leaves room for exactly that.

## 5. Atheist or agnostic?

Susan proposed a thought experiment for Michael: if he began to lie, steal, or kill, would he still feel the same wonder at palm trees and the same happiness? She asked whether he is truly an atheist. Thomas said Michael has no conception of God's place in creation, and is in awe of the creation but not the Creator; his god is the nameless, self-originating whole. That might be God by another name, which would make "atheist" the wrong word. Thomas settled on agnostic: Michael feels he knows nothing and seeks everything, with enthusiasm and without judgment. He recalled the cartoon on Michael's college refrigerator whose last frame read simply "Whatever," and said Michael's version is "whatever" with delight. Charlie called him a human golden retriever.

**Commentary.** Susan's thought experiment is a good one, because it tests the moral order from inside experience rather than by argument. If Michael's joy would not survive his becoming a thief and a liar, then his joy depends on living according to a law he has not acknowledged, which is the point Susan made earlier about common grace. That is a question he could answer honestly, and the answer would itself be evidence.

Thomas's correction of the label is fair-minded. Paul describes those who *"worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator"* **(Romans 1:25)**, and Michael's awe at being is close to that, but without the worship; he admires the whole and addresses no one. "Agnostic" is the more accurate word for a man who asks questions cheerfully and holds his conclusions open, and a friendly, open questioner is a better partner in conversation than many confident believers.

## 6. Claiming the name: the weight of Christian witness

Susan observed that what stops Michael from pursuing Christianity is the record of Christians warring with one another. She read the third commandment, against taking the Lord's name in vain, as forbidding claiming to follow God while not following Him. Thomas felt the weight of this personally: he presents himself as a candidate who knows how to live Christ's life, and asked how he can live up to that. Susan answered that no one can, by his own power; one must give up one's own will and rely on God's help, and many Christians do not realize they are living by their own strength, which is why their lives are not attractive to outsiders. Charlie added that Christ said His disciples would be known by their love for one another, yet Christians are divided and often hostile. Susan cautioned that gang members also love one another, so love alone could deceive; Charlie added that we know them also by their fruits. Thomas concluded that if Christians measured their witness by their love, no area of conduct would be left out, since all the gifts of the Spirit are subsets of love.

**Commentary.** Susan's reading of the commandment has strong support in the Hebrew. The verb usually translated "take" (*nasa*) means to lift up or carry, and many scholars read the commandment as forbidding bearing the Lord's name emptily: wearing His name as His people without living as His people. Swearing is included, but the deeper offense is the one Susan named. That reading also explains Michael's objection better than any apologetic argument could. The Christian wars he cites are, on this reading, violations of the third commandment, carried out under His name.

Her answer to the weight Thomas felt is the classic Christian one and the right one: *"I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me"* **(Philippians 4:13)**, and without Him, nothing **(John 15:5)**. A candidate who claims to know how to live Christ's life is safe only if he also claims to need Christ's help to live it.

The exchange about love and gang members resolves neatly in Jesus' own words. The love by which disciples are known is not in-group loyalty, which gangs have: *"For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye?** do not even the publicans the same?"* **(Matthew 5:46)**. It is love that extends to enemies, which gangs do not have. Charlie's "by their fruits" and Susan's caution are both answered by that distinction. Thomas's closing point is Paul's: *"love is the fulfilling of the law"* **(Romans 13:10)**.

## 7. Offense, forgiveness, and the acute phase

Susan described a small church whose members knew and loved one another, yet lost people one by one to offense and hurt. She sees this as one of Satan's main strategies against people who love God: small injuries become unforgiveness, which cuts believers off from God's forgiveness and spreads cracks through a community. The defense is to expect offenses and deal with them: forgive, talk them through, leave no resentment.

She asked Thomas how the body heals a small wound. He described the acute inflammatory phase, in which swelling and white cells clear away damaged tissue. Suppress it with ice and anti-inflammatories and the wound goes dormant, never quite red and never quite healed; it has gone chronic, and the cure is to re-stimulate the area so the acute phase can finish. The spiritual parallel is direct. Walking off an offense, punching a bag, or sleeping on it suppresses the acute phase. Healing requires going to the brother, naming the hurt, and diagnosing it together: was I wrong to take offense, or must he acknowledge a real wrong, apologize, and make it right? Thomas reads "turn the other cheek" in this light: once the offender has acknowledged the wrong, re-engage and trust him again, exposing yourself not so he can strike but believing he will not. Susan connected this to Jesus' instruction to leave one's gift at the altar and first be reconciled.

**Commentary.** The medical analogy is one of the most useful things said in the meeting, and it is accurate as medicine. Inflammation that is interrupted before it resolves is a recognized route to chronic, low-grade problems that do not heal. The spiritual version matches what pastors see: offenses that are "handled" by suppression stay tender for years and flare at small provocations.

The procedure Thomas described is close to Jesus' own: *"if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother"* **(Matthew 18:15)**. Thomas's added step, asking whether I was wrong to take offense, is wise and biblical: *"it is his glory to pass over a transgression"* **(Proverbs 19:11)**. Some offenses are best healed by recalibrating one's own judgment, and the conversation tells both parties which kind it was.

Susan's view of the spiritual stakes also has a precise text behind it. Paul urges the Corinthians to forgive a repentant brother *"Lest Satan should get an advantage of us: for we are not ignorant of his devices"* **(2 Corinthians 2:11)**. The device Paul names is the one Susan described: unforgiveness inside a community of believers.

On turning the other cheek, Thomas's reading needs one distinction to be fully sound. In Matthew 5 the instruction is unconditional: do not retaliate against an insult. That does not wait for the other man's repentance. What Thomas describes, re-extending trust after the offender acknowledges the wrong, is better grounded in another text: *"If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him"* **(Luke 17:3)**. Non-retaliation is owed always; restored trust follows repentance. Held together, the two readings Thomas mentioned (the group's earlier one and his own) are not alternatives but the two halves of the same teaching.

Susan suggested Thomas write on this subject. He noted that his MyCounselor site exists to collect exactly such counseling insights from 30 years of practice. Given how often offense divides churches, families, and marriages, the acute-phase essay would be a strong first entry.

## 8. Bastiat's principle, and the problem of getting there

Charlie said Thomas could run his whole campaign on Bastiat's *The Law*: government may do only what individuals have a right to do, and anything more is usurpation. Held to, that principle would make government smaller, cheaper, and believable. Thomas agreed and identified the hard part. If government stops taking from some to give to others, who will do what it now does, and how? Charlie's version of the question: how do you turn a cartel into a charity?

**Commentary.** Bastiat's principle has a moral foundation that makes it more than a libertarian slogan. If I may not take my neighbor's money by force to give to a third person, I may not hire the state to do it for me; the act does not become loving by being collective. The test that applies to any law is whether the neighbor on the receiving end of the force would recognize it as love, and much of what Bastiat called legal plunder fails that test. Thomas was right, though, to see that the principle is the easy part. A principle that cannot be implemented without catastrophe will not be implemented, and the second half of the meeting rightly turned to how.

## 9. Separation or reform?

Susan proposed that believers not try to change the government but separate from it: stop accepting its benefits and live under God's rule, perhaps by treaty, in the same territory but outside its jurisdiction. She acknowledged it is a wild idea that would need divine help, like Ezra's fast for a safe journey when he was ashamed to ask the king for soldiers. Thomas pointed to Romans 13: government has a righteous purpose, to defend the people and punish evil, and tribute is owed for it. Susan agreed she would gladly pay for such a government; her objection is to one that has turned shield into sword. She argued that as citizens left for another jurisdiction, the government would be forced to reform. Charlie doubted it, pointing to California, which loses its best citizens and only gets worse. Susan replied that California's leaders may want them gone, to make room for immigrants. Charlie answered that whatever the motive, exit will not convert the leaders, and that reform under the existing Constitution is better and likely bloodless: start by reading the Constitution and obeying it.

**Commentary.** Susan's proposal is less fanciful than she feared, because a small version of it already exists. Under U.S. law, members of certain religious communities that care for their own, such as the Amish, may apply to be exempt from Social Security taxes and benefits. That is a negotiated, jurisdictional separation within the same territory, and it works because the community actually provides what the state would otherwise provide. It shows both the possibility and the price: separation is credible only where the church is ready to carry the load.

The scriptural case for civil separation is thinner than for spiritual separation. Paul's *"come out from among them, and be ye separate"* **(2 Corinthians 6:17)** concerns partnership with idolatry, not exit from civil jurisdiction. When God's people lived under a pagan empire, Jeremiah told them, *"seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it"* **(Jeremiah 29:7)**, and Jesus told His hearers to render to Caesar what is Caesar's. Susan's model of Ezra is apt, however, in its method: when the right way is unclear, fast and ask God for it.

On exit as pressure, Charlie and Susan were both partly right. Exit pressures governments that depend on the people leaving, through lost revenue and votes; it can also leave behind a more uniform constituency that has no reason to change. California's domestic out-migration is real, but its political effect is ambiguous. Susan's claim about its leaders' motives is an attribution of intent for which no evidence was offered, and it is the weakest point in this part of the discussion. The concern underneath it, that some governments seem indifferent to losing their most productive citizens, is reasonable; the imputed motive is not established. Charlie's preference for reform under the Constitution is the prudent one, and it does not rule out Susan's separated communities as examples that show reform what is possible.

### If the followers lead

Thomas holds that if the followers lead, the leaders will follow: change ordinary hearts, and voting, business, and eventually candidates will follow. He admitted that his own belief has not changed the world, but said it would if millions shared it. His campaign is a template: here is what you should do; if you agree, do it. Susan found the precedent in Samuel's promise that if the people served the Lord, both they and their king would continue following Him.

**Commentary.** The precedent fits, and it is honest about its condition: the promise of **(1 Samuel 12:14)** is conditional on the people first. Scripture also records the reverse influence, *"When the righteous are in authority, the people rejoice"* **(Proverbs 29:2)**, so leadership matters as well; the two work together. Thomas's admission that believing has not yet changed the world is the honest starting point for any movement, and Jesus' parable answers it: the kingdom is like leaven hidden in flour *"till the whole was leavened"* **(Matthew 13:33)**. The method is slow, invisible, and pervasive, which is exactly what a template campaign aims to be.

### End times, and the case for building

Charlie had researched Christian views of the end times after a friend delayed her rehabilitation expecting the Rapture. He described several: a rapture and tribulation before Christ's return; a gradual improvement of the world through the gospel until Christ comes; and the Latter-day Saint expectation of rising calamity before His coming. If it falls to Christians to improve the world, Thomas's platform is a divine necessity. Charlie said he believes in planting the olive tree and tending it for a thousand years, and that even if there were no God, the world should be made better. Susan added another motive: a crisis is coming, whether tyranny, economic collapse, or foreign domination, and a house divided cannot stand.

**Commentary.** Charlie's conclusion is right regardless of which end-times view is correct. Jesus' parable of the servants entrusted with money ends with the command *"Occupy till I come"* **(Luke 19:13)**, which forbids the hunker-down posture whatever the timetable. His friend's story is a small, real example of the cost of the opposite view. Susan's argument from coming crisis is a legitimate persuasive appeal, and her point that very different groups share an interest in avoiding the same catastrophes is a sound basis for coalition. It will persuade best when the evidence for each danger is shown rather than asserted.

## 10. Conscience, homosexuality, and the hatred of wickedness

Susan observed that homosexuals would fare far better under Christians than under Sharia law. Thomas argued that many nonetheless side with Islam against Christianity because Christianity touches their conscience. They know the Bible's condemnation is true and want to silence the reminder, while discounting Islam's condemnation as coming from a false religion. He said the church has largely capitulated. Susan noted that many people who leave homosexual life for Christ share testimonies online and report hateful treatment from the community they left, which suggests its tolerance is in word only. Thomas then cited the Psalm, *"I hate them with perfect hatred,"* **(Psalm 139:22)**, and proposed that the bumper sticker "Hate is not a family value" be answered with "Hatred of wickedness is a family value." God loves the person and wants his soul, he said, but hates the lifestyle, and Christians should own that rather than pretend their faith is only "love, love, love."

**Commentary.** The core of Thomas's position is biblical and should be stated without apology. Christians are commanded to *"Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good"* **(Romans 12:9)**, and *"Hate the evil, and love the good"* **(Amos 5:15)**. A love that cannot hate what destroys the beloved is not love; Thomas's argument with Michael about the necessity of a standard applies here as well.

The Psalm needs careful handling, though, and Thomas's own formulation already supplies it. David's "perfect hatred" is directed at persons, God's enemies. In the New Testament, the believer's instruction toward enemies is different: *"Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you"* **(Matthew 5:44)**. The coherent Christian position, and the one Thomas actually stated, is that hatred belongs to wickedness and love to persons. His slogan, "hatred of wickedness is a family value," puts it precisely. His remark that "hatred has its place" will be heard as hatred of people unless it is always tied to that wording, and in print it should be.

Thomas's explanation of hostility to Christianity has real scriptural grounding as a general pattern: *"every one that doeth evil hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved"* **(John 3:20)**. As an explanation of a whole group's political alignments, it is an attribution of motive and should be offered as one. Other explanations also fit the facts: political coalitions form against whoever seems the nearer opponent, and Christians, not Muslims, have been the main opposition to gay-rights legislation in the United States. The alignment is also not uniform. In *Mahmoud v. **Taylor* (2025), Muslim and Christian parents together won the right to opt their children out of school lessons on gender and sexuality. Thomas's thesis, that conscience is at work beneath the hostility, remains plausible, especially in light of Susan's testimonies of how converts are treated. It is a reading of hearts, however, and should be presented as such rather than as an established fact.

The claim that "the church" has capitulated is accurate for several mainline Protestant denominations, which have changed their teaching on marriage and ordination. It is not accurate for the Catholic and Orthodox churches, the Southern Baptist Convention, or most evangelical churches, which have not. And the phrase "homosexual mafia," spoken in conversation, would fail Thomas's own standard of proportion in a published essay; it would cost him the very readers whose consciences he hopes to reach.

Susan's point about converts is the most persuasive in this section, because it is testimony, not inference. Accounts of people who leave and then face hostility from those who preach tolerance are numerous, and they expose a contradiction that argument alone cannot. They are best treated as leads and documented case by case.

## 11. A picture made of dots

Thomas described his campaign method as pointillism: hundreds of essays, 23 in a single week, each a dot, together forming a picture of how God would have the country, families, business, education, and individuals live. Exposed to enough of it, a reader sees the picture arise in his spirit. Charlie connected this to Bastiat's closing appeal to try liberty, and asked whether the fundamental thing is aligning our lives with God's ways. Thomas said that is the action step, but the heart comes first: a man acts from what is in his heart, so the heart must be purified, and then right conduct toward the poor man, the tax collector, and the employee follows.

**Commentary.** The method suits the message. A way of life cannot be conveyed in a slogan, and a large body of concrete applications is how a worldview becomes visible. Thomas's order, heart then action, is Solomon's: *"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life"* **(Proverbs 4:23)**. The risk of pointillism is that the viewer stands too close. Charlie and Susan both said the volume of essays was making their heads spin. A pointillist painting works because the viewer can step back; a short summary page that shows the whole picture at a glance would let readers do the same, and would make the dots worth reading.

## 12. Soul, spirit, and Mike Tyson

Thomas described an insight from the week: the Word of God divides soul from spirit, the soul being the animal man and the spirit the God-man. When Michael blends the two, he treats an animal impulse and a spiritual reality as one thing. Thomas illustrated with the story of Mike Tyson from a Netflix documentary: a gentle, bullied boy from a brutal home who loved a pet pigeon, and became merciless after a bully killed it. Years later, after prison and the loss and recovery of his title, Tyson said that the revenge he chased never filled him; it was a ghost. Thomas's conclusion: a bad environment can nurture the animal and let it run a person, or he can choose to ally with the spirit against the soul and control the animal within.

**Commentary.** The Tyson story is well chosen because it shows both truths at once: environment wounds and shapes, and the person still chooses what he nurtures. Scripture holds both: children suffer for their parents' sins in consequence, but *"the soul that sinneth, it shall die"* **(Ezekiel 18:20)**, each answerable for his own choices. Tyson's mature verdict on revenge is a modern confirmation of an old command: *"Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves... for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord"* **(Romans 12:19)**. Revenge did not satisfy because it was never his to take.

The vocabulary needs one adjustment. Hebrews does speak of the Word *"piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit"* **(Hebrews 4:12)**, but Scripture does not treat the soul as the animal nature; the soul also praises God: *"Bless the LORD, O my soul"* **(Psalm 103:1)**. The Bible's usual word for the animal nature at war with the spirit is "flesh": *"For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh"* **(Galatians 5:17)**. Thomas's distinction is sound; calling the animal side "flesh" rather than "soul," or defining "soul" at its first use, will spare readers a confusion and match his own rule that in-house terms be defined in plain words.

## 13. Evil spirits

Susan suggested that when Tyson turned, he was following an evil spirit, and that besides the flesh and the spirit there are false and unclean spirits, as Ephesians says. People under their influence often think, "That's just who I am." Thomas agreed, adding that the Holy Spirit personifies goodness, that God divided perfection from darkness, and that Christ, the Jehovah of the Old Testament, said He creates good and evil, creating the opportunity, not making people evil. Susan added that one becomes the servant of whatever one obeys, and that materialists like Michael see neither God nor the enemy who entices them. Thomas noted that if there is no good, there is no evil either, only names.

**Commentary.** Susan's addition completes the picture. Classical Christian teaching names three sources of temptation, the world, the flesh, and the devil, and James lists all three in one verse, calling false wisdom *"earthly, sensual, devilish"* **(James 3:15)**. Thomas's model of soul and spirit covers the flesh; Susan's point covers the devil; the Tyson story shows the world. An account of moral struggle that names only one will misdiagnose people who are fighting another.

One textual correction matters for Thomas's argument. The verse he paraphrased reads *"I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil"* **(Isaiah 45:7)**. The Hebrew word *ra* there stands opposite "peace" and is widely translated "calamity" or "disaster" in modern versions. It refers to God's sovereignty over judgment and misfortune, not to God creating moral wickedness. Thomas's own position, that God created the possibility of evil and not evil itself, is better served by that reading than by the King James wording, and he should avoid quoting it as "I create good and I create evil," which a skeptic like Michael would seize on.

## 14. Knowing God's ways by doing them

Armond recalled a man who said he had never seen anyone's life improve through abstinence. Armond's thought was that God's promises can be verified only by trying them. When he debates relatives, he tells them that reading every book means nothing without application, and points to things they unknowingly did according to biblical principle that led to success. Susan cited Jesus' promise that whoever does His will shall know whether the teaching is from God, and Psalm 111:10; Charlie recalled Emerson's "Do the thing, and you shall have the power"; Susan added that no one learns to swim from a book. She also noted, through the parable of the houses on rock and sand, that the consequences of obedience or disobedience often appear only in the storm, while our culture expects immediate results. Armond said conviction is the immediate consequence, but many people numb it with drugs, alcohol, or habit. Susan cited Hebrews 12: those who receive no discipline from the Lord are not His sons, and Thomas read the passage.

**Commentary.** This was the most practically persuasive exchange of the meeting, and it answers the question raised at its start: how to reach a man whose every rebuttal is physical. Jesus did not ask for belief before evidence; He offered an experiment: *"If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine"* **(John 7:17)**. The Psalms make the same invitation, *"O taste and see that the LORD is good"* **(Psalm 34:8)**, and in one place God explicitly invites testing: *"prove me now herewith, saith the LORD of hosts"* **(Malachi 3:10)**. Armond's approach of pointing out where his relatives already benefit from God's principles is the common-grace observation put to work.

Susan's point about delay is the necessary complement. An experiment with a long timescale must be run long enough to show results, and Paul promises results on that timescale: *"for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not"* **(Galatians 6:9)**. It also guards the experiment against a false negative. A believer may obey and still suffer for a season, as Job did; the test is a life's fruit, not a week's.

Armond's observation about numbed conviction has a precise biblical image in consciences *"seared with a hot iron"* **(1 Timothy 4:2)**, and it explains why the man he overheard could not see what others could. Finally, Armond called the Hebrews passage scary, and in one sense it is. But its point is reassuring for anyone who feels corrected: discipline is evidence of belonging, not of rejection, and *"afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby"* **(Hebrews 12:11)**.

## 15. Getting from here to there: harmonious transformation

Thomas warned that running on Bastiat is like turning oneself into a pretzel. With millions dependent on welfare, abruptly ending it would literally kill people, because society has become addicted to what he called a satanic system. Susan answered that implementing Bastiat requires teaching the gospel, and that a crisis is coming that will force the choice if people do not choose now; Thomas listed possible judgments, from an Iranian bomb to a leaked laboratory virus to demographic replacement under Sharia law over 100 to 500 years. Susan then argued that the fear of starvation overlooks believers' charity: churches, charities, and families would help, with accountability, setting terms such as three months of help followed by a review of progress, and weaning people off dependence. Thomas called this harmonious transformation, a term from Chinese medicine, as opposed to the inharmonious kind he had been warning against. He pictured laws granting tax reductions to those who give to relief agencies, building a patchwork of private care that gradually replaces the public kind. He explained why the change is necessary: compulsory redistribution makes the recipient feel entitled rather than given to, and the giver resentful rather than virtuous, so everything good about charity is lost.

**Commentary.** Harmonious transformation is the wisest idea in this part of the discussion, and it has a striking biblical precedent. When God promised Israel the land, He said He would not drive out its inhabitants in one year, *"lest the land become desolate, and the beast of the field multiply against thee.* *By little and little I will drive them out from before thee, until thou be increased"* **(Exodus 23:29–30)**. A sudden victory would have left a vacuum. Thomas's gradualism applies the same wisdom: the private structures must grow before the public ones withdraw.

Susan's accountable charity also follows the New Testament pattern. The early church kept a list of widows it supported, with criteria **(1 Timothy 5:9–10)**, and Paul wrote *"if any would not work, neither should he eat"* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**. Help with terms and review is not stinginess; it is the biblical shape of charity. Thomas's tax-credit idea is also not hypothetical. The federal charitable deduction already exists, and several states offer dollar-for-dollar credits for gifts to qualifying charities serving the poor. Expanding that model is a concrete, legislatable first step.

Thomas's diagnosis of why compulsion corrupts charity is his strongest moral point, and Paul states it in a single line: *"not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver"* **(2 Corinthians 9:7)**. Coerced giving cannot be cheerful, and an entitlement cannot be received as a gift. His label "satanic system," however, will lose readers who would accept the argument. Bastiat's own term, legal plunder, makes the same charge with more precision and less heat, and it is the term already in play.

Of the coming dangers listed, the threat of an Iranian weapon is the most concrete. The origin of COVID-19 remains disputed among U.S. agencies, and current population projections show Muslims at roughly 2% of the U.S. population by mid-century, so the demographic scenario is speculative. The underlying concern, that a nation without shared moral foundations is vulnerable to whatever force fills the vacuum, does not depend on any one of these scenarios, and is better argued directly.

## 16. Taxes, kingship, and the reign of Christ

Susan said she would like to see a person owe no taxes at all. Thomas answered that taxes will remain, because Romans 13 commands tribute to rulers who defend the people and punish evil; they should simply be in proportion. Susan disagreed and explained why through the Old Testament cycle: God's people disobey, foreign rulers oppress them, they cry out, God raises a deliverer, and the cycle repeats. God originally wanted to be Israel's only King. She believes God is now raising a deliverer so that His people may again live under His rule before Christ returns, and her essay argues that Scripture provides a way. Thomas replied that after the judges there were only kings, good and bad, native and foreign, and never again rule by God alone. That will come when Christ returns and all things are subjected to the Father. Until then, every believer may come directly to God through Christ and should, but the nations still need the sword, which the state, not private citizens, bears. One cannot raise a private army against Iran. Susan said the exchange had clarified their difference, that she still disagreed, and that she might change her mind. Thomas said they are all on a journey.

**Commentary.** Each side holds a piece the other needs. Susan is right about God's original desire; when Israel demanded a king, God told Samuel, *"they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them"* **(1 Samuel 8:7)**. Thomas is right about the subsequent history and about the sword: the ruler *"beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil"* **(Romans 13:4)**. The decisive New Testament evidence favors Thomas on present duty. Peter, writing under Nero, told believers to *"Honour the king"* **(1 Peter 2:17)**, and the church of the first three centuries lived as faithful citizens of an empire it did not control, not as a separate jurisdiction.

Yet one episode gives Susan's instinct real support. When collectors asked whether Jesus paid the temple tax, He said that the children of the king are free, and then paid it anyway: *"Notwithstanding, lest we should offend them"* **(Matthew 17:27)**. The principle Susan defends, that God's children are by right free, is affirmed there; so is Thomas's practice, paying what is levied for the sake of peace. The passage suggests that the question is not whether believers are rightly free, but when it is wise to claim that freedom.

The two positions also converge in practice. Thomas's plan (relief through the church, taxes reduced to the cost of defense and justice) would look, after a generation, much like the community Susan imagines, built from within the existing order rather than by withdrawal. And Susan's readiness to say "I might change my mind" while maintaining her disagreement is the posture the fellowship asks of everyone, including Michael.

## 17. Where the discussion stands

Taken together, the second half of the meeting built a coherent program out of separate threads. The heart comes first: offenses must be brought into the open and healed, not suppressed, and God's ways are proven by living them over time. The witness of believers is the decisive evidence for or against their God, which is why claiming His name without following Him is the gravest breach of the third commandment. Public life follows private life: the followers lead, laws can then reward charity rather than compel it, and the transition must be gradual enough that no one is abandoned in the change. On these points the participants largely agreed, and the agreement is well grounded in Scripture.

The discussion's real disagreements were honest and productive: whether God enjoys creation or created for our joy (Scripture affirms both His self-sufficiency and His delight); whether believers should separate from an unjust government or reform it (the New Testament favors faithful presence, while Jesus' word on the temple tax affirms the freedom Susan defends); and whether a public covenant matters to a committed relationship (it does). The weakest moments were the readings of other people's motives, of California's leaders and of homosexuals' political alliances. Each may contain truth, but each is an inference about hearts presented as fact, and in print each should be labeled as such or left out. The places where Thomas's own texts need care are Revelation 4:11, where "pleasure" means will, and Isaiah 45:7, where "evil" means calamity. Both are worth correcting before they appear in published essays.

The meeting ended with Susan's prayer that each member would be enlightened during the week. The discussion itself shows what that looks like among believers who disagree: positions held firmly, reasons given, Scripture consulted, and minds left open to a better argument.
