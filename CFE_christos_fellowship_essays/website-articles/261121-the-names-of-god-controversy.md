# 261121 - The Names of God Controversy

# The Names of God:


### What Was Revealed, What Was Hidden, and What Saves

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 21 November 2026

## The controversy and my stand

Few questions divide sincere Bible readers as sharply as this one: What is God's name, how should it be spoken, and does it matter whether we call His Son "Jesus" or by a Hebrew form such as Yeshua, Yahshua, or Yahusha? On social media the question usually arrives as a picture with Hebrew letters, a few verses, and an urgent message: the true names were hidden, and the Church has been calling on substitutes.

The people who hold this view are often called the *Sacred Name movement*: a family of groups, beginning in the United States in the 1930s, that teach that God must be called only by His Hebrew name and the Son only by His Hebrew name. They do not agree among themselves on what those names are. Some say Yahweh, some Yahuah, some Yehovah, and the Jehovah's Witnesses, who arrived at a similar conviction by another road, say Jehovah. For the Son, the candidates include Yeshua, Yahshua, Yahushua, and Yahusha.

Here is where I stand. God has a personal name. He revealed it on purpose, He said it was His memorial forever, and human tradition, not God, hid it from most believers. On those points the Sacred Name believers are right, and the mainstream Church has been too quick to dismiss them. But no one is saved by pronouncing a name, and no one is lost by pronouncing it in his own language. The name in Scripture stands for the Person: His character, His authority, and His presence. That Person lives inside the experience of everyone who calls on Him, and He knows exactly whom we mean.

In this essay I will set out what God revealed, how the name came to be hidden, what the evidence shows about how it was pronounced, what happened to the Son's name, the strongest case for the Sacred Name position, and why I believe the apostles themselves settled the question of whether translation is betrayal.

## The question under the question

Arguments about vowels and syllables are rarely only about vowels and syllables. Underneath this one I hear three deeper questions, and each deserves a serious answer.

The first is: *Has the Church lost something God gave?* Many believers discover in mid-life that the word "LORD" in their Bible stands for a personal name, and that no one ever told them. They wonder what else was left out. That is a reasonable question to ask.

The second is: *Can I trust the people who handed me my faith?* The history of the name involves rabbis who forbade saying it, translators who replaced it, and scholars who disagree about it. A person who has already been burned by institutions will read that history as a pattern of concealment.

The third, and the deepest, is: *How do I reach the real God, and not a substitute?* This is a holy desire. It is the same desire that made Moses ask, *"What is his name?"* **(Exodus 3:13)**, and that made Agur ask, *"what is his name, and what is his son's name, if thou canst tell?"* **(Proverbs 30:4)**. That verse from Proverbs is said to be the one that set the Sacred Name movement in motion.

I share all three concerns. My answer to the first two is yes, something was hidden, and caution toward institutions is often wise. My answer to the third is the heart of this essay: we reach the real God by receiving the One He sent, and letting His Word live in us. The correct sound is not the doorway. The Person is.

## What God revealed

The Hebrew Bible does not treat God's name as a secret. It treats it as a gift.

When Moses asked what to tell Israel, God first gave the meaning of His name, *"I AM THAT I AM"* **(Exodus 3:14)**, and then the name itself: *"Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, The LORD God of your fathers… hath sent me unto you: this is my name for ever, and this is my memorial unto all generations"* **(Exodus 3:15)**. Where the King James Version prints "LORD" in small capitals, the Hebrew has four letters, יהוה, which scholars call the *Tetragrammaton* (Greek for "four letters"), and which we can write in English as YHWH. The name is almost certainly built on the Hebrew verb "to be." It declares that God is the self-existent One, who is present with His people.

The name appears nearly 6,800 times in the Hebrew Bible, far more than any other word for God. God said He acted *"that my name may be declared throughout all the earth"* **(Exodus 9:16)**, and that *"my name shall be great among the Gentiles"* **(Malachi 1:11)**. He declared, *"I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another"* **(Isaiah 42:8)**. The short form *Yah* survives in praise to this day: "Hallelujah" means "Praise Yah," and the King James Version keeps it once as *"JAH"* **(Psalm 68:4)**.

Ordinary Israelites used the name in daily life. When Boaz came to his field, he greeted his workers, *"The LORD be with you,"* and they answered, *"The LORD bless thee"* **(Ruth 2:4)**. The name was also woven into the names of their children: Elijah ("my God is Yah"), Isaiah ("Yah saves"), Jehoshaphat ("Yah has judged"), Joshua ("Yah is salvation").

At the same time, God guarded His name with a warning: *"Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain"* **(Exodus 20:7)**. And when He proclaimed His name to Moses on the mountain, He proclaimed His character: *"The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth"* **(Exodus 34:6)**. In Scripture, to know God's name is to know who He is.

## How the name was hidden

If God gave His name to be used, how did most of His people stop using it? The record shows a gradual withdrawal over many centuries, driven first by reverence and fear of misuse, then by custom, and finally by translation. It was not a single act of concealment, and the people involved did not all move at once.

Two honest conclusions follow. First, the name really was withdrawn from use, and the withdrawal was a human tradition with no command of God behind it. The Mishnah's own conflicting rulings, one forbidding the name and one commanding it in greetings, show that Jewish teachers themselves were not of one mind.

Second, the motive was mostly reverence, not malice. The rabbis feared breaking the Third Commandment. If a man never speaks the name, he can never take it in vain. That reasoning is understandable, but it builds a fence around the law that God did not build, and the fence ended up hiding the very thing it was meant to protect.

## How was the name pronounced?

No one alive knows for certain. Ancient Hebrew was written with consonants only, and by the time vowel marks were added, the name had not been spoken in public for centuries. Every modern pronunciation is a reconstruction. The useful question is which reconstruction the evidence supports best, and what kind of evidence that is.

The evidence comes in four kinds. The short form *Yah* is certain, preserved in Scripture itself. Names built on the divine name show its opening sounds: they begin with *Yeho-* or *Yo-* (Jehoshaphat, Jonathan) and end with *-yahu* or *-yah* (Isaiah, Elijah). Early writers who recorded the name in Greek letters give us vowels, though the manuscripts sometimes disagree. And the Masoretic vowels, the marks added between AD 600 and 1000, are most likely borrowed from *Adonai*, though that point is disputed too.

My reading of this record is simple. "Yahweh" is the best-supported guess, a three-syllable form is a real possibility, and the people who are most certain are the people whose certainty goes beyond the evidence. That alone should give us pause before we make any one pronunciation a test of faithfulness. If the scholars who have given their lives to the question cannot settle it, God has not hung our salvation on it.

## The Son's name

The Son's Hebrew name has a clearer history than the Father's, because it was an ordinary name borne by many men, and we can follow it through Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and English.


 	- **Yehoshua.** Moses gave this name to his servant Hoshea: *"And Moses called Oshea the son of Nun Jehoshua"* **(Numbers 13:16)**. It means "Yah is salvation."

 	- **Yeshua.** After the Exile the name was commonly shortened. Nehemiah calls Joshua son of Nun "Jeshua" **(Nehemiah 8:17)**, and the high priest of the return is called Jeshua in Ezra **(Ezra 3:2)** and Joshua in Haggai **(Haggai 1:1)**: one man, two forms of one name. This is almost certainly the form His mother and neighbors used in Nazareth.

 	- **Iēsous.** Greek has no "sh" sound and gives masculine names an *-s* ending. So when Jewish scholars translated the Scriptures into Greek, two centuries before Christ, they wrote Joshua's name as *Iēsous* ([Wikipedia, Jesus (name)](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Iesu)). The apostles used the same form, even for Joshua himself. The King James Version shows this plainly: *"Which also our fathers that came after brought in with Jesus into the possession of the Gentiles"* **(Acts 7:45)**, and *"if Jesus had given them rest"* **(Hebrews 4:8)**. Both verses mean Joshua.

 	- **Iesus and Jesus.** Latin wrote the Greek name as *Iesus*. The letter J was first distinguished from I in the 1500s, and the 1611 King James Version still printed "Iesus"; the spelling "Jesus" came in with later printings ([Equip](https://www.equip.org/article/jesus-name-used-vain-answering-sacred-name-movement/)).


Two popular claims fail on this evidence. The first is that "Jesus" means "Hail Zeus" or honors Zeus. It does not. *Zeus* begins with a different Greek letter (zeta) from the *-sous* ending of *Iēsous* (sigma), the Greek word for "hail" is *chaire*, and the Jewish translators who first wrote *Iēsous* would never have built a pagan god into Joshua's name ([Hoshana Rabbah](https://hoshanarabbah.org/blog/tag/zeus); [Bible Hub](https://mail.biblehub.com/q/is_'jesus'_derived_from_'hail_zeus'.htm)). The second is that the Son's "true" name was Yahshua or Yahusha. These are modern reconstructions, made by putting the full divine syllable "Yah" into the name. No ancient manuscript, inscription, or writer gives them. Messianic Jewish scholars such as Michael Brown, who love the Hebrew names, reject them ([Wikipedia, Yahshua](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahshua)).

The reason the reconstruction is attractive is theological, not historical. Sacred Name teachers read Jesus' words, *"I am come in my Father's name"* **(John 5:43)**, as meaning that His own name must contain the Father's name. But Jesus explained what He meant: *"the works that I do in my Father's name, they bear witness of me"* **(John 10:25)**. To come in the Father's name is to come with His authority and do His works, as an ambassador comes in the name of his king. And in any case, every form above, from Yehoshua to Jesus, carries the meaning the angel announced: *"thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins"* **(Matthew 1:21)**.

## The strongest case for the Sacred Name position

Before I give my own conclusion, I want to state the other side as its best advocates would, not as a caricature. Their argument runs like this.


 	- **God commanded the name to be remembered.** He called it *"my memorial unto all generations"* **(Exodus 3:15)** and placed it in Scripture nearly 6,800 times. No verse authorizes replacing it.

 	- **Replacing it with "Lord" echoes Baal worship.** God condemned prophets who *"think to cause my people to forget my name… as their fathers have forgotten my name for Baal"* **(Jeremiah 23:27)**. *Baal* means "lord" or "master." Through Hosea God said, *"thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali.* *For I will take away the names of Baalim out of her mouth"* **(Hosea 2:16–17)**. Sacred Name writers argue that putting "Lord" where God put His name repeats the very exchange God condemned ([Heavenly Father's Great Name](https://tazewellcountyjury.gov/pdfs/the_Heavenly_Fathers_Great_Name.pdf)).

 	- **A title is not a name.** "Mister" is not Sam Jones's name; "Lord" and "God" are titles that pagans also used for their gods ([YRM](https://www.issuu.com/yrministry/docs/challenges_to_the_sacred_name)).

 	- **The ban was a man-made tradition.** Jesus rebuked those who were *"Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition"* **(Mark 7:13)**. The rule against speaking the name comes from the rabbis, not from Moses.

 	- **The earliest Greek Bibles kept the name.** The oldest Jewish copies of the Greek Old Testament wrote the name in Hebrew letters, not as *Kyrios*. In 1977 George Howard, a Baptist professor at the University of Georgia, argued in the *Journal of Biblical Literature* that the original New Testament writings may also have kept the name in Old Testament quotations, and that later copyists replaced it ([Howard's hypothesis](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Howard%E2%80%99s_hypothesis)).

 	- **"Jesus" is a late English form.** No one in the first century ever heard the word "Jesus." It took its present spelling only in the 1600s.

 	- **Scripture promises salvation to those who call on the name.** *"Whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered"* **(Joel 2:32)**, and God says of the faithful, *"I will set him on high, because he hath known my name"* **(Psalm 91:14)**.


Some of this is strong. Points 1 and 4 are simply true. Point 5 is real evidence about Jewish practice before Christ, though Howard's extension of it to the New Testament remains a minority view: no surviving New Testament manuscript contains the Hebrew name, and the earliest, from about AD 175, all use *Kyrios* ([Wikiwand](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Howard's_hypothesis)). Point 2 is a serious reading of serious texts and deserves a serious answer, which I give below. Points 3, 6, and 7 depend on what Scripture means by "name," which is where I believe the argument turns.

## Why the concern remains plausible

Even where I disagree with the Sacred Name conclusion, I do not think its followers are foolish to be concerned. Several things make their suspicion reasonable.

**The loss is real, and it changed how people think about God.** A personal name invites relationship; a title keeps distance. When a reader meets "the LORD" nearly seven thousand times and never meets God's name, he can easily come to think of God as an office rather than a Person. Many who begin using God's name report that their prayer became more personal and their sense of His nearness grew. I treat those reports as more than sentiment. They point to something the Church lost.

**Institutions have reasons to keep things as they are.** Bible translations are made by committees, sold in markets, and read by congregations accustomed to familiar wording. Restoring the name would cost money, invite controversy, and unsettle readers. Those pressures do not make translators dishonest, but they do mean that the absence of the name from most Bibles is not proof that it belongs absent. When the people who decide a question also bear the cost of changing it, their judgment deserves a second look.

**The Church did cut itself off from its Hebrew roots.** Within a few centuries of the apostles, much of the Church had turned away from the Sabbath, the feasts, and the Hebrew way of reading Scripture, and some of that turning was driven by contempt for Jews rather than by the teaching of the apostles. A believer who learns that history can fairly ask what else was lost, and the hiding of God's name is the most visible example.

**The two traditions reinforced each other.** The rabbis hid the name out of fear of profaning it. The Church inherited "Lord" through the Greek Bible and never questioned it. Each tradition made the other look settled. A careful reader who sees that both were human customs, and that neither came from a command of God, is right to ask why we should keep them.

So the concern is not a fantasy. Something precious was set aside, for reasons that were partly reverent and partly institutional. Where I part from the Sacred Name movement is not in recognizing that loss. It is in what follows from it.

## What the apostles did, and what it settles

The decisive evidence, for me, is not in the Masoretic vowels or the Greek transcriptions of the church fathers. It is in what Jesus and His apostles actually did, as the Holy Spirit recorded it.

**Jesus taught His disciples to pray, "Our Father."** When they asked Him how to pray, He did not give them the correct pronunciation of the four letters. He said, *"Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name"* **(Matthew 6:9)**. In Gethsemane He prayed *"Abba, Father"* **(Mark 14:36)**. On the cross He cried *"Eli, Eli"*, "My God, my God" **(Matthew 27:46)**. The Son, who knew the Father's name better than anyone, addressed Him most often by the most intimate title a child has. And when He summed up His ministry, He said, *"I have manifested thy name unto the men which thou gavest me"* **(John 17:6)**. He manifested it by showing them the Father's character, not by teaching them a sound: *"he that hath seen me hath seen the Father"* **(John 14:9)**.

**The Son's name was written in Greek in every surviving manuscript.** Whatever one thinks of Howard's theory about Old Testament quotations, it does not touch the Son's name. In every New Testament manuscript we have, the One the angel named is *Iēsous*. When Peter told the Sanhedrin that *"there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved"* **(Acts 4:12)**, Luke recorded the name two verses earlier in Greek: *"by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth"* **(Acts 4:10)**. The verse most often used to demand the Hebrew name was itself written with the Greek one.

**The apostles applied God's name to Jesus through the word "Lord."** On the day of Pentecost Peter quoted Joel, *"whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved"* **(Acts 2:21)**, and ended his sermon by declaring that God had made Jesus *"both Lord and Christ"* **(Acts 2:36)**. Paul quoted the same verse **(Romans 10:13)** and applied it to Jesus. He took Isaiah's words about the LORD, *"unto me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear"* **(Isaiah 45:23)**, and wrote that God has given Jesus *"a name which is above every name: That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow…* *And that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord"* **(Philippians 2:9–11)**. For the apostles, "Lord" was not a Baal substitute. It was the confession that Jesus shares the identity of the God of Israel.

**The Spirit spoke in every language at once.** At Pentecost the Spirit did not make the nations learn Hebrew. He made the apostles speak the nations' languages, and the crowd said, *"we do hear them speak in our tongues the wonderful works of God"* **(Acts 2:11)**. In heaven John saw a multitude *"of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues"* **(Revelation 7:9)**. God's plan was always that every tongue would confess Him in its own tongue.

**The correct name without the Person has no power.** The seven sons of Sceva tried to cast out a demon *"by Jesus whom Paul preacheth"* **(Acts 19:13)**. They used the name exactly, and the demon answered, *"Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?"* **(Acts 19:15)**. Jesus warned that many would say *"Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name?"* and hear, *"I never knew you"* **(Matthew 7:22–23)**.

This answers the Baal argument too. In Jeremiah and Hosea the sin was not a word; it was a god. Israel had blended the LORD with the Canaanite Baal, worshipping him with Baal's rites and Baal's morals. God was not objecting to the dictionary meaning of "master." He was objecting to Israel treating Him as one more Baal. If saying "Lord" were itself the sin of Jeremiah 23, then Peter and Paul, writing under the Spirit's inspiration, committed it on almost every page.

## Where I stand: the name, the Person, and what saves

I stand with the Sacred Name believers in this: God revealed His name, it should be known and taught, and the Church lost something real when it let tradition bury it. I would be glad to see every Bible print God's name where God put it. I stand against them in this: no pronunciation is a condition of salvation, and no believer is deceived or lost because he calls the Son "Jesus." Here is why.

**A name in Scripture is the Person, not the sound.** The Hebrew word for name, *shem*, means a person's character, reputation, and authority. That is why God answered Moses' request to see His glory by proclaiming His character **(Exodus 34:5–7)**, and why Jesus could say He had manifested the Father's name by revealing the Father. To call on the name of the LORD is to call on the One who bears it, trusting His character and submitting to His authority.

**God has many names because He has many aspects.** He is *El Shaddai*, God Almighty **(Genesis 17:1)**; *Jehovah-jireh*, the LORD who provides **(Genesis 22:14)**; *Jehovah-shalom*, the LORD who is peace **(Judges 6:24)**. The Son is called *"Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace"* **(Isaiah 9:6)**, *Emmanuel*, the Word, the Lamb, the Good Shepherd. No single name holds Him. The very fact that Scripture gives so many tells us that each is a window onto Someone larger than any window.

**A name can become an idol.** When God commanded Moses to make a bronze serpent, those who looked at it were healed. Centuries later King Hezekiah broke it in pieces, *"for unto those days the children of Israel did burn incense to it"* **(2 Kings 18:4)**. A gift of God had become a substitute for God. The same can happen to a name, a pronunciation, or even a page of Scripture. When we treat the sound or the letters as the thing itself, we make an earthbound image of a God who is spirit. *"God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth"* **(John 4:24)**.

**God hears from inside.** This is the deepest reason I am not troubled by the pronunciation question. God is not a distant judge listening through a wall for the right password. He lives inside every person's experience; in a real sense, our lives are His experience of the world. (I set out the argument in my [Premise Change handout](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/10/31/260725-premise-change-handout/) and in [Reconciliation of Man with the Father](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/07/25/260725-reconciliation-of-man-with-the-father/).) He knows the intent of the heart before the words are formed: *"there is not a word in my tongue, but, lo, O LORD, thou knowest it altogether"* **(Psalm 139:4)**. When we cannot form words at all, *"the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered"* **(Romans 8:26)**. The One who lives inside the woman in a village who has only ever heard "Yesu" knows exactly whom she means.

**What saves is the Word received and abiding.** Jesus told the men who knew the Scriptures and the divine name perfectly, *"ye have not his word abiding in you: for whom he hath sent, him ye believe not"* **(John 5:38)**. Their problem was not pronunciation; it was unbelief. What God asks is that we believe the One He sent, and let His Word take root in us: *"Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee"* **(Psalm 119:11)**. Because God lives our lives with us, He feels our choices; we keep His law because we love Him and do not want to wound Him. I develop this in a companion essay, *The Father Who Sends and the Word That Abides*.

So my counsel is simple. If using the Hebrew names draws you closer to God, use them with joy and reverence. Teach others that God has a name. But do not make a reconstruction of it a test of anyone's faith, and do not let it divide brothers whom the Son died to make one. *"By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another"* **(John 13:35)**.

**Limits.** My sourcing leans on mainstream scholarship and on Christian writers who oppose the Sacred Name teaching. Both have interests. Academic scholarship works within a consensus that rarely rewards challenging it, and apologetics ministries exist partly to defend established practice. Several of the sources that document the early Greek manuscripts are also published by the Jehovah's Witnesses, who have their own stake in the question. And the underlying evidence is thin on every side: no recording of Moses survives, the vowel tradition was written down more than a thousand years after him, and the scribes who wrote it had reasons, reverent ones, to obscure the name. I read summaries of several of these sources rather than every primary document, and a reader who wants certainty about pronunciation should follow the scholarly sources directly. What I hold with certainty does not depend on any of them: the apostles wrote the Son's name in Greek under the Spirit's inspiration, and God hears the heart that calls on Him.
