# 260823 - Recap - Triangulation in the Fellowship

# 260823 – Every Man His Own Trench: Triangulation Tested in the Fellowship


**A fellowship essay – Fellowship meeting of Sunday, August 23, 2026**

**Participants: Thomas Abshier, Charlie Gutierrez, Susan Gutierrez, Leonard Hofheins, Michael Sherman, Armond Boulware**


---


## Introduction: The Essay Meets Its Examiners


Last week I sent out “Triangulating Truth,” the essay drawn from my long conversation with John — my friend of fifty years, the honest empiricist who lives uprightly without belief. I invited John to join us, but he couldn’t attend because of other obligations. Instead, Michael took up John’s arguments. Leonard argued that we have too little revelation today, rather than too much. Susan gave us her own three points she uses to triangulate truth. Armond called in from under a shade tree far from his home to join because his home had been without power for the last two weeks, and gave us his theology of adversity in one sentence. And Charlie advocated for revelation by conversation.


What follows is an example of truth being found where independent witnesses converge, and that every soul has its own narrow trench to the target.


## I. Michael Takes Up John’s Part: Is Faith the Enemy of Science?


I opened by describing John to the group — a man whose parents were abusive to each other. He grew up in fear. He claims to think in words rather than pictures. His world is concrete, solid, and complete, with nothing unseen behind it. I asked Michael whether that description resonated with his own way of seeing. He felt that John “is misleading himself, because he can feel cold and hot without seeing it… Light itself, the thing that helps us see, is invisible. Sound is invisible. You can’t hit the force that lifts a paper clip to a magnet — you see only its effects… Do not deny what you clearly see merely because it’s invisible. Surely he has had love, and love is not strictly visible.”


Then Michael noted that the great advances of science were made by men who *refused* faith — Galileo, Kepler, Newton, Einstein — men who would not believe the obvious, who overthrew four centuries of settled physics because they stayed open. “Faith is defiant of, in fact antithetical to, the scientific principle.” His preferred word is not materialism but **reproducibility**: propose a thing, test it under every condition, and assign it “a percent likelihood based on how often it holds true.” The scientist he admires is a seeker, not a believer — “I search” rather than “I believe” — and a true scientist would never even be an atheist, since one cannot prove a negative: “the scientist I’m defining would remain open, one hundred percent.”


Of course, I agree. What Michael described is how science *should* be done, and I have no argument with it whatsoever. My quarrel is with the *culture*. Michael offered that a better description would be the *cult* of science. As a confirmation of my assertion that science reflexively rejects the inclusion of God, I note that every AI I have asked to comment on my writing tells me the same thing about my physics papers — that any mention of God, even by implication, even the mere name “Conscious Point Physics,” will have the work rejected by arXiv, by Nature, by Physical Review Letters; that I will be considered crank physics and dismissed as not-science. Yet the same journals will publish, without irony, that mass arose from nothing by quantum fluctuation, evolved by itself, and became giraffes, poodles, and kangaroos. Those are the beliefs of a faith — the religion of materialism. My assertion is that the question is never faith versus no faith in the spiritualism vs. materialism debate; it is only *what the faith is in*.


Michael’s replied that AIs are not science, because you get “garbage in, garbage out”; they merely mirror the predilections of the culture that wrote them. (He is exactly right, and that is exactly my point: they are the distilled mind-gestalt of the culture, which is why their testimony about that culture is reliable.) And against my claim that even slapping the couch is an act of faith, he told two stories: 1) The ranger on the nature hike who pointed thirty children to “the red bat” in a tree, had them shout to make it flap — ninety percent saw it move — and then revealed it was a red baseball bat: “I just wanted to see if you’d agree when I said it happened.” 2) The night in the all-night coffee shop when, too tired to study, he kept catching a familiar man across the room looking up whenever he looked up, walked over to greet him, and collided with a mirror. “The certainty broken by new data is fun,” he said. “Even the couch — I give it 99.9999 percent, and I love thinking: but maybe not.”


At that point, we were able to come to convergence. I said: “That likelihood and belief held at high confidence without certainty — *that is what I call faith.”* And Michael answered: “Now you’ve crossed a line that causes me to find easier agreement. If it’s likelihood instead of certainty…” Likelihood is his word; faith is mine; they name the same posture. John’s provisionalism, Michael’s percent-likelihood, and the believer’s faith are one epistemic species. The dispute was never over the structure of our knowing. It is only over what the evidence, honestly weighed, points to.


## II. What Is Real? The Puzzle, the Piece, and the Branch Cut Off


Michael then asked: “What is a really good piece of evidence that God exists?” I said: “I don’t know that there’s any actual *evidence* that God exists. I think it’s all inference. I think it’s all triangulation… Everything I see, everywhere I go, all points back at the same source. That’s the best evidence I have.” God is the unifying concept — physics seeks a theory of everything, and my answer is that the everything *is* God’s: “There is nothing that is not of God, in God, from God, about God.”


Michael offered the jigsaw-puzzle analogy to explain our relationship with God. God is the whole puzzle; each of us is a piece — not the puzzle, but of it, distinguishable but not separate. “My skin is not my border,” he added, “or I’d be dead — I need the oxygen outside my skin on a constant basis.” I accepted the analogy as about as good as an analogy can get. We pressed further into definitions. What is “real?” My concept is that “real is the existence independent of any human observer, God as the Observer upon whom all existence depends, the arbiter of realness; the child a couple intends is not yet real in manifestation but real in commitment, desire, and action, a progressive manifestation whose degree God alone adjudicates.” To which Michael asked, “How are you privy to God’s perspective?” My response: “We cannot be certain of God’s perspective. At some point in everything, faith is required. There is no place in our entire experience of life where faith is not required.”


Then Susan said, yes, everything is in God — but “part of the condition of mankind is that he’s been cut off from God, and that can happen *while still being within God*.” The unbeliever is a piece of the puzzle that has lost the larger picture and does not realize he is an integral and important part of the puzzle. She used the analogy of man’s relationship to God of the branch cut from the tree. “When a tree limb is cut off, it looks just the same as when it was connected, and you might say, oh, it’s still alive. But if it’s cut off, it’s cut off from its life source, and it is in the process of death.” Adam was told *“in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die”* **(Genesis 2:17)** — and he lived 930 years.  But in the sense of the branch cut from the tree, he died *that day*: spiritually, as the cut branch dies — green for a season, dead in fact. “This,” Susan said, “is what we call life: the process of death.” The aches, the fading energy, the heart problems; it is life winding down and death working forward, unless we are grafted again into the tree through Christ.


I see the mechanism that makes the world’s play possible: God can allow parts of His own awareness to be disconnected from His own awareness, even while He remains the whole. Susan framed this structure biblically: God has put all things under the authority of Christ; Christ is in the Father, we are in Christ who is in the Father — the creation gated in its perception through the Son. That is precisely the architecture we have discussed before in the 12 Postulates: the Father created the Son; the Son created the creation; and our access runs through Him. *“No man cometh unto the Father, but by me”* **(John 14:6)** is not a gate slammed against man; it is the description of how the circuit is wired.


From there Susan and I discussed the path of salvation and agreed that the commitment of the heart to Godly action is what counts. The goal is to acheive a “well done, thou good and faithful servant.” We can can reach that even in a life that stumbles — *“the good that I would I do not”* **(Romans 7:19)**, yet *“as far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us”* **(Psalm 103:12)**; then sanctification, Christ leading the committed soul *“in the paths of righteousness”* **(Psalm 23:3)**, little by little, “in chunks that they can manage,” until He can *“present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle”* **(Ephesians 5:27)**. And the warning she attached: the one who says “I’m saved, I don’t need to change anything” has misunderstood the transaction — commitment without willingness to be led is the seed on stony ground (Matthew 13), and the connection gained for a little while can be lost. Yet even then, she testified, God does not let go: He recognizes the decision once made, and He engineers the circumstances — as He did with her — until the wanderer says, “I’m done living like this. I want to try God again.”


## III. Susan’s Three Stars: A Testimony in the Form of a Sextant


I asked: “Where in your own life has truth arrived by triangulation?” Susan came to her answer from preparing for the week’s assignment to explain the Gospel of Jesus Christ. She gave us her own three sightings, the three points by which God fixed her position before she could accept Christ.


**The first star: the hard teaching.** Reading **John 6:53** — *“Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you”* — and watching the disciples walk away, she closed the book annoyed and told God so: Jesus didn’t have to teach it that grossly; people walked away unnecessarily. And in a split second God gave her an understanding which, put into words, was: *“I don’t mind losing shallow followers in an effort to push people into a deeper relationship with me.”* He showed her His perspective, and it made sense — not yet enough to believe, but a line on the chart.


**The second star: the lion and the witch.** Walking through the hall while the children watched *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* — a scene she had seen many times when Aslan negotiates with the witch to die in place of the traitor boy. And the new thought came: *maybe that’s why Christ died — to release us from Satan’s grip.* She had always thought God could simply forgive if He wanted; here was the first live possibility of why a death was required. A second line, crossing the first. (It did not escape the fellowship that Charlie had, days before, urged me to watch *The Most Reluctant Convert* — C.S. Lewis’s own journey from atheism. The Oxford don’s fiction was one of the stars God used to navigate Susan home. Myth, as John insisted, can teach us eternal truths.


**The third star: the prayer about sex.** This one she almost kept private. For years her strongest argument against the atonement was that people *abuse* it — “God will forgive me,” and they go off and sin. Her mantra was the Hippocratic oath: first, do no harm. Christ’s teaching, she reasoned, did harm. Then one day, still not believing in Christ but believing in God, she was praying — thanking God for sex, for its beauty between a man and a woman. And God spoke: *“People abuse sex. Should I have withheld that?”* That made sense to her. She would never say the gift of sex should have been withheld because it is abused; then how could she say the gift of atonement should have been withheld because it could be abused? “A big brick wall… just crumbled, because I realized my own logic was faulty, and I was holding God to a standard I didn’t believe in.”


Three sightings, taken years apart, from utterly different quadrants of her life — a Bible annoyance, a children’s movie, and a prayer of thanksgiving. That was where the lines crossed. There, she was told it was time, and she decided: *I guess I do believe in Christ.* “So there’s your triangulation, in my experience.”


I observed that no one will ever repeat these stories. No apologist would have chosen those three points; no tract contains them. And when I asked how she knew the voice was God and not her own thought or Satan’s — she gave the biblical test — *the fruit* made it obvious (Matthew 7:16). The word led to brightness, growth, harmony; and when she accepted Christ she was filled with a love, peace, and joy beyond anything she had experienced, which she expected to fade and which has not. And when it occasionally withdraws, it functions as God’s summons: “You’ve got my attention. What do you want to say to me?”


Charlie added that even at her most anti-Christ period, she had detailed exchanges with God—spending hours writing her ideas of how people could be good to one another, seeking, with no reference to God, the very answer that turned out to be Him. “God,” Charlie said, “is really loving and patient.”


## IV. Mercy Implies a Sentence: Why the Cross Was Necessary


From Susan’s testimony, I raised the question that has driven my Twelve Postulates: the Muslim names God “the Merciful”—but mercy *for what?* Mercy implies a consequence that was due and foregone, a punishment lawful, appropriate, necessary — and then not executed. What is the necessity of punishment that makes mercy possible? Susan’s answer was her cut-branch theology extended to its roots: the warning to Adam was real; the sentence is separation; and each of us recapitulates Adam personally. She brought a verse I had never once considered in this light: *“Thou hast covered me in my mother’s womb”* **(Psalm 139:13)**. Her reading: Christ covers every infant in the womb — every child begins in relationship with the Father — and each of us, at three or four or five, sinning *knowing it was wrong*, is cut off by our own act, exactly as Adam was. We are not victims of Adam, over whom we had no control; we are, as I put it in the moment, victims of our own seduction. Christ alone remained uncovered by that fate through sinlessness — until, at the cross, He was cut off not for His sin but for ours: *“My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?”* **(Matthew 27:46)**. And the terror we cannot grasp, Susan said, is that to die cut off is to spend eternity without God — and *God is love* **(1 John 4:8)** — an eternity without love, which the unbeliever cannot imagine precisely because, even now, he still enjoys so much of Christ’s light without crediting it.


Later in the morning I gave the fellowship my axiomatic frame: God is the wholeness of everything, desiring fellowship with every part of Himself — “be fruitful and multiply” was a call for children, not rocks — and He made us with real free will, able to be His child or to reject Him. He wants no one lost; but His nature *precludes* embracing injustice, cruelty, and non-love, and so a soul that has chosen that polarity cannot be brought into His presence. It is not that He will not; His essence cannot. He grieves — it is God rejecting a piece of Himself, sadly, wanting it — but His nature will not allow it to be otherwise. Susan added the pedagogical purpose: God maintains the separation partly *for contrast—the principle of contrast from this very essay, polarity as the precondition of perception—so *the returning soul tastes the difference. She cited God sending disobedient Israel to serve a foreign king *“that they may know my service, and the service of the kingdoms of the countries”* **(2 Chronicles 12:8)**. “There’s nothing in life without God that I want. The contrast is too amazing.” And Charlie shared the opening of Isaiah, five times over: *“For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still”* **(Isaiah 9:12, 17, 21; 10:4)**. God is very angry with sin, but He is still reaching out. He desires obedience, not sacrifice; mercy was never Plan B.


## V. Leonard and the illusion of the world


Then Leonard said: “I am going to flip the script.” Where John and Michael doubt the unseen, Leonard doubts the *seen*: “Everything we see in this world is an illusion… It’ll pass away. But we won’t.” What is real is what is eternal — the energy within us, the spirit, given or borrowed from our spiritual parents, “God the Father and his cohort.” We have existed forever; he knew his Debbie before this world; this earth is a school where spirits come to learn, usually by sad experience; and the one way to know any of it is not physical investigation but revelation: “The only way I can find out for sure is if it’s revealed to me by the Source… God can reveal that to me if I desire it, if He feels it’s something I need to know.” The physical search, he said, is a dead-end search; he tries to prioritize spiritual things. He told the story of Christ: the disciples were tossed in the boat and saw a figure walking on the waves, thought it was a ghost! But it was *Him*. “He had control over all these things, *because he knew how they all work*.” (Even the Wachowskis, he offered, were really close in *The Matrix*. Which pill are we going to take?)


I engaged Leonard as I engage everyone — by asking what stands beneath the declarations. When he invoked the conservation of energy as proof that we are eternal, I answered that energy is a secondary concept, a feature of the creation; there was a precursor to energy, and the precursor is consciousness. When he countered, “maybe they coexist — maybe energy *is* consciousness,” I named what had happened: he had made a postulate. And postulates are welcome — my whole physics is built on postulates — but a postulate declared is not a postulate grounded. What is incumbent on the one who declares axioms is to *derive the world from them*, to extrapolate from the initial state to the manifest creation, so the claim connects to something beyond itself. “You’ve stated it as truth, but it’s a belief… it isn’t grounded yet.” Leonard’s answer was, “That’s what I believe. If my belief is errant, then it’s errant. But I believe it’s true. You can’t prove me wrong; I can’t prove it right.” I said triangulation is a way to determine what is true. He replied: “It’s not physical. It’s not of this world; it’s revelation.”


Leonard’s epistemology, his way of knowing, is the question being examined by the essay. Is a fact received by declaration true because it was received from an angel, a prophet, or a feeling of eternity within? This is the form of revelation we have examined in the Snuffer series. Leonard attempted to validate his claim that we are co-eternal with God by quoting Paul, but he misquoted the verse; it actually says we are *“joint-heirs with Christ”* **(Romans 8:17). **We are heirs together with Christ; we do not exist together. Coexistence from eternity is not a biblical concept. Susan identified its actual source — the LDS doctrine of pre-existent intelligences, and behind “the Father and his cohort,” the Mother in Heaven — and she stated her standard: “If God doesn’t see fit to mention a mother in heaven, then I don’t see why I should bring mother in heaven into anything… I just want to stick to what’s in the Bible.” Charlie interpreted the “us” of “let us make man in our image” by John 1:1. That verse tells us who the “Us” was: the Father and the Word. Adding to the word, Charlie warned, would be a mistake; “the word is mysterious enough.”


Leonard’s counter-witness was the polarity to John and Michael at one pole who demand evidence for the unseen; Leonard’s polarity dismisses the seen. I believe the biblical position between them is that what is real. God speaks it, or visualizes it, or as in my Conscious Point Physics (CPP), allows it to arise from interactions which are governed by the law embedded in each Conscious Point. What we perceive as real is the perception of His mind; we, as conscious points, are perceiving the existence of other Conscious Points, and the appearance of the world is the perception of groups of Conscious Points formed into various shapes. Thus, human consciousness is aware of groups, self, other, and qualities of organization. Thus, a foundational postulate of CPP is that mind can perceive mind. Thus, that which is real is that which is the configuration of God’s mind. The God behind all of it is unknown, and the fundamental nature of God’s mind, that which we see in aggregated form indirectly through the senses and view in the screen of the brain, is what is real. There is an “is”, and it is what God sees, what the mind of God is at any Moment. And given the problem of the blind man and the elephant, we may be mistaken, thinking that a small part of reality is the whole of reality. This lack of full perspective, thinking we have the whole picture, may be true with regard to our political, moral, relational, spiritual, and scientific perspectives. Another perspective is that God created a world where seeing only one side is a primary feature of the moral drama of good and evil, the development of character, and the reality of God’s perspective. If, in fact, reality is a patchwork of aggregates, recognized only when named, then the fullness of the reality is illuminated by triangulation. In our naive/natural/limited state, we declare. I encourage triangulation over declaration, and using revelation through conversation is a method to extract reality from the one-dimensional perspectives through which we perceive life in its ordinary state.


But Leonard does not live in a world of abstract postulates about an unseen world. He has a faith, and it helps him frame his life in a way that gives him hope. He lives with, takes care of, and loves Debbie, whose aphasia slowly steals her words, who “sees it in her mind and can’t get it out.” As Leonard said, “She could be healed by Christ if it was in His heart… I don’t think she needs that. I need her to be who she is, because she’s proving how I relate to her, to help her, be with her, and endure this… It really comes down to enduring to the end.” Whatever we make of his cosmology, his cross-bearing is orthodox. By their fruits ye shall know them — and this fruit is good.


## VI. The Canon Question: What May Be Added to the Word?


The fellowship continued the discussion that questions about Leonard’s theology had raised. Is there further revelation? Should there be more books? Do we have any justification for telling Leonard that Denver Snuffer and Joseph Smith’s revelations do not belong on the level of the Bible?


Two scriptures are typically used to rule against giving modern prophecy the status of canon: the “add not unto his words” passages — Deuteronomy 4:2 and Revelation 22:18–19. We examined them, and I argued that the verses refer only to *those books*, to those revelation-events. They are not a padlock on the whole future canon. Using the Bible to prove that nothing may follow the Bible is the very self-validation the essay warned against. The Bible’s actual authority stands on other ground. We researched the history together, live: no council *created* the canon — Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) *recognized* what the churches had already been using for centuries; the four Gospels universal by Irenaeus’s day; Paul’s letters treated as Scripture from the beginning; Athanasius’s festal letter of 367 listing our 27 books exactly.


And the Old Testament: no single moment, no real “Council of Jamnia,” but centuries of usage — Torah fixed by Ezra’s era, the Prophets by 200 BC, the Writings last — a canon *recognized, not invented*, formed by the long testimony of a worshiping people. The criterion that emerged in the early church was proximity to the Lord: apostolic connection, first-person witness, contemporaries of Christ who lived it, heard it, wrote it. That is why I accept Paul and do not accept Denver Snuffer. It is not because a verse forbids new prophets, but because the credentials are different. An angelic visitation proves nothing by itself: an angel might be an angel, or might be “an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14). We don’t know who the angel was. That is not evidence; that is declaration.


Charlie then gave the fellowship his view of Scripture. He glued a new revelation into his Doctrine and Covenants once, as a Mormon. The canon debates, he noted, contain no claim that “an angel appeared and said this one, this one, this one” — just faithful men putting their heads together, rational and spiritual, making their best case, coming to a vote. “We’re doing that today.” Life, he said, is gumbo — everything but the kitchen sink, wildly chancy — and Apollo 13 simulations, problem after problem thrown at you; the Bible is Rashomon, one story of God’s dealings told through many witnesses, “all the errors of perception included,” and the combined effect “so deeply moving and poetic and just beautiful.” Jacob’s sons were wild, even monstrous people — and Jacob, parenting them, “probably got a realization that this is what God goes through every day.” Charlie sees contradictions in Scripture — of outlook, of flavor, Paul and Jesus — and it does not make him curse God; it makes him vigilant: “Ultimately we’re just individuals trying to have a relationship with God… If nothing else, we can always fall back on: love God with all your heart, and your neighbor as yourself. It’s very reliable. Everything else — stay awake.”


I offered my resolution-of-paradox principle as the instrument for those contradictions: when two accounts, head to head, appear contradictory, find the underlying truth both were describing from their different vantages — the resolution of the paradox *is* the triangulated truth. Two witnesses, one reality (2 Corinthians 13:1). And Susan used the  Bereans as her standard of examination: “the Bible says to prove all things” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Thus we have two methods of proving the Bible, which I commend to everyone as this week’s homework: **First, put a biblical concept into practice.** Take a concept — the woman who decided to love her enemy — do it in your real life, and watch what happens. Test it. **Second, triangulate within it.** Set verse beside verse beside verse until the concept emerges that all three were sighting. **The third method:** Christ Himself, incarnate, quoted the Old Testament. Every quotation is the Son of God validating a scripture as worthy. We are not trusting tradition’s telephone; we have the Word’s own citations. (Susan said, the Gospel, once known, unlocks verse after verse of the Old Testament — “oh, *that’s* what that verse is about” — the New hidden in the Old, the Old opened by the New.)


Against all of this stands the cautionary tale Charlie supplied from his friend Jeff, after the family Bible-roulette landed on the warning of aged David: “That’s what happens when you turn the scriptures into a parlor trick.” Proving is not divination. The Bereans *“searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so”* **(Acts 17:11)** — daily, systematically, together.


## VII. Armond Under the Shade Tree: The Theology of Adversity


Armond joined us late from Gary, Indiana — under a shade tree, because that is where the email could reach him. Two weeks ago a derecho — straight-line winds over a hundred miles an hour, a “linear tornado” twenty miles wide — went right over his city, and Gary’s great old trees came down on every block. Thirteen days without power; perhaps a week and a half more; a story that should be national news and isn’t. And from inside it, Armond gave his perspective.


The first was the answer he’d been wrestling toward on the oldest question — why do bad things happen to good people? — “the never-ending variables of life… I came up with a really quick answer, and I don’t know if everybody can live with it: **so that the followers of Christ can be an example of how we should handle adversity.**” Charlie said, “That’s pretty good. It seems too simple — like, why didn’t I think of that?” Susan added: if signing up with Christ guaranteed a rosy life, “everyone would want to do that” — Charlie: “a universal insurance company” — and the profession would prove nothing. Christ promised persecution, and His presence (John 16:33; Matthew 5:11–12); adversity becomes the better test of our loyalties: when the rubber meets the road, who still acknowledges Him as Lord? Armond’s verdict on the pairing: “I don’t think you can have one without the other.”


The second gift was the lived demonstration, delivered as comedy. His wife, scrolling beside him, posted that “nine days without power has been so devastating — keep everybody in your prayers.” Armond leaned over: “Honey, can you turn the light on real quick? I just wanted some light while I read this post about being devastated.” Their generator had run since an hour after the storm; she had just cooked hibachi steak from the working refrigerator; she had never filled the generator and didn’t know how to start it. “You’ve never seen the darkness except when you turn your lights off… We’ve got to be careful with the words we use. God’s watching. How do we say we’ve been devastated when we’ve been so blessed that we’re able to bless others with our abundance?”


The second blessing that Armond shared was when the pitman arm: the steering component on his truck, simply snapped — not on the freeway, where, as Charlie observed, its failure means the steering is gone and you could be dead — but in a driveway, at walking speed, with a friend nearby, the tools available, and the repair done in a couple of hours. “It couldn’t have happened at any better point in time… I read Proverbs every day” — the righteous delivered out of trouble (Proverbs 11:8) — “and I only attribute it to God’s blessings, and Him being true and honest: if we do our part, He’s going to keep His promises.”


Here was Section VII of last week’s essay — the swarm of testimony — adding one more data point in real time, from under a shade tree, while the chainsaws worked down the block clearing the way for the linemen. Armond and his family, meanwhile, have hooked up a generator inlet for one neighbor and are cutting trees so the utility crews can reach everyone’s downed lines: the followers of Christ, showing how to handle adversity.


## VIII. The Death Star Trench: What the Morning Taught About Witnessing


Near the end, Charlie asked me directly: did I feel I got across the main lesson from the conversation with John? My essay was titled “Triangulating Truth,” but the morning revealed that the deeper subject was *witnessing.*


Consider what happened. Susan heard my triangulation concept and immediately produced *her own three points* — and they were points no apologist would ever have chosen: a gross-sounding teaching, a scene in a children’s movie, a prayer of thanksgiving for sex. “Everybody doesn’t go, ‘Wow, I believe now because you triangulated those points of life and felt God was real and we should submit our lives to him! I think I’ll believe too.’” No one would. Likewise, my triangulation points are odd to others, just as hers are odd to me. That is the finding: **everybody has their own triangulation.** I used a Star Wars analogy to illustrate it: the Death Star had one weakness, reachable only down one narrow trench, and the pilot had to fly it exactly. Every soul has its own Death Star trench — the one particular approach by which the message penetrates to the center. John is not resisting because he wants to sin; he doesn’t. He resists because nothing in it is yet *real* to him. He is asking the same question as Michael: “What’s the evidence?” Leonard is seeing something different also. No master argument exists that makes everybody go “shazam.” There is no assembly line to conversion. There is individual witnessing, and God drawing (John 6:44), and each person is unique, and their story is unrepeatable.


Charlie’s closing summary was: “The important thing you’re illustrating is the general principle: you engaged John in deep, heart-to-heart, earnest conversation and we were able to benefit from seeing his perspective… When I do that, people tell me near the end of the conversation, ‘I’ve never told this to anybody’ — and it’s not that I’m special. I listen. I ask questions. I’m interested in people. This reminds me of the conversation Job had with his three friends and with God — that whole book is a conversation, so beautiful and real, all the errors of perception included. We talk in America much less than we used to, in spite of the advancement of the means of communication. In the old days people would sit on the porch and talk and talk. If nothing else, you illustrate the value of conversation. And this thing Susan and I keep talking about — *revelation by conversation* — it can only happen when people are talking.”


Amen. Maybe all of us should have more conversations. Maybe that’s really all it is.


## Conclusion: The Fellowship as Instrument


I told the group near the end that the morning had been very grounding for me — and it was, in the navigational sense. The essay claimed that truth is found where independent lines converge. Then six independent witnesses spent three hours sighting the same reality from six different positions — Michael’s likelihood, Susan’s three stars and her cut branch, Leonard’s counter-witness and the cross, Charlie’s gumbo canon and his Isaiah refrain, Armond’s shade-tree providence, and my triangulation. The lines crossed. Not on an argument. On a Person: the God who covers us in the womb, whose anger is not turned away but whose hand is stretched out still, who prunes and grafts the branches, who is the whole puzzle and grieves for every piece that cannot see the picture, who assigns each of His children their own trench, and who was, I am persuaded, present in the conversation — as Susan prayed at the close: “I feel like your spirit was with us as we spoke.”


Her closing prayer set the week’s assignment: that we might understand something more in the Scriptures; that we might understand better how to apply Christ’s gospel in our lives; that we might come back and share it and edify each other; and that God would help us better explain the gospel of Jesus Christ, for Michael, for John, and for everyone in the world who needs to understand it better. That is the work. Prove all things. Fly your trench. And have more conversations.
