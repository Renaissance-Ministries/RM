# 261116 - Protestantism vs Catholicism - The Authority Each Stands Upon

# One Stool or Three? Authority, Interpretation, and the Place of Mary

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 16 November 2026

## The Image and the Real Question

I stand on the side of Scripture as the final authority for the Christian. I hold that position knowing the Catholic argument against it has real weight, and that Protestantism has its own failures to answer for.

A popular image circulating online shows two stools. The Catholic Church sits on three legs: Magisterium, Sacred Scripture, and Sacred Tradition. Protestantism balances on one: Scripture Alone. The message is plain. The Catholic structure is stable, and the Protestant one is bound to tip over.

The image is clever, and it does point to the question that actually divides the two traditions: who has the final word on what Scripture means? The strongest form of the Catholic argument sits behind it. The Bible did not arrive with its own table of contents. The canon was recognized through the Church over several centuries. So when a Protestant says "Scripture alone," he is already relying on a judgment the Church made. That is a fair challenge, and Protestants owe it a serious answer.

## Where the Image Misleads

The image wins its point by drawing the Protestant position in a form few careful Protestants would recognize.

**It pictures "solo scriptura," not "sola scriptura."** The Reformers never claimed Scripture was the only source of anything. Luther, Calvin, and the English Reformers kept the ancient creeds, read the church fathers, and maintained offices of teaching and governance. Their claim was that Scripture is the *final* authority: it judges every other authority and is judged by none. A truer picture would be a stool with several legs, all of them standing on Scripture as the floor.

**The Catholic stool is not really three equal legs.** In Catholic teaching, the Magisterium alone holds the authority to interpret both Scripture and Tradition authentically. In practice, that makes it a one-legged stool as well. The single leg is simply the teaching office rather than the written Word. The real disagreement is not one leg against three. It is which single authority has the last word.

**The image is borrowed.** The best-known "three-legged stool" in church history is Anglican: Scripture, tradition, and reason, usually credited to Richard Hooker. John Wesley later added experience to form what is called the Wesleyan Quadrilateral. Both were Protestant efforts to keep the very balance this image claims Protestants lack.

## Central Authority and the Problem of Capture

The Catholic Church resembles big government. Its claim runs like this: we hold the unbroken line of interpretation and the hierarchy to govern it, so we understand the truth of Scripture better than the scattered Protestant churches can. The problem with that claim is the same problem every large, centralized institution faces. Groupthink sets in, and the leadership can be captured.

When one office holds the final word and also judges whether it has been faithful, nothing outside it can check it. History shows this happening. The Renaissance papacy and the sale of indulgences were not corrected by the hierarchy. The correction came from outside, through Luther. A Catholic will answer that councils, saints, and reform movements inside the Church have corrected abuses many times. That is partly true, but those reformers worked within a system that set its own terms and could overrule them.

I must be fair about the other side. Decentralization has its own way of failing. Without an authority to settle disputes, churches split, and denominations can be captured too. Several mainline Protestant bodies drifted far from their founding confessions within a few generations, often led by their own seminaries. Protestantism is not immune to groupthink. Its groupthink simply happens in smaller units.

The difference is that a believer can leave a captured Protestant body. That freedom to exit is the real advantage, the same one federalism holds over centralized government. Error stays local. Other congregations can test other readings, and the better ones survive.

## Many Readers, One Revelation

Protestants have tradition, scholars, convocations, and universities. Each examines which way of reading the Bible best reflects the truth God intended to convey. That work is necessary because the Bible is not a linear document. Its words take their meaning from the life of each inspired writer and the revelation he received. Read that way, different emphases and patterns emerge.

This is sound method. Scholars call it grammatical-historical interpretation, and it rests on the Reformation principle that Scripture interprets Scripture. Paul stresses faith, James stresses works, and John stresses love and light. These differences do not threaten the truth. They are facets of one revelation that no single writer contains in full.

The Catholic has a fair reply, and I should state it. Differences in *emphasis* are healthy, but contradictions in *substance* cannot all be true. Either baptism regenerates or it does not. Either the bread becomes the body of Christ or it does not. Open debate is good at testing ideas, but it does not always settle them.

So the Protestant must claim that the essentials are clear enough for an honest reader to find, and that believers may differ on secondary matters without breaking fellowship. The old rule says it well: in essentials, unity; in non-essentials, liberty; in all things, charity.

## Mary and the One Mediator

The Catholic Church teaches believers to pray to Mary. Scripture names only one go-between: *"For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus"* **(1 Timothy 2:5)**. It also tells us that Christ himself is the one who intercedes: *"Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them"* **(Hebrews 7:25)**.

Nowhere in Scripture does a believer pray to a departed saint. And the popular practice in many places looks and feels like worship, whatever the official teaching says: titles such as "Queen of Heaven," a rosary weighted heavily toward Mary, shrines and processions in her honor.

The Catholic has an answer, and an honest critique has to deal with it rather than with a cruder version. The Church distinguishes the worship owed to God alone, called *latria*, from the honor given to Mary, called *hyperdulia*. It says that asking Mary to pray for us is like asking a friend to pray for us, and does not make her a second mediator.

I do not find that answer convincing. Asking a living friend for prayer has clear warrant in Scripture. Addressing prayer to the dead has none. And a distinction that holds in the catechism means little if it does not hold in the hearts of the people kneeling at the shrine. Jesus left no room for divided devotion: *"Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve"* **(Matthew 4:10)**.

## What History Shows About Devotion to Mary

A common Protestant account holds that devotion to Mary was inserted under Constantine to win pagans, giving them a priestess-like figure in place of their female deities. I once leaned toward that account myself. The evidence supports a more careful version of it.


 	- **Devotion to Mary predates Constantine.** Around 180, Irenaeus was already contrasting Mary's obedience with Eve's disobedience. The *Protoevangelium of James*, from the second century, elevated her further. A prayer asking for her protection, the *Sub tuum praesidium*, survives on a papyrus most scholars date to the third century, though that date is debated. The practice grew out of the Church's own piety before the empire took hold of it.

 	- **Constantine did not establish the Holy Roman Empire.** He legalized Christianity in 313 and ruled the Roman Empire. The Holy Roman Empire began with Charlemagne's coronation in 800, nearly five centuries later.

 	- **The "replacement goddess" theory** was popularized mainly by Alexander Hislop's *The Two Babylons* in the 1850s. Historians, Protestant and Catholic alike, now regard that book as unreliable.


What the evidence does support is this. Once Christianity became the religion of the empire, great numbers of half-converted pagans filled the churches. Devotion to Mary grew sharply after the Council of Ephesus in 431 declared her *Theotokos*, "God-bearer." That council met in Ephesus, long the city of the goddess Artemis. Some Christian imagery, such as the mother-and-child icon, may echo older forms like Isis holding Horus. That is borrowed style and cultural pressure, not a deliberate imperial policy of substituting Mary for a goddess.

## Why the Concern Remains Plausible

Correcting the history does not dissolve the concern. It sharpens it.

First, there is the intuitive sense that something is out of proportion. A believer who reads the Gospels finds Mary honored, faithful, and then largely silent after Pentecost. He then walks into a basilica and finds her crowned and enthroned. The distance between those two pictures is real, and the believer's unease is not the product of anti-Catholic prejudice.

Second, institutions have motives. A church that adds intercessors, shrines, and pilgrimages also adds places where devotion, and often money, collects. The sale of indulgences shows that the Church was not above turning the economy of grace into revenue.

Third, there is a link between doctrine, system, and behavior. When a single office defines doctrine and no outside authority can correct it, a popular practice that the hierarchy finds useful can harden into dogma. The Immaculate Conception was defined in 1854 and the Assumption in 1950, more than eighteen centuries after the apostles. Neither has clear support in Scripture. That pattern is exactly what the problem of capture would predict.

And the mass conversions after Constantine did bring pagan habits of devotion into the Church. Even if no emperor planned a substitution, a people accustomed to praying to a heavenly mother would have found Mary an easy place to transfer that habit.

## My Stand

I stand with Scripture as the final authority, and I hold that position open to a better argument. The three-legged stool is a good conversation starter but a poor argument. Both traditions finally rest on one authority. The real question is whether that authority should be a fixed, written Word that every believer may read for himself, or a living teaching office that claims protection from error.

Each side has a hard question to answer. The Protestant must explain how the canon was recognized, and why those who read the same Bible disagree so widely. The Catholic must explain what corrects the Magisterium when its teaching drifts from the Scripture it claims to serve.

On Mary, my position is firm. Scripture gives no warrant for praying to her, and over the centuries popular devotion turned honor into something that functions like worship. The flood of pagan converts after Constantine likely sped that along. That argument stands on the record as it is. It needs no legend about an emperor installing a goddess, and it is stronger without one.

## Sources and Their Limits

The historical claims here rest on standard works of church history and on primary texts:


 	- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* (c. 180), on Mary as the new Eve

 	- The *Protoevangelium of James* (2nd century)

 	- The *Sub tuum praesidium* papyrus (John Rylands Library, Manchester), usually dated to the 3rd century; some scholars place it later

 	- The Council of Ephesus (431) and its title *Theotokos*

 	- Alexander Hislop, *The Two Babylons* (1853–1858), and later critiques of it

 	- The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* on *latria*, *hyperdulia*, and the intercession of saints

 	- Richard Hooker, *Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity* (1590s)


This sourcing leans on the mainstream scholarly record, which Catholic and Protestant institutions have curated for centuries. Each has had reasons to shape the account of early devotion to suit its own claims. Dates for early papyri and texts are estimates and remain disputed. The case for pagan influence on popular piety is drawn more from patterns than from documents, since such influence rarely leaves a written trail. Firsthand accounts of how Marian devotion is practiced today, which vary widely by region, are better treated as leads than as proof.
