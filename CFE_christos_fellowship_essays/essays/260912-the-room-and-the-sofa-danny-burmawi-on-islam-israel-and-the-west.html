The Room and the Sofa: Confronting Every Idea in Danny Burmawi's PragerU Interview on Islam, Israel, and the West

By: Thomas Lee Abshier, ND

Date: September 12, 2026

Fellowship Discussion Essay | 12 September 2026

For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father. — Romans 8:15

Occasion. On July 7, 2026, PragerU published Episode 39 of The Jeremy Boreing Show, a long conversation between Boreing and Danny Burmawi under the title "A Former Muslim Exposes Lies About Islam." Burmawi was born in Jordan, lived his first nineteen years as a Muslim, spent a year as a zealous defender of the faith, left it after reading its own sources, became a Christian, lived fifteen years in Lebanon, and now leads the Ideological Defense Institute and has written Islam, Israel and the West: A Former Muslim's Analysis. The interview is worth your ninety minutes. It is one of the most compact and best-structured statements I have heard of the case that Islam is not a religion among religions but a civilizational order that contains a religion — and it makes that case from the inside, by a man who paid for his knowledge with his country. You can watch it at https://www.prageru.com/videos/a-former-muslim-exposes-lies-about-islam-or-ep-39, and I ask you to, because what follows is not a substitute for it.

My charge to myself in this essay is unusual and I state it plainly: I intend to confront every idea in the recording. Not summarize it — the interview is Burmawi's and PragerU's, and I will paraphrase rather than reproduce, quoting him once. But I will not skip the ideas I find inconvenient, and I will not skip the ones where I think he is wrong, and I will not skip the ones where he is right in a way that indicts the Western church. Where the interview touches current events and public controversy — Tucker Carlson, Zohran Mamdani, Canadian Islamophobia legislation, Israel — I follow the discipline this fellowship calls the Issachar Form, after the men of Issachar in David's muster roll who had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do (1 Chronicles 12:32): sort the facts, argue the questions to a stated judgment, and name something concrete to do about it — because an essay that ends at understanding is not finished. Where the interview touches doctrine — grace, sanctification, the sanctification of Muhammad, the nature of the Islamic Jesus, dispensationalism and its critics — I will do what a fellowship essay does: descend from the practical question to the theological one, then to the ontological one, and come back up with what it means for the neighbor.

I take the ideas in an order that is mine, not the interview's.

I. Triage: What the Interview Asserts and What We May Assert After It

Before arguing with a man, sort what he said: what is publicly verifiable, what is informed interpretation, and what rests on a single or contested source. A man speaking for ninety minutes without notes will say some things loosely, and Proverbs 18:17 tells us what to do about it: He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him. I have searched. The ninth commandment governs what this essay asserts as fact, and I have found, as I usually find, that the precise version of Burmawi's claims is more damning than the loose version. This section is hygiene, not the spine.

Tier one — public and verifiable. The Islamic conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries took the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, and Spain from a Christian world; Aramaic, the language of Jesus, gave way to Arabic across the region over the following centuries; Jerash, Burmawi's home city, was a city of the Decapolis and is today a ruin visited by tourists. The Ottoman devşirme — the levy of Christian boys for conversion and service — is documented history. Hagia Sophia in Istanbul was a church for nine centuries, a mosque for five, a museum for eighty-six years, and is a mosque again since 2020, its Christian mosaics curtained during prayers. Jordanian shari'a courts have, in documented cases, treated conversion from Islam as apostasy with civil consequences: marriage annulled, custody lost, inheritance forfeited. The statue of Christ in Fuheis, Jordan's Christian-majority town, was erected by the local youth club in January 2023 and removed by the municipality the next day after a social-media outcry, and relocated to a Christian cemetery; the King visited the town weeks later to affirm coexistence. Zohran Mamdani is mayor of New York; his father Mahmood Mamdani wrote Good Muslim, Bad Muslim (2004). John Calvin approved the execution of Servetus in 1553. The Aisha tradition Burmawi cites is in Sahih al-Bukhari, and it says what he says it says: she remarked that his Lord seemed to hasten to fulfill his wishes. The hadith about expelling Jews and Christians from the Arabian Peninsula is in Sahih Muslim. The account of Jesus asking a companion to be substituted for him at the crucifixion is in the classical commentaries on Qur'an 4:157 — al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir — not in the Qur'an itself, which says only that "it was made to appear so to them." Increase Mather's The Mystery of Israel's Salvation (1669) argued for a future national conversion of the Jews, two centuries before Darby.

Tier two — informed interpretation. The reading of Qur'an 9:29 as an unbounded command, the three-stage pattern of Islamic expansion, the claim that Islamic soteriology is structurally fear-based, the argument that Islam is a total order rather than a religion, the analysis of the red-green alliance, the characterization of Mamdani's commitments, the assessment that the West's post-9/11 accommodation accelerated Islamic growth. These are arguments, and I will argue them.

Tier three — held loosely. Four items need correction or caution.

First, Burmawi paraphrases 9:29 as a command to kill unbelievers until they pay the jizya. The verb in 9:29 is qātilū — fight — not uqtulū — kill; the verse reads, in the standard translations, fight those who do not believe in Allah and the Last Day, and do not hold as forbidden what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, and do not acknowledge the religion of truth, from among the People of the Book, until they pay the jizya with willing submission and feel themselves subdued. The verse that says kill is 9:5, and it is addressed to the polytheists, not the People of the Book. This is not a correction against him; it is a correction in his favor. A command to make war on Christians and Jews until they submit to a humiliating tax is the more accurate reading, and it is the reading that has governed Islamic jurisprudence on the dhimma for thirteen centuries. Precision does not soften the point. It hardens it.

Second, the cat-and-dog example is garbled. The traditions in Bukhari say that a woman was punished for confining a cat until it starved, and that a prostitute was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog; a separate tradition says that keeping a dog other than for hunting or guarding diminishes one's reward daily. Burmawi has the animals reversed and the penalties inflated. More important, the actual traditions do not support his point that Islamic merit is random and chaotic — they read as ordinary moral teaching about cruelty and mercy toward animals. His larger argument about the absence of any clear trajectory to assurance except martyrdom stands on other ground, and I will stand it there. This example should be retired.

Third, the casualty figures — eighty-six million dead in India, a possible total of three hundred eighty million across the history of Islamic conquest — are from a contested literature (K. S. Lal's demographic estimates for India; Bill Warner's aggregate). They are not fabrications; they are upper-bound reconstructions from thin sources. The fellowship's rule is that we do not build on figures our own standards would reject if offered against us. The conquest was vast and murderous by any accounting. Say that. Do not say a number you cannot defend.

Fourth, the Qatar-owned fatwa website story — a Muslim asking whether he may submit to sodomy to accommodate a bomb — is widely circulated and, so far as I can determine, not verifiable to its supposed source. I decline to repeat it as fact. Burmawi himself says the question, not the answer, is what matters; I say that a question we cannot prove was asked cannot bear any weight at all. The reports that Tucker Carlson has bought a home in Qatar I likewise leave in the third tier: reported, not confirmed by me. Nothing in this essay depends on either.

Two items I place between tiers. Burmawi says sixty-five thousand Islamic terror attacks in recent decades; the database our own framework cites (through Dr. Gad Saad) counted about forty-nine thousand since September 2001. Both numbers say the same thing at the order of magnitude that matters. And the anecdote in which Pascal, asked by the king for evidence of God, answers that the Jews are the evidence: the anecdote is apocryphal in that form (it circulates attached to several names), but the substance is authentically Pascal — the Pensées devote extended sections to the survival of the Jewish people as a standing proof of Providence. The IDI essay Burmawi commends should be read as a meditation on Pascal's actual argument, not as a transcript of a conversation that did not happen.

With the ground cleared, I come to the ideas.

II. Fourteen Centuries and the Curtained Mosaics

Burmawi opens with history because Tucker Carlson and his guests have, he says, treated the phrase Islamic jihad as a manufactured narrative — something invented by people with an agenda — and he answers with fourteen centuries. Two-thirds of the ancient Christian world occupied. Languages replaced. Civilizations reduced to ruins that only tourists visit. The dhimma. The devşirme. Churches turned into mosques, of which Hagia Sophia is the last great one, where he stood under the ceiling and saw the Virgin and the apostles curtained off because one cannot pray in a mosque with Christian icons visible, yet they cannot be destroyed because the world's heritage authorities forbid it — so the past is there, erased, under curtains, and the present is Islam praying beneath it.

I want the fellowship to sit with that image, because it is the most important thing in the interview and Burmawi does not fully unpack it. The mosaics are under curtains, not chiseled off. That is the exact condition of the West today. Our Christian past is not destroyed; it is covered. The law, the universities, the hospitals, the very idea that a stranger has rights, the imago Dei that Burmawi correctly names as the foundation of the human-rights declarations that even non-Christian nations sign — all of it is still on the ceiling. We simply do not look up. And a different worship has moved into the room beneath it.

There is a scripture that belongs here, and it is not a comfortable one. The seven churches of Revelation 2 and 3 were in what is now Turkey. Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamos, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea — every one of them is today in a Muslim-majority nation, and the Lord's word to the first of them was: Remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works; or else I will come unto thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his place, except thou repent (Revelation 2:5). The candlesticks of Asia Minor were removed. Not by Islam's strength alone; Islam was the instrument. They were removed because the churches did not do the first works. Burmawi's history lesson is true, and the Christian reading of it is harder than the civilizational one: conquest does not happen to a church that is on fire. It happens to a church that has become a museum of its own past — mosaics on the ceiling, curtains beneath.

III. The Revelation of Convenience

The center of Burmawi's testimony is not emotional; it is textual, and it is the strongest apologetic move in the interview. He became devout in 2006 to defend Muhammad against the Danish cartoons. He describes the propaganda he was raised on — that an astronaut heard the call to prayer on the moon, that a Palestinian invented the computer and the Americans killed him for it — and the sermon by a Kuwaiti imam that moved him to tears, recounting how the Companions tore a poetess in half between two camels for mocking the Prophet. He read the Qur'an, the Sira, and the Hadith not to test Islam but to master it. And what he found was that whenever Muhammad needed something, Gabriel arrived with a revelation to provide it — a settlement of a quarrel with a wife, permission to marry his adopted son's former wife by the expedient of abolishing adoption. And he found that Aisha had noticed it first, and said so, and that her remark is preserved in the most authoritative collection Islam possesses.

Here is where I want to descend a level, because the argument is stronger than Burmawi makes it, and Scripture is the reason.

The Torah gives a test for prophets: When a prophet speaketh in the name of the LORD, if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken, but the prophet hath spoken it presumptuously: thou shalt not be afraid of him (Deuteronomy 18:22). That is the falsification test, and I have written elsewhere that it is Popper in the Pentateuch. But the biblical record supplies a second and subtler test, never stated as a rule and everywhere present as a pattern: the genuine revelation of God rebukes the man who receives it. Nathan walks into David's court and says Thou art the man (2 Samuel 12:7), and the king who could have had him killed writes Psalm 51 instead. Peter, the rock, denies the Lord three times, and the Gospels his own church preserved record every denial. Paul writes that when Peter was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed (Galatians 2:11), and the church canonized the letter. Moses is barred from the land. Jonah sulks. The prophets are, almost without exception, men whom the Word crushes before it uses. A revelation that arrives only to gratify its recipient, that never once tells him he is wrong, that adjusts the moral law of the universe to resolve his domestic difficulties — that is not the signature of the God of Israel. It is the signature of a man.

Burmawi's friend saw it, and Burmawi saw it, and Aisha saw it fourteen centuries ago. The test does not require hostility to Islam. It requires only reading Islam's own books with the question the Torah taught the world to ask.

IV. The Judge, the Shahada, and What a Religious Test Is

Burmawi tells of his Jordanian friend who converted, was sued by his father for apostasy, was jailed, lost his wife and children and civil standing, and was offered a way out by a humane judge: recite the shahada, say the words, and everything is restored; the state does not care about your heart. The friend, broken, said the words. Years later his whole household became Christian. Burmawi's verdict on the judge is exact: a decent man in spite of Islam, not because of it.

I want to name what that courtroom is, in the vocabulary this fellowship has developed. It is a religious test — the thing Article VI, Clause 3 of our Constitution forbids — enacted as the ordinary operation of law. In the April 18 essay on the Kingdom of God and the kingdoms of men (https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/04/18/the-kingdom-of-god-and-kingdoms-of-men/), I distinguished the religious test the Founders prohibited — a legal requirement that you profess something to hold office or civil standing — from testimony, the voluntary declaration of allegiance that the free-exercise clause protects and that Christ commands. The Jordanian court is the religious test in its purest form: say the creed or lose your wife. And the whole Western tradition of liberty of conscience, which the secular world imagines it invented, is in fact the Christian recognition that a creed recited to keep one's children is not faith and cannot be, that choose you this day whom ye will serve (Joshua 24:15) presupposes the liberty to choose otherwise.

I add a pastoral word, because the fellowship will ask it. Did the friend deny Christ? Christ said, whosoever shall deny me before men, him will I also deny before my Father which is in heaven (Matthew 10:33). But the man who first heard those words was Peter, and Peter denied Him, and was restored on a beach at breakfast. The word of Matthew 10 is a summons to courage, not a sentence on the coerced. The Jordanian convert did not deny Christ; he was robbed, and the robber's demand was a sentence in Arabic. The friend's household is Christian today. I am not the one who will tell him he failed.

V. The Room Is Not the Sofa

Now the thesis for which the interview should be remembered. Boreing raises the obvious objection to Burmawi's demand for a State Department program to examine Islamic theology: America has freedom of religion; the state does not critique theologies. And Burmawi answers with the sentence I choose as the one quotation I will take from him: "Islam has a religion." A room has a sofa; the room is not the sofa. A house has a kitchen; the house is not a kitchen. Islam contains prayer, fasting, alms, pilgrimage, creed — a religion — inside a legal, political, military, and civilizational program whose stated end is the rule of Allah's law over all mankind. Call the whole thing a religion, and you have used the word to smuggle the program past the constitutional gate on the sofa's ticket.

This is the etymological pause the argument deserves. Religio — Cicero's derivation is relegere, to read again, to be scrupulous; Lactantius's is religare, to bind fast. Either way the word names a relation between the soul and the divine and the practices that express it. Our First Amendment protects the free exercise of that. It does not protect a program to replace the Constitution, because a program to replace the Constitution is not a relation between a soul and God; it is a relation between a party and a state. Burmawi's communism example is exactly right: if the Party declared Marx a prophet and the Manifesto holy writ, no serious person would grant Marxism-Leninism free-exercise protection for the seizure of power.

Here is the definitional precision the moment demands, because both "religion" and "Islamophobia" are being made to carry loads they cannot bear.

Free exercise of religion means the state may not forbid a man to pray, to fast, to teach his children his creed, to build a mosque with his own money, to wear what his faith prescribes, to change his religion, or to try peacefully to persuade others to change theirs.

It does not mean that a legal system in which apostates lose their children is a protected religious practice.

It does not mean that an organized effort to substitute the law of a god for the law of the land is a protected religious practice.

It does not mean that a demand to punish criticism of a prophet is a protected religious practice.

It does not mean that the state must pretend not to know what a movement's own texts say its ends are.

And "Islamophobia" — a word whose form, as Burmawi shrewdly observes, was chosen to attach the ideology to the persons, so that criticism of the one becomes hatred of the other — does not mean anything at all until we say which of two things we are talking about. Contempt for Muslims as persons is sin; it is a violation of the second great commandment, and I will say so as strongly as any imam. Criticism of Islam as a system of ideas is the ordinary exercise of the mind God gave us, and it is also, in this country, protected speech.

Where I part from Burmawi is on the instrument. He asks where the State Department's program to combat Islamic theology is. I do not want one. A government that is competent to adjudicate theology is a government that will, on some later Tuesday, adjudicate mine. The theological framework this fellowship works under is explicit that the church has the responsibility to teach the truth about Islam, to equip believers, and to welcome converts; the state's job is narrower and it is the job Burmawi's argument actually establishes: to distinguish free exercise from sedition, to refuse blasphemy law in every costume, to screen immigration for allegiance to the program rather than for the practice of the religion, and to protect the apostate from the family and the court that would strip him. That is the constitutional argument done at the level of what the text actually prohibits rather than at the level of talking points — what this fellowship's working notes call the second of four levels an essay should descend through. The state may not test your religion. It may, and must, test your allegiance to the Constitution, and a man whose religion tells him the Constitution is a man-made law to be replaced has told you his answer. The room is not the sofa. Neither is the Bill of Rights a suicide pact.

VI. Moderates, Reformers, and the Source

Boreing puts the decent man's objection well: it was not our Muslim neighbors who flew the planes; it is uniquely Christian not to blame the group for the individual. Burmawi's answer is one of the sharpest in the interview and I want to give it its due before I add to it. He says: absolutely, do not blame the Muslim for the terrorist — but understand that ideas have consequences, and if you deny that (as Marxism did, teaching that conditions make ideas rather than ideas making outcomes), you will misdiagnose the problem and react wrongly, whether left or right. Muslims are not the problem; Islam is. And the moderate who condemns terrorism while forbidding you to examine the text that produced the terrorist is not a neutral party; he is, whatever his intentions, the shield the source hides behind. The reformer who says the peaceful Islam is the true Islam is telling the West that the problem is a few individuals, and so the West spends its trillions killing individuals while the source goes on producing them.

Boreing's principle is biblical, and I want the verse said aloud: The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father (Ezekiel 18:20). Collective guilt is pagan. But Ezekiel is speaking of guilt, not of diagnosis, and Burmawi's distinction holds both: the neighbor is innocent of the bomb, and the book is not.

Let me steelman the moderate Muslim, because the fellowship's rule is that we strike only what we have first stated at full strength. He says: You are asking me to denounce my own scripture in public in order to earn my place in your country. You do not ask that of the Catholic whose church ran the Inquisition. I do not read 9:29 as a standing command; I read it in its seventh-century context, as most of the Muslims I know do. Why is my reading not Islam?

The answer is the one this fellowship reached in the essay on the Oxford debate (the Daniel Johnson engagement): followers lead, leaders follow. The mass of Muslims determines which Islam prevails in any place and time, and in that sense the moderate's reading is an Islam. But it is an Islam that the texts do not support and the tradition does not recognize, and the reformer's honesty requires him to say so — to say, as Burmawi's friend Zuhdi Jasser does not say, that he is reforming Islam rather than recovering it. Burmawi's request to the moderate is perfectly fair and it is the request I would make: if you read it in context, you are not the problem; then step aside and let me deal with the reading that is. The moderate who answers that request by crying Islamophobe has told you which side of the room he stands on.

And now to the distinction that Burmawi rightly calls the most dangerous thing in Islam, and that Boreing restates with real precision: the sanctification of the actions of Muhammad and their transfer into every century. Burmawi, a Presbyterian, says of Calvin's approval of Servetus's burning: he did it, I will not justify it, I will explain the age, and I stand against killing men for their beliefs. He can say that because Calvin is not the standard. In Islam, Muhammad is the standard — al-insān al-kāmil, the perfect man, the pattern for all conduct — and so the caravan raids, the assassinations of poets, the massacre of the Banu Qurayza, the marriage to a child, cannot be wrong, and so must be justified, or contextualized, or blamed on Jewish forgers of the hadith. This is the distinction between a faith's standard and its execution — between what it teaches and what its adherents have done. Christianity's standard is Christ, who conquered nothing, killed no one, and told Peter to put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword (Matthew 26:52). Christianity's execution — the Crusades, the Inquisition, Servetus, the wars of religion — can be condemned by Christians from inside Christianity, by the standard, without a single verse being abandoned. Islam's standard is its execution. There is no higher court. That structural fact, not the body count, is why Burmawi is right that the two systems are not the same kind of thing, and why every Muslim who is a good man is a good man by a standard his own tradition did not give him — the same law written on the heart that Paul says the Gentiles have by nature (Romans 2:14-15), and that Burmawi's Jordanian judge obeyed against his own statute.

VII. Sola Jihad: What Each System Does with the Debt

Boreing asks the question that matters most: is there grace in Islam? Burmawi says no, and then makes the argument that I regard as the theological core of the interview. Islam, he says, is by design a system in which you cannot keep the commandments — ten thousand of them, with hellfire threatened on every page (he issues a challenge: open the Qur'an at random) — so that the one thing that can save you directly is the one thing the text names: die fighting in the way of Allah. He calls it sola jihad, by deliberate analogy to sola fide. Everything else is weighed; martyrdom alone is assured. And the result, he says, is not mainly terrorism, which can be policed, but a people who live under a crushing theological guilt, terrified in prayer, incapable of the confidence that builds. He remembers weeping over the Qur'an and being afraid.

Here the method requires the move I think most Christian writers miss — comparing two systems by their architecture rather than by their doctrinal checklists, which means asking what each one actually does with the moral debt. Islam hopes the scale tips, and offers one certain thumb on the scale, which is death in battle. Judaism — and Burmawi is careful and right here, correcting a common Christian slander — is a covenant in which the law follows the rescue: God brought Israel out of Egypt and then gave the commandments; obedience is the response of the redeemed, not the price of redemption. Christianity is the same architecture brought to its end: the debt is paid, in full, by God, at His own cost, and the life that follows is response, not payment. Burmawi's phrase for this is that the gospel is news, and one's reaction to news does not change the news. That is why he refuses to tell his conversion story in churches: a dramatic testimony teaches people that the gospel is the transformation, and then a man whose transformation is undramatic thinks he has no gospel. I have rarely heard the point made better. The gospel is what God did. Our story is the ripple, not the stone.

I agree with all of that, and I want to push on one thing, because the push matters for the fellowship's doctrine. Burmawi tells the parable of the man who swore to sell his runaway camel for two dinars, and whose wife told him to tie the camel to a cat and sell the cat for two hundred, one not available without the other; he says progressive sanctification is the cat tied to the camel of free grace — a back door through which performance reenters and privileges are lost for not performing. I understand the abuse he is describing and I have seen it: the preacher who tells the sinner not to come to the table until he has fixed himself, when the only qualification for the table is to know that one cannot. On that, Burmawi is right and Boreing's amen is mine. But I do not think sanctification is the cat. Paul says in one breath that we are saved by grace... through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast, and in the next breath that we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them (Ephesians 2:8-10). The works are not tied to the grace as a price; they grow from it as fruit grows from a tree. This fellowship's whole theology of the Kingdom is that God's method is transformation — the heart changed, and the changed heart producing a changed life and a changed culture — as against Islam's method of coercion. If sanctification were a cat tied to a camel, the Kingdom would have no way to arrive. It arrives because grace is not inert. The man at the table is unworthy, and Paul's instruction is still let a man examine himself, and so let him eat (1 Corinthians 11:28) — not to earn the bread, but because a man who will not look at himself cannot receive it. Examination is not performance. It is honesty, and honesty is the beginning of the very transformation Burmawi fears will become a toll booth.

Now the ontological floor, because this is where the argument stops being a comparison of preferences. Burmawi says the relationship with God in Islam is a horror show. The Apostle John says: There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love (1 John 4:18). Why should we believe John rather than the Qur'an? Not because we prefer him. Because the nature of reality is at stake. If — as this fellowship holds, and as the Conscious Point Physics argues from the ground up — reality itself is constituted by the consciousness of God, and God is love, then a system whose central emotion toward God is terror is a system misaligned with the substance of existence. Its fruits will show the misalignment, and Burmawi describes the fruits: guilt that crushes, conformity that forbids uniqueness, a people who cannot scale or build. A system aligned with the nature of things produces what he also describes: the high-trust society, the confidence that comes from knowing one's standing is settled, the freedom to fail and stand again. He notes that some attribute the West's high trust to the Catholic ban on cousin marriage and the nuclear family it produced, and says he believes it is rather the spirit of the gospel. It is both, and the second explains the first: a church that believed each soul stood alone before God, unrepresented by clan, was the church that dismantled the clan. Structure follows theology. Theology, if it is true, follows reality.

VIII. The Red-Green Alliance and the Mayor of New York

Boreing asks how a movement of secular, postmodern, sexually revolutionary progressives makes common cause with a religion that executes homosexuals. Burmawi's answer is a compact history of the left: Marxism promised the poor a revolution; in America the poor became comfortable and declined to revolt; the neo-Marxists substituted identity for class — race, sex, orientation as the new proletariat — and Islam, being non-Western, non-white, once colonized, and anti-Western by nature, checks every box. The two agree on nothing but the enemy, and they have agreed to take down the system first and settle who gets the ruins later. Boreing's analogy is Stalin, Churchill, and Roosevelt against Hitler. Burmawi says exactly.

This fellowship has a discipline for claims of coordination, and I apply it. Every pattern of aligned action is examined on two tracks — as possibly an intentional conspiracy, and as possibly a de facto one — aligned interests producing convergent behavior without anyone coordinating — and that it often does not matter which track is operative, because the Kingdom's response is the same. The red-green alliance is a de facto conspiracy in exactly this sense. No one needs to have signed anything. Two movements that both require the dethroning of the biblical God will find themselves marching in the same direction, and Paul has already told us who is at the head of the column: For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places (Ephesians 6:12). The alliance is not strange once you see that both partners serve the same principal, and that the principal does not care which of his servants inherits the wreckage.

On Mamdani, Burmawi reasons carefully and I follow him carefully. If the mayor were a communist who merely identifies as Muslim for votes, Burmawi argues, he would push the Marxist agenda and neglect the Islamic one; but his defense of Hamas, his hostility to Israel, and his appointments say otherwise, and his daily bones thrown to the progressive coalition are the price of their letting him fight his own fight. The Issachar discipline permits me to deliberate on this without asserting as fact what I cannot verify. I state my judgment conditionally and at full moral strength: if the mayor's public conduct is as described — and the public record on Hamas and Israel is not in dispute — then it is reasonable to read him as sincerely both, a real Muslim and a real socialist, and the more important reading is the one Burmawi implies rather than states: the sincerity does not matter to the outcome. A de facto alliance does not need its members to be insincere. It needs them to be pointed the same way. The Kingdom response is the same on either reading: pray for the man by name, oppose the program by name, and do not confuse the two.

IX. Islamophobia Is Not Antisemitism, and Ideology Is Not Identity

Boreing raises the objection from the dissident right: there are special protections for Jews — antisemitism is singled out — so why not the same for Muslims? Burmawi's answer has three parts and each is correct. First, Islam is not a race, and the attempt to make it one is the door through which blasphemy law walks in. Second, no one has ever called it antisemitic to criticize the Hebrew Bible, the God of the Old Testament, or the Talmud; you may say what you like about the ideas. Antisemitism is proven by its fruits — the pogroms, the expulsions, the Holocaust — and it is hatred of a people, not of a doctrine. Third, therefore, the two words are inverted: antisemitism is hatred of Jews, not criticism of Judaism; Islamophobia as its advocates deploy it is criticism of Islam, not hatred of Muslims. Boreing states the inversion crisply, and Burmawi adds the George Carlin observation, which I paraphrase: when your ideology is your identity, every criticism of the idea is an assault on the self — and that, he says, is precisely the condition of the Muslim, whose ontology is Islam, so that Islamophobia is, from inside, honestly experienced as an attack on the person.

I want to descend beneath this, because it is where the gospel answers the whole problem and Burmawi, who knows it, does not quite say it. The Christian's identity is not his ideology. It is Christ — I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me (Galatians 2:20). Because the Christian's standing is settled by a Person and not by a program, he can hear his church's history criticized, his Reformers' crimes named, his own denomination's errors exposed, and lose nothing. Boreing says it in passing about Christ: He does not need us to defend Him like a big brother; He has overcome the world. That is the exact difference. A man whose worth is secured by grace can afford to be criticized. A man whose worth is secured by adherence cannot. The fury of the blasphemy laws is the fury of a system in which the self has nothing under it but the creed. And the way out is not to demand that Muslims toughen up. It is to offer them the ground the Christian stands on — the adoption, the Abba, Father — so that criticism of an idea is once again only criticism of an idea.

Two judgments follow and I state them. Islamophobia laws — Burmawi cites a Canadian demand for forty million dollars and curriculum mandates, which I have not verified but which fits a documented pattern — are blasphemy laws by another door, and the church must oppose every one of them, in Canada, in Britain, in the American states, on First Amendment grounds and on the deeper ground that a faith which cannot bear examination has confessed something about itself. And the Christian who says that Jews are protected and Muslims are not has confused a protection of persons, earned in the ashes of the twentieth century, with a demand for the protection of a program. The confusion is not innocent. Whoever taught it to him wanted it taught.

X. The Dissident Right, the Weaponized Christ, and the Islamic Jesus

Now the part of the interview for which it was made, and the part that the fellowship most needs to hear because the error it addresses is spreading among people who call themselves our brothers.

Boreing describes the dissident right's new line: Christians and Muslims share a common enemy in the Jews and in Israel; Muslims at least honor Jesus as a prophet, which Jews do not; the Gulf states are kinder to Christians than Israel is; and Christ is King has become the banner of the argument. Burmawi answers each. Let me take them in order and add what this fellowship has to add.

On influence. Burmawi asks the right question: what is the difference between Jews seeking influence in America and Muslims seeking it? Jewish influence — he names AIPAC and says its aim is stated on its website: keep Congress pro-Israel — is above the table, and the worst outcome anyone can name is more aid to Israel, which Netanyahu has lately said Israel means to wean itself from. Islamic influence, by its own thousands of recorded statements, aims to replace the Constitution with the rule of Allah. Boreing adds what Christians often miss: Judaism does not want you; it is not an evangelistic religion and makes conversion difficult; there is no vision of a Jewish America. Christianity is evangelistic — we want you to know Christ. Islam is conquering — it wants you to convert or submit. Three religions, three postures toward the neighbor's soul, and only one of them is a threat to the neighbor's country. I have nothing to add except that the argument is decisive and the men who evade it know it.

On the Islamic Jesus. This is the argument I most want the fellowship to be able to make, because it is the one the Christ is King crowd cannot answer. The claim is that Muslims honor Jesus and Jews do not, so Muslims are nearer to us. Burmawi's answer: the Jesus of Islam is a different person. He is not divine; that Christians and Jews already disagree about, and Burmawi (rightly, in this context) sets it aside as a matter for the next life. What he will not set aside is that the Islamic Jesus is a coward — in the classical commentaries on Qur'an 4:157, Jesus, warned that the soldiers are coming, asks his companions which of them will take on his likeness and die in his place, and promises the volunteer Paradise. Burmawi notes the currency: Muhammad bought loyalty with the same promise — kill this man, and you will have rivers in Paradise. That is not the Jesus who set his face like a flint toward Jerusalem (Isaiah 50:7; Luke 9:51) and said no man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself (John 10:18). It is a Jesus recast as a Muhammad — and that is the whole method of Islam toward the Bible: Abraham was a Muslim who built the Kaaba, Moses was a Muslim, Jesus was a Muslim, every prophet is conscripted to testify for the last one. Meanwhile the Jew, as Burmawi says with real generosity, is at least not a hypocrite: he says plainly that he does not believe Jesus is the Messiah, because the Messiah he awaits will restore Israel and rebuild the Temple — which is to say, the Jew is waiting for the Second Coming and does not know it. Islam praises a Jesus who does not exist in order to deny the One who does. The Qur'an's respect is not a bridge. It is a substitution — the same substitution it makes on the cross.

On spitting. Burmawi handles the viral videos of Orthodox Jews spitting on Christian missionaries in Jerusalem with the only thing that can handle them, which is proportion. There were, by the report he cites, about sixty incidents of harassment in 2025. He has been spat on in Times Square for preaching the gospel. Those same Jews spit on secular Jews. And the comparison being drawn is between sixty spittings in the one Middle Eastern country where a Christian may preach at the Western Wall and go home safe, and fourteen centuries of dhimma, enslavement, and slaughter, continuing this week in Nigeria and Syria. He says — and I feel the weight of it — that he wishes the Muslims of Jordan would spit on him, if only they would let him come home to visit his family. Israel is the only country in the Middle East where the Christian population is growing. Christians across the Islamic world would move there if they could. I have said elsewhere that hyperbole is lying by proportion. The spitting narrative is the textbook case: a true thing, sixty incidents, told at a scale that makes it a lie. And Burmawi's account of why Middle Eastern Christians themselves so often speak for their regimes and against Israel is as compassionate as it is damning: they were Islamized by culture, they live in a bubble, they do not know what freedom is, they do not know they are persecuted, and the ones who found out are the reason no one else tries.

On Jordan. Burmawi loves his King. He says so twice, and it is not sarcasm — he describes a man whose mother is Christian, who protects Christians as far as he is able. And then he says what the King cannot say: the tyranny of public opinion sets the limits, and the King can protect Christians only as long as they behave. The Fuheis statue is the proof, and the sorting of the record in the first section sharpened it: erected by the youth club, removed by the municipality the next day after a social-media outcry, exiled to a cemetery, and then the King visited to affirm coexistence. That is the whole condition of the dhimmi in the kindest Muslim state on earth: tolerated, protected by a decent ruler against his own people, and required to keep their Lord in the graveyard where He cannot offend. The King is a good man in spite of Islam. Burmawi's phrase, twice over.

On the weaponized King. And now the hardest thing in the interview, and I think the truest. Burmawi says that the crowd shouting Christ is King at the Jews reminds him of the crowd in John 6 that tried to seize Jesus and make Him a king — not because they loved Him but because they wanted a king against Rome — and Jesus departed again into a mountain himself alone (John 6:15). Boreing says he has never heard the point made that way. Neither had I, and it is exactly right, and it is exactly this fellowship's theology. In the April essay I argued that the Kingdom of God differs from every theocracy not in content but in structure — that my kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight (John 18:36). A Christ conscripted as the banner of a faction against the Jews is a Christ made a king by force, for political reasons, by men who do not love Him. He will do what He did in Galilee. He will not be there. The slogan will be shouted over an empty throne, and the people shouting it will find they have enlisted under the same principal as the red-green alliance, whose signature is precisely that it does not care which servant carries the flag.

And Burmawi's dismissal of the Jews killed Jesus needs one verse to complete it, and Luke supplies it: For of a truth against thy holy child Jesus, whom thou hast anointed, both Herod, and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles, and the people of Israel, were gathered together (Acts 4:27). Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, Israel — that is everyone. The cross was a joint venture of the whole human race, and if I want to know who killed Jesus I need a mirror, not a map. Burmawi's own line is that after two thousand years of Jews persecuted on account of Jesus, he will not crucify them for a few sentences in the Talmud. He is a Christian saying it. So am I. Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do (Luke 23:34) — that was said over the whole assembly of Acts 4:27, and it was not a suggestion to the rest of us.

XI. Darby, Scofield, Replacement, and the Olive Tree

The interview closes on the eschatological front of the same war, and here I owe the fellowship precision, because this fellowship's position is not Burmawi's and not his opponents'.

The dissident-right narrative Burmawi describes runs: Christianity never believed in the restoration of Israel; John Nelson Darby invented dispensationalism in the 1830s, perhaps at Jewish instigation; the Scofield Reference Bible, funded by Jews, globalized it; and American Christian Zionism is therefore a Jewish plant. Burmawi answers with history: Puritan divines wrote of the future salvation of the Jews in the seventeenth century; Increase Mather's book is a matter of record; Pascal's Pensées treat the survival of the Jews as evidence for God; annotated Bibles do not shape imaginations as much as the theory requires; and the cabal-that-runs-the-world theory fails the simplest test of human nature — married couples cannot keep secrets through a divorce, and we are asked to believe a cabal has kept one for centuries without a single defector or deathbed manuscript. On the last point he is not merely right; he is applying the rule that a conspiracy claim must be graded by the evidence actually available for it — documented, demonstrated, inferred, speculated, dubious — and a two-thousand-year secret with no leak sits at the bottom of that ladder.

Now the precision. In the May essay on eschatology and the Christian underground (Endurance, Not Escape), I laid out at length why I reject the Darby system — the two-phase Second Coming, the pre-tribulation rapture, the Church as a parenthesis, and Israel and the Church as two separate peoples with two separate destinies. No Church Father taught it; no Reformer taught it; it is a nineteenth-century innovation, and I hold with the historic premillennial reading that Christ returns once, visibly, after the tribulation, and that the Church is the olive tree of Romans 11 into which Jew and Gentile alike are grafted by faith. I also said there that American Christian Zionism built on the two-peoples doctrine is theologically muddled, and I still think so.

But Burmawi is not defending Darby. He says he was once a dispensationalist and is no longer, that he holds no settled eschatology at all because the apocalyptic books are too easy to speculate from, and that he will know the sequence when it happens. What he does say is that he is not a replacement theologian — that he will not explain away God's promises to Israel as shadows — and on that I stand exactly where he stands, and the reason is the same chapter that gives me the olive tree. I say then, Hath God cast away his people? God forbid (Romans 11:1). Boast not against the branches. But if thou boast, thou bearest not the root, but the root thee (Romans 11:18). For the gifts and calling of God are without repentance (Romans 11:29). One can reject Darby's two peoples and still refuse to say that God is finished with the people from whom the Messiah came; indeed Romans 11 forbids the second more sternly than it permits the first. The historic Church, for all its supersessionist theologians, kept reading Romans 11 and kept expecting the Jews' return to their Messiah; the Puritans' restorationism grew from the olive tree, not from Darby's chart. And the fellowship's support for Israel — expressed most fully in the July essay on Amir Tsarfati's June update and the Sons of Issachar standard — rests not on a dispensational timetable but on two things that need no chart: the justice of a people's right to exist and defend itself, and I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee (Genesis 12:3), which was said to Abraham before there was a Jew or a Christian to argue about it. That is not uncritical. Israel is a state and states err, and the same essay that defended her deliberated her government's conduct on the merits. Support is not worship. But contempt is not analysis either, and the man who has decided that God's ancient people are simply a nation like any other — the position Burmawi attributes to one of Carlson's guests — has decided to read Romans 11 with the pages glued.

Boreing's closing line on prophecy deserves to be set in Scripture, because it is Scripture: God does not give prophecy so that we may know the future, but so that when the future arrives we may know that He is God. That is Isaiah — I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the beginning (Isaiah 46:9-10) — and it is Jesus in the upper room: And now I have told you before it come to pass, that, when it is come to pass, ye might believe (John 14:29). The people who understood the prophets best crucified the One they prophesied. Falling in love with one's chart of the future is the surest way to miss it when it comes. Burmawi's agnosticism and my historic premillennialism agree on this, and it is the only part of eschatology on which I would stake a great deal.

XII. The War of Stories, and the Floor Under It

Burmawi's frame for all of it is that the West was built on a story — covenant, human depravity, a system designed to help selfish men rise again after failure — and that another story has come to replace it, and that the danger is not the bomb but the replacement of the story, which alters everything downstream. He says Islam is leaving the Middle East, where atheism is spreading, and that Europe is the new center of jihad; he says the West apologized to the thief while holding the stolen goods, and the thief, condemned if he did not, doubled down; he says Islam won the lottery in the West after 9/11 because the accommodation that followed the accusation was worth more than a century of migration. And he says at the end that Israel is under attack not for what it does but for what it is, that the war against Israel is the vehicle for smuggling jihad into the West rebranded as liberation and resistance, that fifty years of postmodernism and relativism emptied the throne of the biblical God, and that foreign gods are now competing for the seat.

I accept the frame, and I want to give it the floor it lacks, because war of stories is where the comparison of architectures ends and where a Christian argument must go one floor further down. If it is only a war of stories, then the relativist's checkmate stands: your story, their story, who is to say? And the Islamist's reply stands too: I prefer the story of submission; you prefer the story of grace; your preference is not law. The answer this fellowship gives is the ontological one — the floor beneath every comparison — and I will not retreat from it into comparative sociology: reality has a nature. The world is not a neutral stage on which stories compete; it is the sustained thought of a God who is love, and every story is either aligned with the nature of the One who holds the world in being, or misaligned with it. The Conscious Point Physics is our attempt to say this in the language of physics — that the substance of existence is conscious, that in him we live, and move, and have our being (Acts 17:28) is description and not metaphor — and its moral consequence is that alignment with God's nature is not a preference; it is what good means, the way alignment with the grain is what a straight cut means. A story of submission to a master whose every page threatens is a story misaligned with the ground of being, and the misalignment shows in the fruits Burmawi catalogued from the inside: fear, guilt, conformity, stagnation, the poetess between the camels. A story of adoption by a Father who paid the debt Himself is aligned with the ground of being, and its fruits are the high-trust society, the confidence to build, the freedom of the neighbor, the imago Dei that even the unbelieving nations sign. Ye shall know them by their fruits (Matthew 7:16) is not a rule of thumb; it is what the alignment of a story with reality looks like from the outside. The West will not win a war of stories by telling its story louder. It will win by remembering that its story is true — and by living as if it were.

And that is why the empty throne is the right image and the wrong conclusion. The throne of the biblical God is not empty. The West vacated its acknowledgment of Him, and the gods now competing for the seat are competing for a chair that was never theirs. The remedy is not to elect a better god. It is repentance — remember therefore from whence thou art fallen — and the first works.

XIII. The Sons of Issachar: Judgments and Levers

The men of Issachar understood the times and knew what Israel ought to do, and the second half is the one that costs something. An essay that ends at understanding is not finished. Here are the judgments, stated provisionally and stated anyway, and the levers.

Judgments.

  1. Islam is a comprehensive civilizational order that contains a religion. The First Amendment protects the religion — the prayer, the fast, the creed, the mosque, the conversion in either direction. It does not protect the program, and a republic that cannot tell the sofa from the room will lose the room. On this I judge Burmawi correct without reservation.
  2. The instrument is not the State Department. Government theology is a sword that turns. The state's proper work is to distinguish exercise from sedition, to refuse blasphemy law in every costume including the costume of Islamophobia, to screen immigration for allegiance to the program, and to shelter the apostate. The church's work is everything Burmawi asked the state to do: to read the texts, tell the truth, and equip believers. On this I judge him half right and correct the half.
  3. Muslims are neighbors and captives to rescue, not enemies to defeat; Islam is a system to be opposed and named as false. The moderate's context-reading is welcome and his shielding of the source is not. Love the man; do not accept the shield.
  4. The dissident right's turn — Muslims as allies, Jews as the common enemy, Christ as the banner — is a lie by proportion in its facts and a seizure of Christ by force in its theology, and He will not be found under that banner. I judge it not merely mistaken but spiritually dangerous, and I say so to brothers whom I would rather not offend.
  5. On Israel, the fellowship's ground is Romans 11 and Genesis 12:3, not Darby's chart. Reject the two-peoples system and reject replacement theology in the same breath; support Israel's right to exist and defend herself on grounds of justice and covenant; retain the liberty to criticize her government as we criticize our own.
  6. The red-green alliance is a de facto conspiracy that needs no coordination because its members serve one principal. The response is spiritual before it is political: name the principal, and refuse to be recruited by either of his servants — including the one wearing a cross.

Levers.

Prayer, named and specific. For Danny Burmawi, who cannot go home, and for the Ideological Defense Institute. For the Jordanian convert whose household is now Christian, and for every secret believer in Jordan, Egypt, Pakistan, and the Gulf whose faith is a capital offense in the court of public opinion if not of law. For the Christians of Nigeria and Syria being killed this week. For the Muslim families in our own towns, that they may meet the Jesus who did not run. For Zohran Mamdani, by name, that the man be saved and the program fail. For Tucker Carlson and the young men who trust him, that a captured audience be freed from the story that is capturing it — they are not our enemies; they are ours to win back. For the King of Jordan, that a good man in spite of Islam might become a good man because of Christ.

Speech where speech reaches power. Through the Christos Voting Network, three asks: that our representatives oppose any federal or state measure that would define criticism of Islam as a hate offense; that they support asylum for apostates from Islam as a named category of religious persecution; and that the persecution of Christians in Nigeria and Syria be treated in foreign policy as the emergency it is. Small levers, named truthfully — for who hath despised the day of small things? (Zechariah 4:10) — outrank large levers imagined.

Formation. Burmawi issued a challenge: open the Qur'an at random and read. I convert it into an assignment for the fellowship. Read it — the Qur'an, and a Sira, and the Bukhari collection — not to master it as Burmawi once did, but as Bereans, who searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so (Acts 17:11). A Christian who has never read what Islam says about itself is defenseless against both the imam and the Tucker Carlson guest who tells him what it says. And apply the second test of Section III as you read: does this revelation ever rebuke its recipient? You will not need my essay after that. You will have Aisha's.

Hospitality. The counsel this fellowship has long held on engaging Muslims stands: relationship first, questions before arguments, Jesus before Muhammad, patience, and no lone rangers. The man at the mosque down the road is not an ideology. He is a neighbor who has been told all his life that Christians know Islam is true and refuse it out of rebellion. He has never met one who loves him and can also tell him, gently, what his own book says about the substitution on the cross. Be that one.

The watcher's soul. Luke 21:28 and 21:34 held together: look up, and do not let the heart be overcharged. A fellowship inflamed cannot sit on the council of Issachar. We are not afraid of Islam. We are afraid of a church that has become a museum of itself, and that fear is the beginning of the first works.

XIV. The Bet I Am Making

Burmawi said he did not convert because Christianity was the natural alternative once Islam failed, but because it was magnificent — the solution to everything — and that the gospel is everywhere, because everything must die so that the new may be born. He said it as a man who knows the price of the alternative in his own body: years of exile, a country he cannot enter, a family he cannot visit, because he left Islam and then did the thing that in Jordan is the worse crime: he defended Israel. He said the relationship with God in Islam is terror. He wept over the Qur'an and was afraid.

I believe him, and I believe the reason, and I stake myself on it. The God who holds the world in being is not a master who threatens on every page; He is a Father who paid what we owed and calls us sons. That is not my preference against the Islamist's. It is the nature of things, and every civilization that has forgotten it has ended up under curtains. The candlesticks of Asia can be lit again. The mosaics are still on the ceiling. Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage (Galatians 5:1) — not the bondage of Sharia, and not the bondage of the fear that makes a church too polite to say that Sharia is bondage.

For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father. — Romans 8:15
