# Paradox Is Not Contradiction: What Quantum Physics Can and Cannot Tell Us About Christ


A physicist has written a short and moving testimony that is making its way around the internet this week under the title [*“I was an atheist. Quantum physics set me on the path to Jesus.”*](https://www.msn.com/en-us/lifestyle/other/i-was-an-atheist-quantum-physics-set-me-on-the-path-to-jesus/ar-AA2dw4fA?ctsrc=dgst&ocid=edgntpruby&pc=EDGEDB&cvid=6ac1aac523aa408d9430569c3f66d207&cvpid=8d2e51c3760c495b8fce24aeb1346c56&uxmode=ruby&ei=19) [AUTHOR], who was trained in physics and spent his early years as a convinced atheist, tells how the strangeness of quantum mechanics broke the machine-picture of the world he had inherited, how he then opened the New Testament and found that Jesus spoke with the same unsettling strangeness, and how decades of study, honest science, and personal experience finally brought him to confess Jesus as Lord. [Read it here.](https://www.msn.com/en-us/lifestyle/other/i-was-an-atheist-quantum-physics-set-me-on-the-path-to-jesus/ar-AA2dw4fA) It is worth your five minutes.


I rejoice in his testimony. He came to the right Person by a road I know well. I have spent some forty years working at the junction of physics and faith, and I agree with his destination, with his diagnosis of the skeptic, and with his conviction that a man can be fully a scientist and fully a Christian without dishonesty in either direction.


But I think he takes one wrong turn on the way, and it is a turn many Christians are tempted to take, so it is worth slowing down to look at it. He argues that the central doctrines of the faith — that Jesus is fully God and fully man, that God is both the Creator of all and the One who walks with Adam in the garden, that God is one and also three — do not make sense, and that this is acceptable because quantum physics does not make sense either. Then he offers quantum *superposition* as the scientific explanation of all three.


I want to show why that move gives away too much, why superposition is the wrong picture of Christ and of the Trinity, and where quantum physics really does point toward God. The short version: Christianity is paradoxical, but it is not contradictory. And the bridge between physics and faith is not that reality is illogical. It is that reality is *conscious*.


---


## Where he is right


Start with what he gets exactly right. The skeptic he describes is real, and he steelmans him fairly. The *classical* worldview — named after Newton’s classical physics — holds that the universe is a machine, matter is all there is, the body is meat, the mind is the brain’s electrical weather, and the cosmos is a colossal accident. Stephen Hawking gave the view its most famous expression when he told an interviewer that he regarded the brain as a computer that stops when its parts fail, and that heaven is a story for people who are afraid of the dark.


That is a coherent position. It is not stupid, and the men who hold it are not stupid. It is what a thoughtful person concludes if he believes that the deepest layer of reality is dead stuff obeying blind rules.


And it is precisely that belief which twentieth-century physics damaged beyond repair. Matter, examined closely, did not turn out to be little hard marbles. It turned out to be something stranger: entities whose behavior is governed by probabilities, which can be correlated across distances with no signal passing between them, and whose properties are not fully settled until they interact. Our author is right that this broke his mechanistic picture. It should break anyone’s.


So far, we agree completely.


---


## Paradox is not contradiction


Here is the turn. When the skeptic objects that “fully God and fully man” makes no sense, our author concedes the point — the skeptic is right, he says, these beliefs make no sense — and then answers that quantum physics makes no sense either. He enlists Richard Feynman’s famous remark that nobody really understands quantum mechanics.


I cannot make that concession, and I do not think any Christian should.


Words matter here, so let me be precise. A *contradiction* is a claim that something is and is not the same thing, in the same sense, at the same time. “This triangle has four sides” is a contradiction. No amount of mystery rescues it; it is simply false.


A *paradox* is something else. The word comes from the Greek *para* (“beside” or “against”) and *doxa* (“opinion” or “expectation”). A paradox is a truth that runs against our expectation — a statement that *seems* contradictory until we see the distinction that makes both halves true.


- A paradox does not mean the claim is irrational.

- It does not mean logic has been suspended.

- It does not mean we must believe it in spite of our minds.

- It means our first expectation was too small for the thing in front of us.


When Jesus says, *“whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it”* (Matthew 16:25), He is not abandoning logic. He is using the word *life* at two levels — the life of the self-protecting ego, and the life that is eternal fellowship with God — and telling us that clinging to the first costs us the second. The saying startles us, but it is perfectly coherent once the two levels are seen. The same is true of loving our enemies, of the first being last, and of dying in order to live. These are not violations of reason. They are reason operating at a depth we did not expect.


This matters because the God of the Bible does not ask us to abandon our minds. *“Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD”* (Isaiah 1:18). *“God is not the author of confusion”* (1 Corinthians 14:33). And the Gospel of John opens by naming the Son as the *Logos* — the Word, the Reason, the ordering principle by which all things were made (John 1:1–3). Logic is not an enemy Christ had to overcome. It is one of the things He made.


So when the skeptic says “your doctrines don’t make sense,” the right answer is not “you’re right, but neither does physics.” The right answer is: “They make more sense than you have been told. Let me show you the distinction.”

## What superposition is, and what it isn’t


Now to the physics. I want to be careful here, because I do not want to correct one overstatement with another.


Superposition is real. In my own June 2025 essay [“Is Quantum Superposition Real?”](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2025/06/08/is-quantum-superposition-real/) I argued that the superposition of states within a quantum of energy is a genuine physical fact and not merely bookkeeping — and that the instantaneous correlations of entangled particles point to an underlying operating system beneath the visible world, one that keeps energy and momentum perfectly conserved across any distance.


But notice what superposition actually describes. A system in superposition is not two finished things at once. It is one system whose outcome is not yet settled, described as a weighted combination of possible outcomes. When it interacts — when it is measured — it does not show up as both. It shows up as *one*. The electron is found here, not there. The detector clicks or it does not.


Schrödinger’s famous cat, dead and alive at once, was never offered as a description of how cats are. Erwin Schrödinger proposed it as a *reductio ad absurdum* — an argument meant to show how absurd it would be to carry the quantum description up to the scale of living things without asking what is actually happening underneath.


And light is not “fully solid as a rock and fully supple as water.” Light is one thing — the photon — which shows particle-like behavior when it is absorbed or emitted, and wave-like behavior as it travels. Einstein’s point was that neither the particle picture nor the wave picture captures it alone; together they describe it. In the physics this ministry has been developing, called Conscious Point Physics — which holds that the substance of reality is conscious, God’s sustaining thought, so that *in him we live, and move, and have our being* is a description rather than a metaphor — the photon is one real entity whose wave-like behavior comes from the medium it passes through. Space is densely filled with bound pairs of opposite-charged points, which we call Dipole Particles, and the photon’s passage through that sea is what produces the interference patterns we call wave-like. One thing, one nature, two kinds of behavior depending on what it is doing.


In other words, wave-particle duality is not a contradiction either. It is a paradox — a surprise that dissolves when the underlying mechanism is understood.

## Why superposition is the wrong picture of Christ


Here is my central concern, and it is not a quibble about physics. If we take superposition seriously as a *model* of the Incarnation and the Trinity, it leads us into the very heresies the early Church fought hardest to exclude.


Consider what the model would say.


**On Christ’s two natures.** In a superposition, each possible state is present only as a weighted share — a fraction of the whole — and when the system is observed, one state appears and the other is gone. Applied to Jesus, that would mean He is partly God and partly man, and that whenever anyone actually encountered Him, He was found to be *either* the man *or* God. That is the opposite of the faith. The Council of Chalcedon in AD 451 confessed one Christ *“in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation.”* Not a mixture. Not one nature showing up at a time. Both, wholly, always.


And the Gospels show Him being *observed* as both at once. Thomas the doubter — a man after a scientist’s own heart, who refused to believe without evidence he could touch — put his hand into the wounds of a crucified human body and in the same breath said, *“My Lord and my God”* (John 20:28). The observation did not collapse Christ into one nature. It revealed both.


**On the Trinity.** If Father, Son, and Holy Spirit were three possible states of the one God, waiting to resolve into one of them depending on how we encounter Him, we would have reinvented *modalism* — the third-century teaching associated with Sabellius, that the one God merely appears as Father in one mode, as Son in another, as Spirit in a third. The Church rejected it because Scripture will not allow it. At the baptism of Jesus the Son stands in the water, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father speaks from heaven — all three, present and distinct, in the same moment (Matthew 3:16–17). Jesus prays to the Father. The Father sends the Spirit in the Son’s name. These are not states of one person taking turns. They are three persons in one Being.


So the structure of superposition is exactly wrong for the claim Christianity makes. Superposition says *one of these, not yet determined*. Christianity says *all of these, fully, and determinately so*. Our author’s instinct that the Christian claims are strong and surprising is right. But the Christian claim is stronger than superposition, not a case of it.


---


## Elohim and YHWH are not two natures


A smaller point, but a telling one. Our author reads Genesis 1, where God is called *Elohim*, and Genesis 2, where God is called *YHWH*, as presenting two opposing natures: the universal Creator and the personal God who walks with Adam.


It is true that the two names carry different emphases — *Elohim* the Mighty One who creates by His word, *YHWH* the covenant name, “I AM,” the One who binds Himself to a people. But Scripture does not set them against each other. From Genesis 2:4 onward the text deliberately joins them: *the LORD God* — *YHWH Elohim* — formed man of the dust, planted the garden, walked in it in the cool of the day. The one who made the galaxies is the one who calls out “Where art thou?” (Genesis 3:9). And the great confession of Israel binds both names into a single sentence: *“Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD”* (Deuteronomy 6:4) — *YHWH Eloheinu, YHWH echad*.


The universal God is personal not *in spite of* being universal but *because* of it. Paul told the philosophers in Athens that God *“be not far from every one of us: For in him we live, and move, and have our being”* (Acts 17:27–28). A God who is present to every point of His creation is not distant from any of it. His universality is the ground of His nearness.

## A practical test


Someone might say this is all just a matter of emphasis — that our author’s analogy is helpful even if it is imperfect. Here is a practical test of whether the difference matters: **what happens in the next exchange with the skeptic?**


If we say, “Our doctrines are nonsense, but so is quantum physics,” the skeptic has an easy reply: *“Quantum physics comes with equations that predict experiments to many decimal places. Your nonsense comes with none. And if physicists someday find a deeper theory that makes the quantum world intelligible — as many are trying to do — your defense of the faith collapses with it.”* He is right on both counts. We would have tied the Creed, which is two thousand years old, to one interpretation of a physics that is one hundred years old.


If instead we say, “Our doctrines are paradoxes, not contradictions — here is the distinction that makes each coherent, and here is the evidence of history and experience that they are true,” the conversation keeps going. We have not asked him to give up reason in order to come to Christ. We have invited him to use it more fully.

## Where quantum physics really points


So what *does* quantum physics tell us about God? Here I think our author’s intuition is right, even though his model is not.


What broke the classical worldview was not that nature turned out to be illogical. It was that nature turned out not to be *dead*. At the bottom of things, physicists did not find inert stuff pushed around by blind forces. They found something that behaves as though it perceives its surroundings and responds to them by rule — that keeps perfect accounts of energy and momentum across any distance, instantaneously — that is ordered, lawful, and yet not mechanical in the old sense.


The physics I have spent forty years developing takes that observation at face value. It proposes that the smallest constituents of the universe are not bits of matter but *points of consciousness* — Conscious Points — each one perceiving its neighbors, computing its response, and moving, moment by moment, under laws God has given them. On this view the universe is not a machine that happens to contain minds. It is the ongoing thought of a Mind, which has made room within itself for other minds.


That is not pantheism. A wave is *of* the ocean but is not *the* ocean; the creation is *of* God — sustained by Him, existing within His upholding thought — but it is not God. Only the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are God. But it means that Hawking’s broken-down computer was never the right picture of a human being, because there was never any dead matter to build a mere computer out of. The materialist’s foundation is not there.


This is where science and Scripture genuinely meet. Not in the claim that both are absurd, but in the claim that both describe a reality held together, moment by moment, by a living Word — *“upholding all things by the word of his power”* (Hebrews 1:3).

## My stake


I am grateful for our author’s witness. He found the Lord, and he is telling the world about it at some cost; the hostility he describes is real, and I have felt it too. The skeptics who mock him are not our enemies. They are captives to rescue — men held by a picture of reality that is too small, and that their own physics has already cracked.


But we will not rescue them by agreeing that our faith is nonsense. I do not believe it is. I believe the Incarnation is the most coherent fact in the universe — that the Mind whose thought constitutes every particle took on flesh made of those very particles, without ceasing to be the Mind that sustains them. I believe the Trinity is not a riddle that physics excuses but the shape of a God who is love before there was anyone else to love. And I believe that when the skeptic finally looks beneath the matter he trusts, he will find not chaos and not absurdity, but the Word.


*“For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible … all things were created by him, and for him: And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.”* — Colossians 1:16–17
