## 1. The number that should drive every other decision

The call derived this live and then moved past it without registering what
it means. Using the group's own figures — $3,500 cost of goods per unit,
$12,000 retail, Medicare paying 75% of MSRP, DME taking 30%:

| Channel | Ideomotion revenue per unit | Gross margin | Units/yr to cover the $192,000 burn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct retail (cash or financed) | $12,000 | $8,500 | 23 |
| Own DME + Medicare | $9,000 | $5,500 | 35 (before DME operating cost) |
| Third-party DME + Medicare | $6,000 | $2,500 | 77 |

The third-party DME route requires more than three times the sales volume to
keep the lights on. That is the single most important fact on this page.

It does not mean abandon the DME route. It means the DME route is a volume
strategy that only works at scale you do not yet have, and it should be
funded out of profits from the full-margin channels rather than pursued
instead of them.

## 2. Correcting the frame: Medicare is not where the money is

Thomas's formulation in the call was "we go to the Medicare people because
they got the money." They do have the money. But look at what happens to it
in transit: Medicare pays 75% of MSRP, the DME keeps 30% of that, and
$12,000 of retail value arrives as $6,000. The Medicare dollar is the most
heavily taxed dollar available to Ideomotion — it loses roughly half its
value before it reaches you.

Three channels pay near-full freight with no DME, no HCPCS code, and no
Medicare enrollment:

- Private pay with financing — full $12,000, available the moment insurance and a finance partner are in place
- Facility capital budgets — adult foster homes, IDD group homes, rehab clinics, PT practices; they buy from a budget line, not a benefit
- VA Federal Supply Schedule 65 II F — no code, no benefit category, no coverage determination required

The honest counterweight, which Thomas is right about: those three channels
reach only people who can pay, and Medicare reaches everyone else. A society
where healthcare is treated as an entitlement genuinely does shrink the
cash-paying market. That's a real observation about the terrain, not a
reason to accept a 70% haircut before you've proven the product sells at
all.

Position: run all four. But size the effort by margin, not by market size,
until you have data. Every hour spent on Medicare in the next 90 days is an
hour not spent on a channel that pays 3.4x more per unit.

## 3. The DME decision — and a timing fact that changes it

The three options discussed were: buy an aged DME (~$1.2M), buy a young one
($100-400k, Medicare doesn't transfer), or apply for your own (~1 year,
~$200k of salary already being spent). Three regulatory facts the call
didn't have:

The nationwide enrollment moratorium expired on 27 August 2026 — thirteen
days ago. CMS imposed a nationwide freeze on new DMEPOS supplier enrollments
on 27 February 2026, the first in the program's history. It ran six months,
CMS chose not to extend it, and the National Provider Enrollment contractors
(Palmetto GBA and Novitas) are now accepting initial applications again. CMS
is explicitly warning that a backlog is forming and that delays in
submitting may push applications past the processing window for the 2028
competitive bidding round.

That is a live, closing window. If you are going to apply, apply now — not
because a decision has been made, but because the cost of applying is low
and the cost of applying six months into a post-moratorium surge is a longer
queue.

The 36-month rule expanded to DMEPOS on 1 January 2026. If a supplier
changes majority ownership within 36 months of initial enrollment or its
most recent ownership change, Medicare billing privileges do not transfer —
the buyer must enroll as a new supplier and obtain new accreditation. This
is precisely why an aged DME costs $1.2M: the rule made seasoned enrollments
scarce. Thomas had this right.

It also complicates his exit thesis ("keep it three years, sell it for a
million"). That resale value exists because CMS tightened the rule this
year. A rule that was tightened once can be tightened again, and CMS's
posture here is plainly aggressive — a nationwide moratorium and a rule
expansion in the same twelve months. Do not underwrite a $1.2M asset value
on a regulation eighteen months old.

Accreditation moved from a three-year cycle to mandatory annual surveys.
This is an ongoing cost not in the current budget. Owning a DME is not a
one-time $200k; it is $200k plus a recurring annual compliance and survey
burden forever.

**Recommendation**

Apply. Don't buy. Sell through someone else's in the meantime.

- Apply now — the window just reopened, the application cost is mostly salary you are already spending, and a year from now you either have an asset or you have a clear answer that the path isn't worth it. Thomas's read on this at the end of the call was correct.
- Don't buy — $1.2M is roughly 140 units of direct-retail gross margin, spent on an asset whose value rests on a regulation CMS is actively rewriting.
- Run third-party DME in parallel — it is the only option that produces revenue during the wait, and it generates exactly the sales data you need to decide whether the owned DME is worth completing.

## 4. "Educating the competition" — this worry is misplaced

Thomas's concern was that going through DMEs teaches them the market exists,
and they'll cut you out. I don't think this survives scrutiny.

DMEs are distributors, not manufacturers. A DME learning that lift-walkers
sell does not give them a lift-walker. What gives you a lift-walker is
Leonard's CAD suite, Charlie's manufacturing knowledge, and the year of
development on the split gear head. Distribution channels don't
backward-integrate into fabrication on the strength of one product moving
well.

The real risks of the DME channel are different and worth naming honestly:
margin compression (Section 1) and channel dependence — if a DME becomes 60%
of your volume, they set your terms at renewal.

Joelle's Walmart analogy is the right read. Distribution at a loss to gain
reach is a standard and rational move when you're unknown. And Thomas's own
follow-on idea is the right way to run it: start with one or two local or
regional DMEs — Bakersfield, Boise — rather than a national. Low exposure,
real data, no large relationship to unwind if you later go direct.

Have Isak ask each candidate their actual percentage. The 20-40% range is a
market average, not a quote. A small regional DME hungry for a
differentiated product may take 20%; a national with shelf-space leverage
will take 40%.

## 5. The buyback program — pause before this reaches any sales material

Joelle's buyback idea was the most creative thing in the call, and Thomas
was right that it's outside-the-box thinking. It also has two problems that
need resolving before it's said to a customer.

**The legal problem.** The proposal as discussed is: buy this now for $12,000
cash, and if you later get Medicare to pay for one, we'll buy your unit back
for $6,000. That offers a beneficiary $6,000 of value contingent on their
obtaining a Medicare-paid item from you. That is remuneration to a
beneficiary tied to a federal healthcare program transaction, which is the
exact territory the Anti-Kickback Statute and the Beneficiary Inducements
civil monetary penalty provisions govern. Thomas raised anti-kickback
himself in this same call, regarding the $4,000 check Margo received and
returned. The instinct that made that check obviously wrong applies here
too.

**The coverage problem, which may moot the legal one.** Medicare's reasonable
useful lifetime for durable medical equipment is five years, and it
generally will not pay for equipment a beneficiary already owns and that
still functions. The buyback premise requires Medicare to buy a second
identical device for someone who demonstrably has a working first one. That
claim is very likely denied on medical necessity before anyone reaches the
kickback question.

**What survives, and is worth building:**

- An unconditional trade-in program. "We'll take your unit in trade toward an upgrade, or buy it back at wholesale, at your option, any time after 12 months." Not conditioned on Medicare, not conditioned on anything. This removes the customer's fear of being stranded — which is the real objection Joelle was solving for — without tying the payment to a federal program transaction.
- Rent-to-own or financing with a buyout. Same psychological effect, ordinary commercial structure, no exposure.

Action: anything in this family goes past healthcare counsel before it
appears on the website, in a one-pager, or in a phone script. This is a
one-hour consult, not a project, and it is much cheaper than the
alternative.

## 7. Price — resolve the $12,000 / $16,000 conflict this week

There is currently an inconsistency in the market. Isak sent SciFit Petaluma
a price list showing the Traveler at $16,000. In the same call Thomas said
16 is optimistic and 12 is realistic, and the group then built the entire
buyback and wholesale model on $12,000 retail / $6,000 wholesale. Two people
are quoting two numbers.

That has to be fixed before more quotes go out, because both the Medicare
and VA paths anchor on price:

- Medicare pays a percentage of MSRP, so a low MSRP permanently lowers every future reimbursement
- VA FSS negotiates on most-favored-customer commercial pricing, so your transacted price becomes your federal ceiling

Both argue for holding a high list.

**Recommendation:** MSRP $16,000, with one published and defensible direct
price.

Do not simply "list 16 and sell at 12." A list price nobody pays is not a
list price — it's a discount schedule, and if every transaction is $12,000
then $12,000 is your price of record and you get no benefit from the higher
number. If the direct price is $12,000, it needs a stated reason that
distinguishes it: direct-from-manufacturer, no dealer margin, cash or
financed, no third-party billing. That is a real distinction and it survives
scrutiny from both CMS and a VA contracting officer.

Also note the product ladder you already have and may not be using
deliberately. Isak's price list showed the Traveler at $16,000 and the
original Liftwalker models at $6,000-8,000. That is a good-better structure,
and it answers Thomas's competitive worry from August about being priced
above the shiny cheap units — you have an answer at both ends. Make sure the
two products have distinct names, distinct spec sheets, and distinct target
channels rather than being described interchangeably.

**Decision needed:** one number, one price list, both Isak and Charlie quoting
it. This week.

## 8. Charlie's "superpower" — decompose it rather than admire it

Thomas asked the sharpest question in the call: we need to find out how
people who don't have superpowers can sell. This deserves a real answer.

Charlie's advantage is probably not mystical. He sells in person, to the end
user, with the machine present and demonstrated. That is a demonstration
sale — a specific, teachable form. The customer sees the device do the
thing, the objection surfaces in the room, and he answers it while they're
looking at the answer.

The parts that transfer: what he shows first, the order he addresses
objections in, which features he leads with and which he never mentions, how
he closes. The part that doesn't transfer is rapport — and rapport is not
what closes a $12,000 medical device to a family that has never heard of the
product.

Action, and it changes what's already planned: Isak is scheduling a filming
session with Charlie for website photos and video. Repurpose it. Do the
product photography, but also film Charlie doing a complete sale — a real
one if a prospect will consent, a full role-play if not — end to end,
uninterrupted, no coaching. Then transcribe it and extract a one-page
demonstration script.

Isak already identified the underlying problem: "every time we talk, there's
a lot of useful stuff that, if it's not recorded, kind of gets lost." That's
exactly right, and the sales method is the highest-value thing currently
being lost. That one-page script is worth more to the company than the
website is.

## 10. The prescriber question, answered

The call left this open. Here is what's established:

Who can order DME under Medicare: a treating practitioner — physician (MD or
DO), physician assistant, nurse practitioner, or clinical nurse specialist.
Physical therapists and occupational therapists cannot order DME.
Chiropractors cannot order DME.

But PTs and OTs are not a dead end. They typically perform the mobility
evaluation that supports and justifies the order. They are the influencers
who identify the need and route the patient; the physician signs. Selling to
PTs and OTs is building exactly the referral wedge Thomas described — PT
identifies the candidate, refers to the orthopedist or physiatrist,
physician writes the order. Joelle's chiropractor contact who serves
wheelchair-using patients works the same way: he can't sign, but he can
refer, and he sees the population.

The specialty Thomas couldn't name is most likely physical medicine and
rehabilitation (physiatry) — physiatrists are the specialty most associated
with wheelchair and mobility equipment prescribing and re-evaluation.
Neurology, orthopedics, and geriatrics are secondary. Target physiatrists
first.

On the annual re-prescription Joelle observed: Medicare does not require
annual re-prescription for equipment a beneficiary already owns — the
reasonable useful lifetime for DME is five years. The likely explanations
for what her friend described are that it is Medicaid rather than Medicare
(state Medicaid programs commonly do require periodic reauthorization), that
the chair is on a rental arrangement rather than owned, or that the annual
visit is authorizing repairs or replacement parts.

Thomas's reaction — "that opens up an entirely new concept" — was pointed at
recurring revenue. Worth being clear: recurring revenue does exist in this
industry, and its name is rental, which the group has already said it
doesn't want. Confirm which program and whether it's rental before building
anything on this. It's one text message to Joelle's friend.

## Plain Language summary

The most important number from the call is one you worked out and then
walked past. Selling direct at $12,000 nets you $8,500 a unit. Selling
through somebody else's DME to Medicare nets you $2,500. That means 23 units
a year covers the payroll direct, or 77 units through a DME. Same product,
same work, three times the volume required. Medicare has the money, but it
keeps a quarter and the DME keeps a third, so it's the most expensive dollar
you can chase. Chase it — just don't chase it first, and don't fund it
instead of the channels that pay full freight.

On the DME question: apply, don't buy, and sell through someone else's while
you wait. There's a timing reason to move — CMS had a nationwide freeze on
new DME enrollments that expired on August 27th, thirteen days ago, and
they're warning about a backlog forming. The window is open right now.
Buying one instead would cost $1.2 million for an asset whose value depends
entirely on a rule CMS rewrote eight months ago and could rewrite again.

they're distributors, not manufacturers, and knowing the market exists

you suggested. But the buyback idea does need a lawyer before it's said to a
