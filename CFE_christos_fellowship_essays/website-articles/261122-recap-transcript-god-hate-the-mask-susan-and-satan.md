# 261122 - Recap - Transcript - God-Hate, The Mask, Susan and Satan

# God and Hate, The Mask, and Susan's Version


### Edited transcript of the Christos Fellowship meeting

22 November 2026 · Thomas  Lee Abshier, ND

## Editor's note

This is an edited transcript of the Sunday, 22 November 2026 Christos Fellowship meeting (Renaissance Ministries), about 10:01 AM to 1:42 PM. Participants: Thomas Lee Abshier, ND (host), Michael Sherman, Armond Boulware, Leonard Hofheins, Charlie Gutierrez and Susan Gutierrez. Claude edited it for clarity from the automatic speech-to-text record.

**Notes.** Indented notes marked *Editor's note* give Scripture references or corrections where the discussion missed a reference or got something wrong. Scripture is quoted from the King James Version unless stated.

## Part One: God and Hate


### 1. Opening verses: "The Spirit is like the wind" and "Love thy neighbor"

**Thomas:** Charlie, did you choose "the Spirit is like the wind" as a statement of some sort, or was it just the next thing up on the stack?

**Charlie:** It's next on the stack. "The Spirit is like the wind" is from John 3. It's just something I like. Jesus said it. I don't understand it yet.
> *Editor's note:* The verse is John 3:8, not 3:7: *"The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth: so is every one that is born of the Spirit."* **(John 3:8)**

**Michael:** Good morning. Love thy neighbor. That's just one of my favorites. I woke up thinking of it, so I thought I'd share it. I was looking up where it is in the Bible, and it turns out it's in a lot of places. Leviticus is the earliest, but it's all over.
> *Editor's note:* *"Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself"* **(Leviticus 19:18)**; repeated by Jesus in **(Matthew 22:39)**, **(Mark 12:31)** and **(Luke 10:27)**, and by Paul in **(Romans 13:9)** and **(Galatians 5:14)**, and by James in **(James 2:8)**.

**Charlie:** You're such a loving guy.

**Michael:** Why wouldn't one be? You've got a choice: shall I hate today, or shall I love?

### 2. Why we choose love over hate: Michael's answer

**Thomas:** Why do you think we choose love instead of hate?

**Michael:** It feels good. I tried hate, and it makes my skin crawl. Hate is also cumulative; it builds on itself. I truly enjoy my fellow members of the species. And I don't even stop with them: little animals, trees, life itself, ecosystems, the universe. It's a beautiful thing.

### 3. Love of persons versus love of things

**Thomas:** Last time we talked about God being love, and you made the point that you would be content alone, like being with Wilson on the deserted island. You could enjoy anything, wherever you were.

**Michael:** I'm good with people or without.

**Thomas:** I've made the thesis that God is everything. If you have to have an "other," the other can be anything. Rocks are other, so you can love the rock; you can love Wilson. So the question came up: why create humans, and why would that be anything special? I think we saw in the Bible that He made us in His image. So it was actually a peer relationship, rather than a relationship with the props and the lesser creatures. Humanity is a particular kind of love, one that is more satisfying than the love of Wilson, or the rocks, the palm trees, the ocean and the waves.

**Michael:** Well, they're all unique. The human ones are more complicated.

**Thomas:** Absolutely.

**Michael:** But there is also just reveling in mere breathing, with people or without.

**Thomas:** So you can have a wonderful experience loving a rock, or a seashore and waves, or palm trees and coconuts. All of that is wonderful, and that would be an example of God loving and experiencing love. Can you see any distinction between that kind of love and the love of a human? You said complicated. Is there anything more than that?

### 4. Love as entwinement: "I of you"

**Michael:** Infinitely more. And I want to refine the definition of love as I experience it, to be a little different from a subject–object relationship. When I love, I don't see "I love you" or "I love it." I don't look at us as separate. Instead, it's entwined. I love the air this morning, and it's going to be in me, technically within my body's boundaries. It's part of me; in fact, it's essential for my being me.

A family member could say, "I love this family," but not as if it were separate from him. Your daughter or your son is part of you. If something happens to hurt or help your child, you feel it everywhere, if you're a loving parent. I don't make that separation. I'm part of everything. You touch some fine fabric and say, "Wow, that feels good," and it's not just your finger; it's your brain that registered the softness. It permeates. When I have a really good bite of something delicious, everywhere feels it. That goes for everything else, and it goes for the way I love.

I want to say I love things, but it's the way I entwine with them. I think I told you: once I tried to tell Jenny "I love you," and it came out "I of you," which is even more true. We met in '71, so that's 55 years. In 55 years, enough of each of us has entwined into the other. I have a voice I got from my relationship with her, and even from my relationship with you, Tom.

I don't know if I told the others this. Tom was in high school with me. I was a freshman, two years younger, mystified by chemistry. I looked at his chemistry book and said, "Tell me something good about chemistry." And he said, as though it were the big secret of the universe, "Al(OH)3 is amphoteric." It was the most hilarious thing. I didn't know what it meant, but it was as if there were some mystical language that revealed these truths. We both cackled for a good long while at the silliness of it. You don't even need a context, really. I didn't understand a word of it, but it was funny. Just enjoying the everythingness of it.

### 5. God experiences us; God would rather love than hate

**Thomas:** I think what you've described is the love God feels with us. One thing I've said over and over is that God experiences us as we relate in the world, and that this is a very important principle for what we do with each other and how we speak and relate. You've described love of another person as a particular kind of "being of." The waves and the palm trees and the coconuts are a different experience, but you're "of" them also. That was my point: God created us to have that fullness of the experience of relationship, and that was the point of creating life.

One of the things we batted back and forth last time was good and evil, and that there were things God didn't want to experience. You said you tried hate and didn't like it. You hate hate because you don't like that experience. You chose love; hate didn't suit you. So you had a preference for love, and that is an example of how God feels about the experience of being with us. He would rather love us than hate us.

### 6. Michael does not hate hate; understanding the one who hates

**Michael:** I don't hate hate, because then I'm hating. It's antithetical for me to hate hate. Instead, and this is the vital thing in my practice of love, if somebody is doing something truly hateful (you can think of endless examples), instead of hating him or even the action, I have two ears and one mouth. My first job is to understand why this person thinks it's okay to do. If I can grasp that, which often takes quite a set of contortions, then perhaps I can address that person on his own terms, in ways that help him see that there's a more fulfilling way to achieve the objectives he seeks through hatred.

I forget who said it: the church is not a museum for saints; it's a hospital for sinners. I think it was Mother Teresa. In that way, no, I don't hate hate. It is the sinner who is hostile who might use my hospital. Hate is not the opposite of love. Both are a person experiencing life and interpreting it a certain way, for understandable reasons.

My mom used to beat us very regularly, because she learned that's how parents raise their kids. She was beaten herself, with stick, belt, the whole schmear. I didn't hate her for it. I felt sorry for her, even though I was the one getting beaten. It was like, "How sad for you to see through this lens." Sometimes I can help that person, and sometimes I can't. Do what you can with what you've got.
> *Editor's note:* The saying is commonly attributed to Abigail Van Buren ("Dear Abby") and to various preachers; no reliable source ties it to Mother Teresa.


### 7. Does God hate anything?

**Michael:** Now, does God hate anything?

**Thomas:** Yes. It's scriptural: "I hate evil."
> *Editor's note:* The closest verses are *"The fear of the LORD is to hate evil: pride, and arrogancy, and the evil way, and the froward mouth, do I hate"* **(Proverbs 8:13)**, spoken by Wisdom, and *"Ye that love the LORD, hate evil"* **(Psalm 97:10)**. Verses that say the LORD Himself hates include **(Proverbs 6:16–19)**, **(Psalm 5:5)**, **(Psalm 11:5)**, **(Isaiah 61:8)** and **(Zechariah 8:17)**.

**Michael:** He takes a different point of view from me, then. He invented evil, so that means He invented something He hates.

**Thomas:** He defined evil. He didn't invent it. God didn't invent evil; God defined evil.

**Michael:** Was it here before He made anything?

**Thomas:** No. As we went through last time, it started with nothing. There was God; God created the Son; the Son created everything that was manifest. And the things that were not of His nature, He hates.
> *Editor's note:* The historic creeds hold that the Son is eternally "begotten, not made" (Nicene Creed, 325/381). Thomas's phrase "God created the Son" is his own framing, which he develops later in the meeting.


### 8. Michael as "guidance counselor for God": hate is learned

**Michael:** Boy, if I were the guidance counselor for God, I'd say: if you really love love, then instead of hating them, converse with them. Help them see; help them really get love. I know people who hate and who do horrible things, but if I know them at all, I usually know how they got there. It's usually because they learned from experience: this is what we do.

But I'm not God, and He can go ahead and hate. For me, those are people I can grow by, because I can grow, by effort, an understanding of how to actually affect things positively. If I stick only with people of my own ilk, where's the growth? How have I helped? We're just singing from the same hymnal. But others, like the Salvation Army, are trying to help the people on the street. They're not going for the ones who believe the same; they're trying to help people who need, as they would say, God's Word.

### 9. God's play: letting people see what isn't working

**Thomas:** Let's look at God from the frame you just described. You said, help them see that what they're doing isn't working. Perhaps this entire play of life is God helping people see that what they're doing isn't working. When you hate, you pretty much get back what you give; what goes around comes around. People find out what that feels like, and God is helping them by letting them know the outcome of what they do.

You said you see a difference between the things people hate and the things they love. That is what God is doing by showing the fruit of the Spirit and the fruit of hate through the experience of life. The whole world operates the way you're describing. You said this is a way of helping people grow, and God is helping people grow into people who love Him, by seeing that when you do this, you get that. There is a fruit and an observation, and people grow, because God desires that none be lost. This is a way of letting people be free and letting them grow, so that they can be of His nature and choose Him.
> *Editor's note:* *"The Lord is not slack concerning his promise… not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance"* **(2 Peter 3:9)**; compare **(Matthew 18:14)**. The fruit of the Spirit is listed in **(Galatians 5:22–23)**.


### 10. God defined hate; a world of possibility needs a distinction

**Michael:** He chose to have hate in His heart. He didn't have to.

**Thomas:** No. What He chose was to define that which was hate. He said, "These are the things I do not want in my heart, do not want in my presence. This is something I hate." The definition of hate is that which He does not want in His presence, in His world.

The world has possibilities in it, many configurations. If you tried to separate out the world and make one with no possibility of hate in it, you wouldn't have a world with love in it, because there would be no comparison. If you create a world with possibilities, where any atom can move any way, any neuron can fire any way, any person can say anything, pick up anything and do anything or nothing, and God defines what is good and what is not, then He would say: you can pick up that rock and chisel the Pietà out of it, or you can pick up that rock and hit your neighbor on the head with it. He would say, "I hate the one and love the other."

If God had not made the distinction, it would simply be: "Do I hit somebody today, or make a beautiful statue? I don't know. They're all the same. Whatever." You have made a distinction too. You have defined the person who has hate in his heart, tried to understand him, and tried to help him grow into a person who, instead of acting out those ways, acts out the ways of loving people. You looked at the one and the other and said, "This way is better." That's the same thing God did.

### 11. The Father and the Son: "with God" and "was God"

**Michael:** In rock climbing you keep three limbs on the wall and try a new hold. So we have a nice baseline with the "God is everything" thing, and then a second step: duality, where there's God and other, or, as you say, He made the Son, something that's not God.

**Thomas:** No, it was God. The Father and the Son are one, but there's an aspect of God that God created. He said, "You are the Word, you are the Son, you are the other, you are the second, and your job is to create everything." We saw that in John 1: the Father and Son were together, and the Son was given the job of creating everything. Anything that was made was made by the Son.

**Michael:** I'm still trying to get this picture. Say a circle is the everything. Then, like a yin-yang, we divide it in two, and God and the Son are both part of the whole. Am I missing it?

**Thomas:** They're part of the whole. They both are the whole, and they are part of the whole.

**Michael:** Then, to follow your model, the Son makes something that's not the yin-yang: an other, a universe, a world. Am I on, or did I miss it?

**Thomas:** There really isn't anything that is not God, ever, because we start out with everything. You can't make something out of everything that's other than everything. All you can say is that the things that are made are not aware of the whole. I think that's all we can say about the Son too. Jesus said the Father has told Him everything and He has done the Father's will, and He said, "The Father and I are one." So both are God. John 1 says, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." So we have both "with" and "was."

That is a contradiction, and it has puzzled theologians forever. They say, "I don't know; let's make something up," and everybody fights about it because they don't understand it, because it's mutually exclusive. People don't get it because you can't get it; the definitions don't allow it. If "with" means other, then being together is not the same as being apart. So that verse is a contradiction, and nobody has any idea what it actually means, but it's what it says. We have to say there is a Father and there is a Son, and they are the same thing, but they're different.

The only way I can reconcile it is to say the Son is everything, but in some way He has chosen to be unaware of the point of view of the Father, to separate Himself and forget that portion of the full Godness that the Son is. He said, "I will choose not to look out of the Father's eyes. I will look out of my eyes, and I will let the Father look out of His." They are the same thing, but they choose not to look out of each other's eyes completely.
> *Editor's note:* **(John 1:1–3)**; *"I and my Father are one"* **(John 10:30)**; *"my Father is greater than I"* **(John 14:28)**; *"there is none good but one, that is, God"* **(Mark 10:18)**.


### 12. Michael's two models: "I'm with her" and the nested yin-yang

**Michael:** Two models come to mind; tell me if either matches. One: when someone who's been married a while says, "I'm with her," it doesn't mean they came to the play together. It means he's with her in a unified, marriage-vow kind of way. Doesn't have to be the vow, but that kind. That's also what I meant by "I of you." How can you be both "with" and "of" at the same time? Easy. Think of any two people closely related. In ways, you're of me, you're in me.

The other model: the circle I gave you at the start, where God and Jesus are part of the same circle. What if that original circle is God-Jesus, and now we break it in two again: God-Jesus on one side of the yin-yang, and things on the other. They're both part of the whole, but now you can at least make a distinction for the purpose of conversation.

**Thomas:** We're trying to describe something that has contradiction in it, something mutually exclusive, so any model we make will be incomplete. But for the purpose of conversation, it's as good as anything. The way I see it, the Father is all of all: the circle with no yin-yang on it. Then we superimpose the Son on it, like speckles, and the speckles completely fill up the circle. Father and Son are completely intertwined. What's the difference between the speckles that fill the whole circle and the whole circle? We call one the Father and one the Son, and both have the same thing.

I make the distinction only because that's how it shows up in the Bible. Jesus talks about doing the Father's will, praying to the Father; the Father is greater than I; there is none good but the Father. He makes Himself separate. And John 1 says, in the same sentence, that the Word was with God and was God. Your yin-yang, with God and God-Jesus on one half and everything created on the other, is a good conversational icon. We just need to recognize that we're talking with symbols that don't fully match the reality.

### 13. Michael: suspect what you are surest of

**Michael:** As Mark Twain said, "It ain't what you don't know that gets you in trouble; it's what you know for sure that just ain't so." No matter what I claim to know, the more strongly I claim it, the more I suspect it, because then I have a bigger premise to question. Look through history: the great inventions that transformed civilizations often questioned the most roundly accepted truths. "If God had meant man to fly, He would have given him wings," so airplanes are an impossibility. "Look out there; the world is flat; anyone who looks can see it." Another Twain: "Whenever you find yourself on the side of the majority, it's time to pause and reflect" — even the majority of voices in my own head. Every time I get close to "that's a universal truth," I say, let's poke at it and see how universal it is.
> *Editor's note:* The first saying is widely attributed to Twain, but no source in his writings has been found; similar lines appear in Josh Billings. The second is genuine Twain, from his 1904 notebook.


### 14. Duality lets us choose any emotion; Michael differs with Thomas's God

**Michael:** Now, the issue of hate. We've got God-Jesus, or God, and things being separate. Me and any thing or person in my life are separate in some ways and together in others. I'm part of a family and an ecosystem. I'm not separate from it; I'm a viable contributor to it, even if it's just the out-breath of carbon dioxide. So I am of it, but separate from it too. Once I've established an element of duality, I can have any emotion I want toward it, or none, and I get to choose. I choose to embrace it. Even the things I would hate, I say, "Oh no, this is an area for investigation."

God, in your model, has a bigger role. Let's stick with the parts that are the same first. Having created an other, God can choose any emotion at all toward it. And if I read you right, God chooses adversarial positions that make Him love some things and hate others. Am I off on that?

**Thomas:** No, that's true. He says, "These are the things I choose to embrace and hold close and pull into me and be in close relationship with, and these are the things I push away: I don't want to be in relationship with that." He embraces some and doesn't embrace others, and it's a choice.

### 15. Michael's alternative: understand instead of reject

**Michael:** That's where I differ with that version of God, for myself. I accept and respect that viewpoint, but here's how I do it differently. The things I disagree with, instead of rejecting and hating them, which is a dynamic I think is counterproductive to my understanding of the world, I try to learn from. What would make this person do that? I have to drop my presumptions and biases, if I really want to get it, and take on that person's suppositions, really from his heart, to understand him well enough to have a positive impact. That happens more rarely than I'd like, but I've seen it: I can help someone see in ways that encourage the diminution of the egregious behavior.

Often, saying "Don't do that" makes people do the opposite: "Don't tell me what to do." When I was living on the street, a guy said, "That man will split my head if I take a loaf of his bread." But I befriended him, and he split five with me. Not in the sense of capitalistically finding five loaves, but if I enjoin someone, the whole panoply of better things may emerge, including his desire to end such behavior. More than ending it, because I'm not putting a thumb on it and squishing it; I'm enjoining it to be on my team. "Team" is the wrong word, but you know. I don't squash every bug with my thumb; many are our friends. The praying mantis eats the aphids that kill our plants.

God, in your model, instead of seeing distasteful behavior and seeking to understand it, takes an adversarial position: "That is bad; I feel what I feel about it, and I will act on that feeling." I feel like Mr. Rogers: "We all feel mad sometimes when things seem against us. And it's important, children, not to act on those feelings until we find out what we really want." I want to say that to God. But He's God, so He can do what He wants. If He wants to play the vengeance, adversarial game, as author of the whole darn shtick He certainly can. But why? Because it fulfills Him.

### 16. God as Author: the Twilight Zone, and the way of the world

**Michael:** My favorite Twilight Zone — I'll keep it to under a minute. The protagonist is an author who has the power to create a character with his tape recorder, and the character comes alive in front of him. That's what authors do, God included. He makes a beautiful woman, or whatever he wants. It's 1950s TV, nothing racy. At the end, Rod Serling, the narrator, comes on to say, "Of course, we know this doesn't happen in real life; this is just an author creating things." And the author says, "Oh no, Rod," picks up a tape labeled "Rod Serling," throws it in the fire, and Rod Serling disappears. He's in complete control of the Twilight Zone.

God is that guy. He can throw any tape in the fire; He can do anything, because He's writing the whole script. He can have hatred, He can have the opportunity for hatred, He can define the things He finds hateful. Personally, if I were writing the script, I don't see the value in that, but He did. He even chose to define things He found hateful, where I don't know that I'd go that far. Even murder has a place, if, say, it's the murder of a deadly bacterium. But that's the way He wants to play. He's the author.
> *Editor's note:* The episode is "A World of His Own" (Season 1, 1960). The playwright Gregory West (Keenan Wynn) creates people by dictating descriptions into a tape recorder, and at the end burns Serling's tape.

There's a Grimm's fairy tale. I was an avid reader of myths and fairy tales at about ten, and one stopped all my reading for a week. It's a one-page story called "Cat and Mouse in Partnership." They make a deal to save the cream for winter, but the cat has been cheating and eating it. The mouse figures it out and says, "Aha! You've been stealing what we were saving for the winter!" The cat says, "Say one more word and I'll eat you too." The mouse can't stop the sentence already in progress, and — chomp. The cat eats his partner. The last line, which stopped my reading, was: "And that, dear children, is the way of the world."

**Charlie:** That's pretty spooky.

**Michael:** I'd had fifty straight happily-ever-afters. Whoa: is that the way of the world? By that point I'd heard of Hitler and all kinds of other stuff. I'd better realign the way I'm looking at this happily-ever-after stuff I've been feeding myself and figure out what to do about it, if that's the way of the world. Anyway, I've been talking too long.
> *Editor's note:* In the Grimm tale the cat and mouse save a pot of fat, not cream, and the closing line is *"Verily, that is the way of the world."*


### 17. Thomas: what Michael advocates is what God does

**Thomas:** Your standard is that you accept everything, appreciate it all, and let it play out. I'd propose that what you've described is what God has done. When we look at the world, we very seldom see the big thumb come out of the sky and squash the bad guy. What you love and advocate for as who God should be is actually who God is. The only thing I've added is that He defined the one and defined the other.

You've defined things too. You've said, "The way these people act doesn't work well for them. I'm going to let them play it out, try to help them grow out of it and understand who they are. I'm going to be the person who appreciates everything, but I recognize that some things work well and feel good and other things don't." I'd propose that's exactly the play we see unfolding before our eyes.

What we see in addition, behind the scenes, is a God who is grieved by anyone who does the things that cause pain and hurt. He hates the hate, the hurt, the pain, the death. But all these things had to exist, or there was no play. If there were no transformation, there would be no play. He wants everyone to come into the Mother Teresa kind of world you described as the best. I believe you've described the world God describes as the best too, so it sounds like you and He are on the same page. But you're saying God shouldn't feel what He feels, or act the way He acts, or define the way He defines.

### 18. Armond: Godly love and performance-based love

**Armond:** I have a question for you too, Michael. As I understand it, you operate from a perspective of Godly love; that's how I'd define it. What I'd offer is that you don't always operate in Godly love. In some parts of your life you operate in a performance-based love. And God gets to operate in that as well.

You're a few years older than me, and you speak of your wife and your decades-long relationship. I'm sure she's not the only person you've known that long. You met other people back then, but you don't hold the same kind of love for them. Those are two different kinds of love. You're still operating in love, but one is definitely performance-based. I think God operates in that performance base as well. Help me understand: is that something you recognize?

### 19. Michael: God is the Author; Frankl's freedom; the fog at Big Sur

**Michael:** Everything you said is spot on, except one thing. God differs from me in a very important feature: He's the Author. I don't get to write this script. I play a big role in the script of my life, but deep down I'm bound to this planet and all kinds of other things. God can erase the board and write everything. Lest I mislead myself into thinking I have that kind of power, I don't. That's a huge difference.

What we do share is this. Viktor Frankl, the famous psychotherapist, said that when the Nazis tortured him for four years in the prison camp, his revelation came when he figured out that although they had the ultimate liberty to do anything they wanted with him, which he didn't have, he had a freedom they didn't have: to make of it what he would. I can choose to make of this life what I will. My life has had its deep hardships and its joys, but it's mine to make of them what I will.

The first time I rode a three-speed bike from LA to Berkeley to go to school, it took eleven days, and I was looking forward to Big Sur the whole way. On that day of the journey it was totally fogged in. I could barely see the road. I thought, "All right. This long-awaited view of Big Sur is in a fog. I'll just appreciate that, because it's all I've got." God gets to rewrite the script. Okay, good for you, fella.
> *Editor's note:* Frankl was held in Nazi camps from September 1942 to April 1945, about two and a half years. The idea is from *Man's Search for Meaning*: everything can be taken from a man but one thing, the last of the human freedoms, to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances.


### 20. Michael: love without requirements; the rattlesnake; the lion

**Michael:** So far, so good, until we get to the part about biases. If I had that authorship role, I wouldn't put that in my script.
