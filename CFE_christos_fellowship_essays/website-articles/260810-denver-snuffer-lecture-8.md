# 260810 - Denver Snuffer - Lecture 8

# An Academic Assessment of Denver Snuffer’s 500th-Anniversary Lecture 8


## Historical Accuracy and Biblical Consistency


**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 10, 2026**


[**Source:**](https://restorationarchives.com/blog/index.php/2020/08/18/8th-address-to-christians/) Denver Snuffer Jr., eighth and final lecture of the series delivered to Christian audiences on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of the Protestant Reformation; per the transcript’s header, delivered May 18, 2019, in Montgomery, Alabama — establishing that the series as compiled spans 2017 (the anniversary trio) through 2019. The transcript carries parenthetical cross-references to the movement’s own Restoration Edition scriptures (“RE”), an editorial artifact of the published transcript noted for the record. The series and its supporting materials are published at https://www.christianreformation500years.info and at https://www.learnofchrist.org. Readers are encouraged to hear or read the lecture in full. **Method:** As in the assessments of Lectures 1–7, this essay is an academic evaluation only, on two axes: (1) **historical accuracy** against the documented record and (2) **biblical consistency** against the text of Scripture (King James Version), with the text-reportage check retained. Autobiographical matter is recorded as testimony where not independently checkable. Claims already assessed in earlier essays are cross-referenced rather than re-argued. A closing section takes stock of the series as a whole, this being its final installment.


---


## I. Claims Concerning Apostolic Diversity


### 1. The opening frame


**Claims as delivered.** Religion should not be a source of friction; the Author of the religion commands love of one another; with Christ in common, Christians ought to de-emphasize dissimilarities.


**Biblical assessment.** Consistent (John 13:34–35; Romans 14:1–13; Ephesians 4:2–3). No issue.


### 2. Matthean, Pauline, and Petrine Christianities; diffusion by design


**Claims as delivered.** Christianity in the immediate aftermath had various kinds — Matthean, Pauline, Petrine — the Petrine gaining the broadest sweep and issuing in the Catholic Church; Christianity started diffused, not centralized, “almost as if” Christ wanted basic things held in common and the rest left open.


**Assessment.** The taxonomy reprises the apostolic-communities heuristic assessed at Lecture 2 §13 (a contested scholarly construct presented as description) and the Petrine-to-Rome genealogy assessed there; the diffusion-versus-centralization claim carries the Lecture 7 §6 finding, where the strong form (“no attempt at a unified message”) was answered from Acts 2:42, Acts 15, Galatians 2:2–9, and 1 Corinthians 1:12–13 and 15:11. It is noted that this lecture’s form is more hedged (“almost as if what Christ wanted”), and that its positive content — a common core with liberty beyond it — is itself an old Christian maxim (the *in necessariis unitas* formula of seventeenth-century irenicism) with biblical resonance (Romans 14; Acts 15:28, “no greater burden than these necessary things”). The hedged form is compatible with the record; the historical description underneath it retains the prior findings.


### 3. John’s gospel as retrospective understanding


**Claims as delivered.** John makes clear the disciples were not up to speed during the ministry — Christ would say things they did not understand; only after the resurrection, looking back, did they comprehend, and the gospel is written from that retrospective vantage.


**Biblical assessment.** Accurate, and verified from John’s own explicit statements: “When therefore he was risen from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this unto them; and they believed” (John 2:22); “These things understood not his disciples at the first: but when Jesus was glorified, **then remembered they** that these things were written of him” (12:16); “For as yet they knew not the scripture, that he must rise again from the dead” (20:9); with the promised mechanism stated in advance — “the Holy Ghost… shall teach you all things, and **bring all things to your remembrance**” (14:26). This is among the most exactly supported historical-critical claims in the series and is recorded as such.


### 4. The Damascus road: the pricks, “Cornelius,” and the scales


**Claims as delivered.** Saul, commissioned to bring Christians to justice, is interrupted; “it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks” — the goad that wounds the kicking animal; Saul is told to go to **Cornelius**; he goes to Cornelius, is baptized, and scales fall from his eyes; “scales” is a great English translation because it implies both the opaque covering and *judgment* — scales to be put aright.


**Biblical assessment.** The pricks explanation is sound (the ox-goad; the proverb’s sense as delivered is standard). Two corrections. (a) The man is **Ananias**, not Cornelius: “And there was a certain disciple at Damascus, named Ananias” (Acts 9:10–18), who lays hands on Saul and baptizes him; Cornelius is the centurion of Caesarea whose conversion belongs to **Peter’s** ministry a chapter later (Acts 10). The names are confused twice. (b) The judgment-scales point is wordplay available only in English: the Greek of Acts 9:18 is *lepides* — flakes, as of a fish’s scales — a word with no connection to weighing or judgment; the homonymy of English “scales” (fish-covering / balance) is an accident of translation, so the “implies judgment” reading is a homiletic pun without basis in the text’s language. Recorded as rhetorical ornament, not exegesis.


### 5. Peter and Paul “never on the same page”


**Claims as delivered.** Paul withstood Peter to his face because he was to be blamed (Galatians 2:11); therefore one could be authentically Christian as Paul and authentically Christian as Peter “and the two of you absolutely not agree on much of anything”; Christ thus set up a Christianity of necessary diversity and tolerance.


**Biblical assessment.** The quotation is accurate; the generalization is contradicted by the very chapter quoted and by the wider record. In Galatians 2 itself, *before* the Antioch incident, Peter, James, and John examined Paul’s gospel and “gave to me and Barnabas the right hands of fellowship” on a **common** gospel with a divided mission field (2:7–9), Paul having submitted his message “lest by any means I should run, or had run, in vain” (2:2). The Antioch confrontation (2:11–14) concerned **conduct**, not doctrine: Paul’s charge is *dissimulation* — hypocrisy — that Peter, “before that certain came from James, did eat with the Gentiles” and then withdrew, walking “not uprightly according to the truth of the gospel” **which both men held**; the accusation of hypocrisy presupposes the shared conviction Peter failed to act on. Paul’s own unity formula covers them both — “whether it were I or they, so we preach, and so ye believed” (1 Corinthians 15:11) — and Peter’s final canonical word on Paul is fraternal endorsement: “our beloved brother Paul also according to the wisdom given unto him hath written unto you… as also in all his epistles,” ranking them with “the other scriptures” (2 Peter 3:15–16). “Absolutely not agree on much of anything” is falsified by the documents cited for it. The diversity-and-tolerance conclusion survives in the weaker form the texts do support — real difference of emphasis, mission, and temperament within one confessed gospel. A minor attribution note: the “unity of faith” phrase the lecture assigns to Peter’s writing is Paul’s (Ephesians 4:13); Peter’s parallel is “be ye all of one mind” (1 Peter 3:8).

## II. Money and Ministry


### 6. Unpaid ministry; the clergy’s financial stake as the root of interdenominational hostility


**Claims as delivered.** Money must be taken out of religion; faith requires sacrifice; no one should be paid for religious service; the speaker’s fellowships collect tithes in small groups and spend them on needs within the group, with no one paid; the reason Catholic priests are hostile to Lutherans, Lutherans to Methodists, Methodists to Baptists, and Baptists to the Church of Christ is that the clergy have a financial stake in their denomination’s survival; take the money out of Christianity and most ministers would go into politics.


**Assessment.** The no-compensation doctrine carries the standing finding of Lectures 1 §16 and 2 §11: as a description of apostolic Christianity it is contradicted by Luke 10:7, 1 Corinthians 9:7–14 (“even so hath the Lord ordained that they which preach the gospel should live of the gospel”), Galatians 6:6, 1 Timothy 5:17–18, and Philippians 4:15–18 — Paul’s personal waiver presupposing the right; as a voluntary discipline of the speaker’s fellowship it is unobjectionable and consistent with Paul’s own tentmaking practice. The faith-requires-sacrifice axiom carries the Lecture 3 §23 note (its necessary-condition form being the doctrine of the sixth Lecture on Faith, a Restoration source). The *motive* claim — that clerical hostility across denominations is caused by financial stake — is a universal attribution of interior motive to entire professions, structurally parallel to the universal-corruption claims assessed at Lecture 1 §15: unverifiable in principle, and against it stand both the biblical caution on judging hearts (1 Corinthians 4:5) and the documented record of salaried clergy leading ecumenical rapprochement (the modern Lutheran–Catholic and Methodist–Catholic dialogues were conducted almost entirely by compensated clergy and theologians). A financial-interest *pressure* on institutions is a defensible sociological observation; “the reason… is because” as a total explanation is not. The Methodist-chaplain anecdote (dual pensions) is unremarkable as fact and carries no probative weight beyond the pressure observation.


### 7. “The majority of Christian ministers do not have faith”


**Claim as delivered.** Polls of Christian ministers asking whether they believe Jesus Christ is the Son of God who was resurrected show that the majority do not have faith — what they have is a career they cannot abandon.


**Historical assessment.** Unsupported as stated. Surveys of clergy belief exist, and some yield striking findings — studies and polls have found *substantial minorities* of clergy in particular, principally liberal-Protestant and European, bodies doubting specific doctrines (bodily resurrection among them), and qualitative work has documented unbelieving clergy who remain in post (the phenomenon is real and studied). But no documented survey known to this assessment finds that **a majority of Christian ministers** — across denominations, including the evangelical, Catholic, Orthodox, and global-South clergy who constitute most of the world’s ministry — disbelieve the resurrection or “do not have faith.” The claim generalizes the most dramatic minority findings from the most skeptical subpopulations into a universal about the profession. The pastoral kernel (careerism is a real spiritual hazard; John 10:12–13’s hireling; Philippians 1:15–17’s mixed motives) is biblically attested; the statistical claim is not sustained by the polling record it invokes.

## III. Claims Concerning Church History


### 8. Constantine, “324 AD,” and the arrest of the bishops, again


**Claims as delivered.** Between the first generation and 324 AD, when Constantine determined it was a mistake to have made Christianity the religion of Rome, factions were killing one another; he summoned the bishops to Nicaea, put them under arrest, and told them they could not leave until they reached agreement; they nearly achieved unanimity; a handful who would not agree were exiled from Rome; thus a state religion agreed upon; the ante-Nicene fathers preserve first-generation teachings that begin to evaporate after 324; years of conflict and killing followed before a stable orthodoxy.


**Historical assessment.** The paragraph reprises, with variations, the cluster corrected at Lectures 2 §§16–17, 3 §6, and 7 §7, and those findings govern: pre-Nicene Christians-killing-Christians is anachronistic (the coercive power ran the other way until 313; Priscillian, 385, is the first execution at Christian instigation); Constantine legalized and favored but did not make Christianity the state religion (Theodosius, 380); and the arrest/detention of the bishops is attested in no source — the documented picture is imperial summons with transport and hospitality, confessor attendees, weeks of open dispute, and coercion arriving only *after* the decision. Three details in this telling are separately noted. (a) The date “324” conflates Constantine’s attainment of sole rule (324) with the council (325). (b) The exile kernel is genuine and here rendered nearly right: Arius and two Libyan bishops (Theonas and Secundus) who refused subscription were exiled — “a handful… exiled” is accurate; “exiled from Rome” is a slip of place (the council sat at Nicaea in Bithynia and the exiles were imperial banishments, not expulsions from Rome). (c) The evaporation claim has a defensible instance the lecture does not name — ante-Nicene chiliasm (the millenarian expectation common in Justin, Irenaeus, and Tertullian) did recede in later orthodoxy — and the post-Nicene “years of conflict and killing” is, unlike the pre-Nicene version, historically correct (the Arian controversies, Donatist violence, and imperial enforcement of the fourth century). Where the lecture places the violence *after* Constantine it matches the record; where before, it does not.


### 9. Proto-orthodoxy as the Petrine, priestly view; the schism of 1054


**Claims as delivered.** Among the pre-324 Christianities, the one later called proto-orthodox — so named in hindsight because it won — was the Petrine view emphasizing priestly authority and a priestly intervenor; it held sway until the East–West split at about 1000 AD between two bishops vying for primacy; the Orthodox churches preserved teachings dropped in the West.


**Assessment.** The hindsight-labeling point is the Bauer-frame observation assessed at Lecture 2 §16, with the counterweight recorded there (the *regula fidei* attested across provinces before any victory). The priestly-intervenor characterization is anachronistic for the second century but has genuine third-century footing (Cyprian’s sacerdotal episcopate), so as a description of the *later* pre-Nicene period it is defensible. The schism datum is serviceable: 1054 for “about a thousand”; “two bishops vying for primacy” compresses the actors (the mutual excommunications were exchanged by the papal legate Humbert and Patriarch Michael Cerularius, over primacy together with the filioque and liturgical disputes) but names the sees correctly. The Eastern-preservation claim was granted at Lecture 2 §13 (theosis as the standing example). Substantially fair with the noted compressions.


### 10. Luther: the priest-bound universe, the Romans citation, and the cohort


**Claims as delivered.** Luther, as devout as any man in the Catholic faith, saw signs of God’s intervention in his life; visiting Rome, he was horrified by corruption; in his universe salvation required a priest, and his dilemma was whether Christianity apart from the clergy was even possible; reading **in the book of Romans** the passage that says it is by grace you are saved, through the instrumentality of faith, he conceived that salvation might be possible apart from the Catholic clergy, and founded the Protestant movement; immediately, John Calvin, John Wesley, Zwingli, and others said Luther got one thing right but not everything, and the movement divided into dozens, hundreds, and now thousands of denominations.


**Historical assessment.** The devotion, the providential-signs element (the 1505 thunderstorm vow to St. Anne belongs to the tradition), the 1510–11 Rome journey and disillusionment (as Luther later recounted it), and the immediate fissiparation of Protestantism are all substantially accurate; the salvation-requires-a-priest framing is a fair popularization of the late-medieval penitential system Luther inhabited. Two corrections. (a) *The citation:* “by grace are ye saved through faith” is **Ephesians 2:8**, not Romans; Luther’s documented breakthrough texts were Romans 1:17 (“the just shall live by faith”) and the argument of Romans 3 (“justified by faith without the deeds of the law,” 3:28). The formula quoted and the book named do not match; either the verse or the epistle must be corrected. (b) *The cohort:* placing **John Wesley** among the immediate respondents to Luther repeats, in aggravated form, the cohort error corrected at Lecture 7 §9 — Zwingli was Luther’s contemporary and Calvin the next generation, but Wesley (1703–1791) postdates Luther by two centuries; the Methodist movement belongs to the eighteenth-century Evangelical Revival, not the Reformation generation. The denominational-multiplication endpoint carries the counting qualification of Lecture 1 §8.


### 11. The courtroom-witness argument and the gospel variations


**Claims as delivered.** Truthful witnesses agree in broad brush and disagree in details; if they agree on all details, someone is lying; the four gospels arose in a single generation and agree on the massive truth — Son of God, sacrificial lamb, rejected, died, resurrected, ascended — while differing in details: only Matthew has the Sermon on the Mount; some mention feeding five thousand, some mention feeding **seven thousand**, and some mention both; the witnesses’ transformation from cowering to willing martyrdom shows something actually happened.


**Biblical assessment.** The argumentative frame is sound and was affirmed at Lectures 1 §4 and 2 §16 (perspectival variation as a mark of independent testimony; the transformation argument well grounded in Acts and 1 Corinthians 15). Details: “only Matthew has the Sermon on the Mount” is accurate as to the Sermon as such (Luke’s Sermon on the Plain, Luke 6:17–49, is the shorter parallel); “a single generation” is defensible on conventional datings for the synoptics and stretched for John (c. 90s), within the tolerance of the argument. One figure is wrong: the second feeding is of **four thousand** (Matthew 15:38; Mark 8:9 — “seven” belongs to the loaves and the baskets, Matthew 15:34, 37, likely the source of the slip); the feeding of the five thousand appears in all four gospels, and Matthew and Mark alone record both feedings — so the structure of the point (some one, some both) survives on corrected numbers.


### 12. The popes of the speaker’s youth


**Claims as delivered.** In the speaker’s boyhood the pope “was Pope John VI… seemed like a decent enough chap”; the first pope to impress him was John Paul I, a fan of Mark Twain — “the greatest pope that ever lived.”


**Historical assessment.** “Pope John VI” reigned AD 701–705; the popes of the speaker’s youth (b. 1953) were Pius XII, **John XXIII** (1958–63), and Paul VI (1963–78) — the name as delivered conflates John XXIII and Paul VI into a nonexistent contemporary. The John Paul I detail, by contrast, checks out precisely: Albino Luciani (reigned 33 days, August–September 1978) published *Illustrissimi* (1976), a collection of open letters to historical and literary figures that includes a letter to **Mark Twain** — the Twain fandom is documented. One error and one verified curiosity in the same breath; the superlative is opinion, outside the axes.

## IV. Holiness, Francis, and the Sermon on the Mount


### 13. Mother Teresa and St. Francis; the Franciscan retelling’s additions


**Claims as delivered.** Mother Teresa and Francis stand as evidence that Catholic holiness exists; the pope laughed at Francis and said he would grant an order only if Francis returned with twelve men willing to live the Sermon on the Mount; Francis, forbidden by his wealthy father when he decided “to become a priest,” stripped off his clothes, returned them, and came to the clergy poor and naked; when he returned with twelve believers the Franciscans were commissioned; the current pope is named for him; in his last two months Francis reported angels visiting him, which should not be dismissed as a dying body’s frailty.


**Historical assessment.** The core stands as assessed at Lecture 7 §15 (papal hesitation before Innocent III; the company of twelve; the serial coat-giving; deathbed ministration within the hagiographic tradition; the assessment of such claims as tradition-reportage). This telling adds two details requiring correction against the sources. (a) The *bring-me-twelve precondition* — approval withheld until Francis returned with twelve — is an embellishment: in the early lives (Celano; the Legend of the Three Companions; Bonaventure) Francis comes to Innocent III already accompanied by his eleven companions, the hesitation and approval occurring in that one embassy (with the tradition’s dream of the tottering Lateran); no source makes a twelve-man recruitment the pope’s stated condition. (b) Francis did not decide “to become a priest” and never became one: he remained a deacon throughout his life, by his own humility — a fixed datum of the tradition; the renunciation before his father (the disrobing in the bishop’s court of Assisi, 1206, which is genuinely documented and accurately told) concerned his embrace of religious poverty, not ordination. The Pope Francis naming (2013) is accurate. The Mother Teresa appeal is evaluative and consonant with the lecture’s own thesis that holiness persists inside the institutions — which, as noted at Lecture 7 §15, functions as a standing counterexample to the series’ total-corruption claims; the tension is carried forward.


### 14. “Do not taste death”; the Sermon on the Mount as intensification


**Claims as delivered.** An expression “found in places some of you would find dubious” says some do not taste death — not that they do not die, but that death is sweet, in the company of those on the other side; all authentic Christians of any denomination find death sweet. Jesus took the law as the standard and raised it: not killing is not enough — anger and malice must go; not committing adultery is not enough — the lustful look must go; the Sermon’s purpose is to make one like Him.


**Assessment.** The dubious-places expression is Doctrine and Covenants 42:46, as identified at Lecture 7 §16, and the winking provenance flag (“places some of you would find dubious”) is recorded as this lecture’s characteristic semi-attribution; the not-die-but-sweet gloss matches the source’s wording and coheres with the Johannine antecedent (John 8:51–52) as previously noted. The Sermon exposition is accurate to Matthew 5:21–22 and 5:27–28 and biblically consistent throughout (the *telos* stated at Matthew 5:48; the imitation principle at 1 Peter 2:21; 1 John 2:6); the all-authentic-Christians-die-sweetly universal is a comfort claim beyond the texts (Scripture records faithful deaths in distress as well as peace, 2 Timothy 4:16 notwithstanding 4:6–8) but is pastoral rather than doctrinal in force. No material issue.


### 15. The parables of authenticity


**Claims as delivered.** Christ looks on the inner person; the ten virgins are good religious people, yet only five enter; the wedding feast’s invited friends do not come, so the invitation goes to the highways and byways, and any who come wearing the wedding garment are welcomed; the Good Samaritan, from a Jewish vantage an apostate, is the illustration of authentic discipleship; it is not what goes into the mouth but what comes out.


**Biblical assessment.** Accurate and consistent throughout: 1 Samuel 16:7 (the heart); Matthew 25:1–13 (the virgins — the all-religious reading is a standard one, all ten being invited attendants with lamps); Matthew 22:1–14 with Luke 14:16–24 (the feast, the byways, the garment — the two parables are blended, a common homiletic conflation, noted without objection since each element is genuine to its text); Luke 10:25–37 (the Samaritan, whose religiously suspect status to the lawyer’s ears is exactly the parable’s edge); Matthew 15:11 (the mouth). The application — that denominational affiliation is not Christ’s criterion — is consistent with the passages used and with the series’ own better moments (Lecture 2 §27); the assessment records it as the lecture’s soundest extended section.

## V. Claims Concerning Mormon History


### 16. The self-assessment and the Sunstone exchange


**Claims as delivered.** The speaker knows Mormon history better than anyone alive, having read every recorded word of Joseph Smith; at the Sunstone Symposium he presented a paper on Brigham Young’s megalomaniacal presiding (late 1840s–1850s), including murders on Young’s watch and Young’s self-regard as an actual king from 1847 until deposed as territorial governor in 1857; the respondent, Thomas Alexander, “the Dean of Mormon History,” rebutted with quotations from 1860 and 1870, which the speaker dismantled in five minutes, since post-deposition statements cannot evidence pre-deposition self-conception.


**Assessment.** The superlative self-assessment is unfalsifiable and recorded without adjudication. The verifiable elements check out as to existence: the Sunstone Symposium is accurately characterized (an independent Mormon-studies forum of shifting temper); Thomas G. Alexander is a genuinely eminent Latter-day Saint historian and did respond to a Snuffer Sunstone paper; the exchange occurred. The substantive claims about Young divide as at prior lectures between documented anchors and contested characterization: documented — the theocratic governance of early Utah, the Council of Fifty’s royal theology (within which Joseph Smith had been anointed king in 1844), Young’s replacement as territorial governor in the Utah War settlement (Alfred Cumming appointed 1857, installed 1858), and the gravest violence of the period falling on his watch (Mountain Meadows, September 1857); contested — Young’s personal self-conception as “an actual king,” the attribution of the era’s murders to his presiding, and the degree of his responsibility for Mountain Meadows, all of which remain live disputes in the professional literature. The methodological point scored against the rebuttal (later statements cannot straightforwardly evidence earlier self-conception) is fair as method; the five-minute-dismantling verdict is the speaker’s own scorecard, one-sided by nature and recorded as such.


### 17. “The Mormon church is a cult”


**Claim as delivered.** The Mormon church is a cult and not an authentic Christian organization — while authentic Christians can be found among Mormons and in every denomination.


**Assessment.** A classification without a definition: “cult” carries a sociological sense (a high-demand new religious movement), a theological sense (a body deviating from creedal orthodoxy — the sense of Walter Martin’s *Kingdom of the Cults*, the very handbook the speaker reports absorbing in Lecture 5’s autobiography), and a purely polemical sense; the lecture supplies none, so the claim is not assessable on the two axes beyond noting that by the theological definition the speaker’s own movement — sharing the Book of Mormon, continuing revelation, and the rejection of the ecumenical creeds — falls inside the same category, an application the lecture does not address. The accompanying charity (Christians in every denomination, Mormons included) is consistent with the parables section (§15) and is recorded.


### 18. The Nauvoo Legion: “the largest standing Army in the United States”


**Claims as delivered.** In 1844 the largest standing army in the United States — larger than the federal government’s, larger than any state militia — was under Major General Joseph Smith in Nauvoo; he could literally have taken on and defeated the United States Army; instead he disarmed his soldiers, turned the cannon over to Illinois, surrendered to the governor, and three days later was murdered in jail, preferring to die rather than have men die over a religious dispute.


**Historical assessment.** The surrender sequence is substantially accurate: on Governor Ford’s demand the Legion surrendered its state-issued arms (three cannon and roughly two hundred stand of small arms) on June 24, 1844; Smith submitted to custody at Carthage; he was killed June 27 — the pacific framing of the choice is a defensible reading of the documented sequence, and the causation findings of Lectures 1 §12 and 2 §19 (the Expositor chain; the non-Mormon mob) stand alongside it. The size claim does not survive the numbers. The Nauvoo Legion’s enrolled strength in 1844 is commonly estimated at roughly 2,500 (with outer claims to about 5,000 on paper); the United States regular army of 1844 numbered approximately 8,500. The Legion was the largest *municipal militia* in the country and a formidable local force exceeding what Illinois could quickly field against it — which is the true kernel — but “larger than the federal government[‘s army]” is arithmetically false, and “could have defeated the United States Army” is rhetorical. The claim in this maximal form is a fixture of Mormon folklore that the documentary numbers do not support; the accurate statement (largest militia body of its kind; capable of making Carthage’s environs very costly; voluntarily stood down) preserves the lecture’s moral point without the inflation.


### 19. Missouri: the year, the two speeches, Avard, Richmond, and Liberty


**Claims as delivered.** In **1837** Joseph Smith was in Missouri when hostilities broke out; leaders around him were spoiling for a fight; Sidney Rigdon gave a speech threatening a war of extermination — “it’s called the Salt Speech; it was delivered on July the fourth”; Sampson Avard, a Mormon, formed the Danites (named from Dan’s blessing in Genesis 49) and provoked hostilities — burning, stealing cattle and hogs; Joseph, discovering it, demoted Avard to a cook; the Missouri governor turned the extermination language back on the Mormons and expelled them; at Far West, Joseph — holding a defensible position — surrendered and deflated the tension; charged with treason, he was held over solely on the testimony of Avard, the actual perpetrator; he spent six months over winter in an unheated dungeon; he was allowed to escape; from Liberty Jail he composed a letter breathing Christian compassion; the speaker’s book *A Man Without Doubt* frames Smith’s three longest personal compositions — the Liberty Jail letter, the Lectures on Faith, and his history, written because the church historian had stolen the manuscripts; time and again Smith’s worst enemies were Mormons.


**Historical assessment.** The narrative is substantially right in outline with a cluster of checkable slips and two contested attributions. (a) *Year:* the Missouri war is **1838** (Smith relocated to Far West early in 1838; hostilities ran August–November 1838); “1837” is off by one. (b) *The two speeches conflated:* Rigdon’s **Salt Sermon** (June 17, 1838, against the Mormon dissenters, on salt that has lost its savor) and his **Fourth of July Oration** (July 4, 1838, containing the “war of extermination” threat against mobs) are two different speeches; the lecture fuses them, attaching the Salt name to the July date and the extermination content. (c) *The echo:* Governor Boggs’s Extermination Order (October 27, 1838 — Mormons “must be exterminated or driven from the State”) echoing Rigdon’s July language is a genuine and standard historiographical observation; accurate. (d) *Avard and the Danites:* Avard’s leadership of the Danites, the Daviess County depredations (October 1838), his turning state’s evidence, and his role as the pivotal witness at the Richmond preliminary hearing (November 1838) blaming Smith are all documented; the Genesis 49:17 derivation is within the attested naming traditions. Two elements are softer: the *demoted-to-cook* detail is not locatable in the principal sources known to this assessment (Avard, a physician, was removed from military responsibility in the reorganization and excommunicated in March 1839; the cook detail appears to be embellishment or a stray tradition); and *Smith’s total non-involvement* is the maximally exculpatory pole of a genuinely divided historiography — the documented record (including the Joseph Smith Papers’ treatments) supports Smith’s awareness and sanction of the Danite organization’s existence while leaving the authorization of Avard’s depredations disputed; “no one could prove… until Avard” overstates a hearing at which some forty prosecution witnesses appeared, though Avard’s testimony was indeed decisive for the treason holding. (e) *Liberty:* custody ran from the surrender (October 31–November 1, 1838) with confinement in Liberty Jail from December 1, 1838, to the April 1839 removal — roughly five months total, four in Liberty; “six months over a winter” slightly overstates; the dungeon conditions (cold, darkness, foul provisions) are documented; the April 1839 escape during the change of venue, with the guards’ evident connivance, matches “allowed to escape.” (f) *The letter:* the March 1839 Liberty Jail letter is genuine and its celebrated passages (the sources of D&C 121–123) do breathe as described — the characterization is fair, with the note that the same letter also contains sharp imprecations against the Saints’ enemies; selective but not false. (g) *The book’s frame:* *A Man Without Doubt* is accurately described as to its three documents; two qualifications attach — the “stolen” manuscripts refers to John Whitmer, the church historian, *retaining* his manuscript history upon his 1838 excommunication (retention by its custodian, polemically rendered as theft), and the Lectures on Faith’s authorship is contested in the scholarship, the dominant analysis assigning principal composition to **Rigdon** with Smith’s participation — so its listing among Smith’s three longest personal writings states a disputed attribution as fact. (h) The betrayed-by-Mormons refrain carries the split finding of Lectures 1 §12 and 2 §19: dissenter betrayal documented (Avard at Richmond; the Expositor circle at Nauvoo); the killing itself by non-Mormon actors.


### 20. Plural marriage: “Brigham Young introduced the practice”


**Claims as delivered.** Brigham Young introduced plural marriage; Joseph Smith was its ardent opponent; Smith has been falsely portrayed because Young could not introduce the practice without laying it at Smith’s feet; the stories attributed to Smith (private practice, public denial) are inconsistent with his character; the letters between Joseph and Emma show her as his stronger, better-educated, trusted counselor.


**Historical assessment.** This is the lecture’s — and in some respects the series’ — largest single historical claim, and it is presented as fact where the documentary record runs heavily the other way. The evidence attributing the practice’s introduction to Smith in his lifetime includes contemporaneous documents that predate any Utah motive: William Clayton’s Nauvoo journal recording, on **July 12, 1843**, Smith’s dictation of the revelation on celestial and plural marriage (the text later canonized as D&C 132) and Clayton’s own contemporaneous involvement; the **Nauvoo Expositor** of June 7, 1844 — published by dissenters *during Smith’s life* and precipitating the Carthage chain the lecture’s own earlier installments rehearse — whose central charge was precisely the private practice of plural marriage under Smith; sealing and marriage records, and a large body of affidavit and reminiscent testimony from the participants (the corpus underlying the standard scholarly treatments, which document on the order of thirty wives). The modern LDS Church’s own historical essays — issued by the institution the lecture elsewhere accuses of sanitizing its past — *acknowledge* Smith’s plural marriages; that is, on this point the lecture is more exculpatory of Smith than the institution it charges with whitewashing. The contrary case the lecture voices — Smith’s and Emma’s public denials, the Reorganized tradition’s long insistence, and challenges to the Utah-era affidavits as retrospective and interested — is a genuine minority revisionist position with a real evidentiary program, and it is recorded as such; but it must explain away, rather than explain, the contemporaneous Clayton journal and the Expositor. And here the series generates its own internal contradiction: **Lectures 1 and 2 of this series ground Smith’s death in the Expositor affair** — dissenters exposing what they charged was Smith’s secret practice — a narrative unintelligible if the practice did not exist until Young invented it afterward. The claim as delivered is therefore (i) against the preponderance of the contemporaneous documentation, (ii) more lenient toward Smith than the current position of the church the series accuses of dishonesty, and (iii) inconsistent with the causal account of Smith’s death given earlier in the same lecture series. The Emma characterization (education, counsel, the “elect lady” role of the 1830 revelation) is fair in its milder elements; “stronger personality” is impression, outside the axes. **Finding:** a minority revisionist thesis asserted as settled fact, standing against the documentary record and against the series’ own prior narrative.

## VI. Summary of Findings


Assessed on historical accuracy: accurate or substantially accurate — the retrospective character of John’s gospel, exactly supported by John 2:22, 12:16, and 20:9 (§3); the pricks idiom (§4); the post-Constantinian placement of Christian-on-Christian violence and the exile kernel at Nicaea (§8, in those parts); the third-century footing of the sacerdotal characterization and the 1054 material with compressions (§9); Luther’s devotion, Rome journey, and Protestantism’s immediate division (§10); the witness-variation frame and the Matthean Sermon datum (§11); the John Paul I–Mark Twain detail (§12); the Franciscan core as tradition, including the documented disrobing (§13); the Sunstone/Alexander exchange as an event, with the documented anchors of early Utah theocracy and the 1857–58 gubernatorial replacement (§16); the Nauvoo surrender sequence (§18); and the Missouri outline — Boggs’s echo of Rigdon, Avard’s pivotal Richmond role, Liberty’s conditions, the connived escape, and the Liberty letter (§19). Inaccurate or materially overstated: Ananias called Cornelius, twice (§4); “absolutely not agree on much of anything” for Peter and Paul, against Galatians 2:2–9, 1 Corinthians 15:11, and 2 Peter 3:15–16 (§5); the majority-of-ministers-lack-faith claim, beyond any documented survey (§7); the arrest of the bishops and the pre-Nicene killing chronology, again (§8); Ephesians 2:8 cited as Romans (§10a); Wesley placed in Luther’s generation (§10b); “seven thousand” for the feeding of the four thousand (§11); “Pope John VI” (§12); the bring-me-twelve papal precondition and Francis “becoming a priest” (§13); the largest-standing-army and could-defeat-the-United-States claims, against the force numbers (§18); the 1837 date, the Salt-Speech/Fourth-of-July conflation, the unlocatable cook detail, the six-month figure, “stolen” for Whitmer’s retention, and the Lectures on Faith attribution stated as fact (§19); and the Brigham-Young-introduced-plural-marriage thesis, against the contemporaneous record, the church’s own acknowledgment, and the series’ own Expositor narrative (§20).


Assessed on biblical consistency: consistent — the opening love frame (§1); the hedged common-core principle (§2); the retrospective-understanding claim (§3); the Sermon-as-intensification exposition (§14); and the parables section, the lecture’s soundest (§15). Inconsistent or unsupported: the judgment-scales reading as exegesis (§4); the necessary-disagreement ecclesiology built on Galatians 2:11 against that chapter’s own fellowship verses (§5); the no-compensation doctrine as apostolic description and the total financial-motive attribution (§6, per the standing findings); and the universal comfort claim in its absolute form (§14, noted as pastoral).


## VII. The Series in Retrospect


With this eighth assessment the series is complete, and three cumulative observations belong to the record, stated within the same two axes that have governed throughout. First, the pattern of **source attribution** traced across the essays — Restoration scripture read to general Christian audiences without identification in the early lectures (Moses 8 as Genesis; 3 Nephi 11 as “the doctrine of Christ,” three times; D&C 101 as “what the Lord has said”), with attribution improving in the later installments (D&C 76 described with provenance; the Book of Mormon cited by book; Ehrman and Barker named; D&C 42:46 flagged as from “dubious” places) — is itself a documentary finding of the series. Second, the **recurring corrections** cluster: the Constantine complex (state religion, the arrest of the bishops, pre-Nicene killing) corrected at Lectures 2, 3, 7, and 8; the textual-corruption complex (the baptismal voice) degrading from a contested Luke-only variant (Lecture 2) to a nonexistent Matthean one plus a Proverbs-for-Psalms citation (Lecture 6); the escalation habit (the magi’s two years, from flagged inference to “according to the record”); and the arguments whose proof-texts answer them (Daniel 2:44 against the deferred kingdom; Isaiah 2:3’s parallelism against the two capitals; Galatians 2’s fellowship verses against apostolic disunity; Exodus 34:1 against the withdrawn greater law). Third, the **internal contradictions** the series generates against itself: the cross as the place of payment affirmed in Lecture 4 against Lecture 3’s denial; Menno Simons cited as a Reformation father in the lecture claiming no Reformation father sought restoration; St. Francis ministered to by angels inside the church declared wholly corrupt; and the Expositor death-narrative of Lectures 1–2 against Lecture 8’s claim that the practice the Expositor exposed did not yet exist. Set against these are the findings consistently recorded in the lectures’ favor: the resurrection-witness argument (Lectures 1, 2, 8), the retrospective composition of John (Lecture 8), the servant-leadership and persuaded-unity principles (Lectures 2, 7, 8), the parables of authenticity (Lecture 8), the Justin Martyr and Wesley quotations as quotations, the neglect-of-the-Book-of-Mormon history (Lecture 6), and the steady insistence — shared ground with this assessment — that Christ, not affiliation, is the criterion. The series’ governing theses (universal corruption, restored commission, new canon, deferred kingdom, relocated atonement, revised Mormon history) were each assessed at their principal statements and found, at the sections cited, to rest on premises that are inverted, unattested, contested-presented-as-settled, or self-undermining; its accompanying ethical exhortation (love, sacrifice, care for the poor, welcome of the stranger) was found, throughout, biblically consistent and often finely stated. The two verdicts stand together, each on its own evidence, which has been the method of these eight essays; and as each installment has closed, so the series closes: the findings concern the arguments as delivered, not the sincerity of the witness delivering them.
