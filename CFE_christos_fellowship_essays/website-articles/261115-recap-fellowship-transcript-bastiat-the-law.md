## 1. Michael's reflection: the world as God's script

Michael shared a written digest of the previous week's premise, that the world was made for God, not for us. He framed God as an author who could write anything. An author who chose to write this world, he said, presumably did not want a story in which everyone simply bows down; he wanted goods and bads, and then punishment of the bads. On that reading, whatever happens is God's ideal result. Michael compared this to the Muslim saying that something is "the will of Allah," and noted that it amounts to predetermination of human events.

He asked what themes and considerations went through the Author's mind, and what the current scenario does for the Creator psychologically. He offered the "good folks, bad folks" theme as one option and gave examples: European invaders devastating native cultures, and Christians, Protestants, and Catholics each claiming "God is with us." He suggested an alternative to labeling people good or bad: intervene and help them work together, as was done in creating the European Union.

Michael added a short assessment of the Iran war. In his view it had three basic effects: the United States obliterated Iran's defenses, caused serious destruction and deaths, and left Iran sure that it needs new leadership under the threat of further attack. Iran, however, now controls the Strait of Hormuz, and unless the United States wants to keep bombing, the war is over.

## 2. Thomas's clarification: creation as God's experience of Himself

Thomas said Michael's reading was a literal and simplified version of what he had said. He acknowledged that he had stated the premise (that God did not create the world for us) too cavalierly, without qualification. The qualification is that God created the world to experience Himself, so what God experiences is our experience.

The real question, then, is what optimizes God's experience. Thomas said the Great Commandment answers it: love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, mind, and strength, and your neighbor as yourself. Because God experiences every side, when Native Americans are wiped out they are not having a good time, and so God is not having a good time. Both sides must experience life in a way we would conventionally call enjoyable. To take the premise as Michael did removes it from the context that God experiences both sides.

Thomas added a second factor, the first commandment: love of God Himself. So the question becomes what God actually enjoys. Most of what people enjoy, God enjoys too, and when both enjoy it, God is satisfied. But there are things people enjoy that God does not, such as adultery and sexual perversion. People claim a right to them, and God says He does not like them. God seldom intervenes directly to show His displeasure. What we have instead is the Bible, other holy books with competing claims, and secular people who ask who God is to tell them what feels good. The result is a scenario that permits free will, which Thomas said is required for God to be loved. God is loved when people choose to love Him by obeying His law, which may not be obvious but is revealed.

**Editor's note.** The Great Commandment is found in **(Mark 12:30–31)**: *"And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind, and with all thy strength: this is the first commandment.** And the second is like, namely this, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself."*

## 3. Free will or a scripted play?

Michael said Thomas's account was no more complicated than his own. The Author could make anything, chose to make what Thomas calls free will, but still sets up exactly how people will or will not "freely" choose Him. Charlie agreed this was a fair statement of the problem.

Thomas called this the key point, almost the source of all philosophical discussion. If everything is set up by God and simply runs its course, Michael is right: there is no free will, the play means nothing, and it is simply God doing everything. Thomas's account makes sense only if free will is real, and he said he disagrees completely with the determinist reading because he believes God made the world with genuine choice.

Michael clarified that the premises were not his own. He was walking Thomas's road to understand it, like someone learning baseball who must be told to run to first base first. Within those rules, he pressed further: God has the power to set exactly how much free will humans have, enough that some will not choose Him, so that He can have His revenge and cast them into hell for eternity. He then tested the claim that God does not like adultery. Suppose two couples, as in the film *Bob & Carol & Ted & Alice*, agree to swap partners. That is to their liking but not to God's, yet God must have wanted it, or He would not have made it.

## 4. Where good and evil came from

Thomas said Michael's claim ("He wanted it, or He would not have made it") raises the question of what good and evil are and where evil came from, which had been the topic after Michael left the previous week. He laid out his account as a sequence.


 	- **The axiom.** God began from nothing and became aware of Himself. Thomas does not try to explain how; this is the posture from which the game starts. Michael asked whether God had not always existed. Thomas answered that one may start there instead, but the mystery is the same: how could He always be? The choice of starting point makes no difference.

 	- **The Father defines.** God, the source of everything, divided all that is into what He endorses, allows, and wants, and what He does not want. The part we call God is the part that loves what we call goodness. In that sense there is God and not-God, though everything began as nothing but God. Thomas said it takes some gymnastics to hold this, because it depends on which layer one looks at.

 	- **The Son creates.** The Father created the Son and said, in effect, "Son, create everything." The Son knew what the Father liked and did not like. He made all the possibilities of existence: the particles discussed in earlier meetings, planets, the Earth, animals, men and women, and the possibility of criminals, saints, angels, and demons. Everything that was made was made by the Word. Thomas said this is the content of John 1.

 	- **Innocence in Eden.** At first the people in the Garden knew only good. They knew nothing of adultery, murder, or thievery; they tended the garden and were happy. God told them not to eat of one tree. Whether the account is symbolic or literal, at some point humans gained the ability to discriminate good from evil. Once they knew of adultery, murder, theft, and covetousness, they realized the world they had been given allowed them to do these things.

 	- **Revelation of the playbook.** The distinction between good and evil was already established by the Father before the Son made the world. The Son built a play in which people could choose evil or choose the fruits of the Spirit. The Old Testament story, which Thomas noted Michael knew from his Bar Mitzvah training, revealed which was which: God communicated "do this, don't do that," and from then on people knew the playbook.

 	- **The outcome of choice.** Those who choose what God likes hear, "Well done, good and faithful servant," and enter His place. Those who choose the other side go to the side they chose and apparently liked. That is what we conventionally call hell: they get to live the life they chose, but not with God, because God does not accept that into His presence.


Michael corrected the tree's name: it was not the tree of evil but the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Thomas agreed. Michael said he had always loved the Eden story as an analogy for human development. Every person begins totally innocent, "out of the chute," a baby in blissful nakedness. Once we absorb the opinion that there is good and evil, that innocence turns into "now I've got to find a job" and deal with the world.

**Editor's note.** The passages Thomas drew on are **(John 1:3)**, *"All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made,"* and **(Matthew 25:21)**, *"Well done, thou good and faithful servant... enter thou into the joy of thy lord."* The tree is named in **(Genesis 2:17)**: *"But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it."*

## 5. A universe without God: randomness, order, and morality

Michael asked why one should put a middleman, God, into the concept of the universe at all. The universe could simply have been here the whole time. He accepted Thomas's premise as a premise but did not see that it was needed.

Thomas answered that if the universe simply did it, then all the planning and definition of things must arise by accident. Every coincidence of evolution and every ordering of matter must be done randomly; one makes randomness one's god. Order then arises here and there out of chaos, and the only moral standard is whatever produces an order each of us individually likes. We become the arbiters of good and evil. That is a world without a standard, with no point at which God divided and said, "This is good, and this is evil." Good and evil exist only in man's conception of them.

Thomas said Aleister Crowley put that world best: do what thou wilt is the whole of the law. His Kabbalah teacher, Daniel Bloxham, regarded Crowley as without compassion and said he should have added, "Take what you want, and pay for it," meaning that everything has consequences. In a universe with no directing or defining force, those two sayings are the whole of it, and Bob, Carol, Ted, and Alice may do as they please.

Michael drew two points from this. The first came from the opening day of his statistics class at UC Berkeley. The professor divided the class into ten groups and left the room. Nine groups flipped a coin 100 times and recorded the sequence; one group invented its results. When she returned, she identified the invented list before reading halfway through the papers. It was the only one with no run of four or five identical outcomes in a row. Michael's conclusion: randomness includes structure, and a random world produces structures, a kind of faux order. He lost the thread of his second point.

Thomas replied that he had acknowledged this in his monologue. Chaos theory shows self-assembly, the order that arises from chaos through chaotic attractors. His point was not that a random world cannot produce order. It was that in such a world the self-assembling order is our creator, and there is no place for any definition of right and wrong except the individual observer's. Without God, good and evil are entirely subjective: a vote, a whim, a group agreement, human-defined, with nothing eternal about them.

**Editor's note.** Crowley's line in *The Book of the Law* (1904) is *"Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law."* On the coin flips: in 100 fair tosses, the chance of at least one run of five identical outcomes is about 97%, and of at least six about 81%, so a list with no such runs is a strong sign of invention.

## 6. Whose good? Human claims and God's standard

Michael countered that even with a God there is only personal interpretation. The Nazis had "God is with us" inscribed on their belts, and every Christian-on-Christian war, which he said is most of history, rested on a sense of good and evil specific enough to call for annihilating the other country. Peoples who never heard of Christianity have evolved their own versions of good and evil. In a small tribe, nearly everyone sees a birth as good without any code telling them so. If one man becomes a tyrant, ten weaker men can band together and tell him that he can beat any one of them but not all ten. Michael called that the origin of government: good and evil arrived at individually, then agreed on together, becoming law. Even with God telling people what is good, Christian nations war and kill to support it, which he said is not a very good good.

Thomas said Michael had made a category error by combining two domains. One is the domain of God. The other is the domain of individuals and of groups of individuals. The Nazi belt buckle treats a person's self-declaration of what is good as if it made that thing good for God, which implies that the person is God and makes the definition. Thomas rejected that. God has decided what is good and evil; humans, individually and in groups, try to discern that absolute and may or may not get it right. Both he and Michael agree that the Nazis did not get it right, whatever they believed.

Michael restated Thomas's position: regardless of the warring Christian nations each claiming God, there is a universal, empirical good. He then quoted a comedy line about being made "the big Jell-O sheriff of the house," which Leonard identified as Bill Cosby. Michael said he loves the line, though he hates what Cosby did later in life. His point was that whoever claims his own version is the true one tends to conclude that everyone else is wrong. If Thomas stood between two warring Christian nations and told them both that neither was the good, they would both kill him. Discerning what the universal good actually is remains the essential question.

Thomas agreed that this is the point he has been making when he speaks of the absolute perspective. People may claim what they like, but God has defined right and wrong and revealed it, for example in the Ten Commandments given through Moses. He then said, "Guys, go out and play." Those who follow His way are the ones He likes and who may be with Him; those who do not, He does not want around, and they go to play in the place that makes them happy.

Thomas said that what connects God and humanity is also what separates them: free will. Without free will there would be no separation, only God. With it, God holds the domain of good and evil, separate from the domain of two people each insisting "This is God's will," either of whom may be right, wrong, or partly right. Free will lets each choose what he takes God's will to be, whether God exists, or simply to do whatever he wants.

**Editor's note.** *Gott mit uns* ("God with us") was stamped on the belt buckles of the German Army (Wehrmacht), continuing a Prussian military motto; SS buckles carried a different motto. Many European wars were fought between Christian states, but "most of history" overstates it: much of the world's warfare occurred outside Christendom.

## 7. God is everything, yet distinct from His creatures

Michael asked how separation from God fits Thomas's earlier claim that God is everything. If something is separate from God, then God is not everything, or there is something beyond everything that needs another name.

Thomas called this the crux. His thesis is that there is nothing that is not God, and the question is how that is compatible with a God who is distinct from humanity. His answer is that God implemented the system by layering and disguise. Neither he nor Michael has ever seen God; each has only mental constructs. Following Conscious Point Physics, one starts with conscious points, builds quarks and subatomic particles, atoms, molecules, physiology, and finally a nervous system. That nervous system is trained by nature and nurture: it is hurt and calls that bad, it feels pleasure and calls that good, and it reads the Bible and learns what God calls good and bad. Such a person has a naive point of view. Assembled from particles of consciousness, he can see only what is around him and is not privy to good and evil as God defines them. Thomas offered this as a story rather than a proof: it is all one, but from inside it does not look like one.

Michael said subdivision is not hard to grasp, since a person's eyes are part of him. His question was what Thomas meant by "everything," if some things stand apart from God. Thomas said nothing stands apart from God. Michael concluded that God is then the evil too. Thomas said God defined evil and created the Son, who created actors who might act out what God defined as evil.

Michael proposed that God creates things He finds distasteful because it is to His taste to do so. Thomas said this blended things together. The Father is completely good, with no shadow of turning, and had nothing to do with creating evil; He defined good and evil. The Son, a subset of the Father, created everything. Even the Son created no evil thing, only potentialities that could choose good or evil. Evil appears only where people choose what God has said not to do.

Thomas said a snail darter or an octopus cannot do evil. Animals have a nature and act on it, and everything nature does is good. Evil entered only when humans arrived, able to manipulate the parts of life and to choose consciously the parts God calls evil. Before humans there was no evil, because there was no conscious choice.

Michael noted that these animals are venomous and kill, and asked whether "thou shalt not kill" applies only to humans. Thomas said the commandment is "thou shalt not murder." Murder carries intent: the unrighteous taking of another's life, knowing it is wrong. An animal that kills does what it was built to do. Michael raised a man holding ten people hostage: if you shoot him first, that is intentional killing. Thomas agreed it was intentional and good, a justified killing because it stopped him from killing those he had unrighteously seized. Michael remarked that this is the creed of every soldier.

Michael then said that God chose to create the distinction, or the Son did. Thomas said the Father made the distinction; the Son created a world in which those possibilities could be actualized. Michael asked why God allowed so much evil rather than one part in a billion. Thomas said God did not create a quantity of evil. He created a platform for choice, and the amount of evil is simply the amount people choose.

Michael said that made the choosing sound random, or else a matter of B. F. Skinner-style conditioning, in which upbringing trains people into what one group calls good and another calls bad. Thomas agreed that people are imprinted. A child raised in a crack house or a Mafia family may think that life is normal. But that is downstream of the Father defining good and evil and the Son creating everything. God also gave each person a conscience. Cain murdered Abel, knew it was wrong, and hid the body. The Ten Commandments were not strictly necessary, but they made the law formal, known, and accountable to the group, perhaps in a governmental way. Good and evil were primal, prior to the Son's creation.

Michael said that God still chose to create the prospect of evil in the universe. Thomas said it was already possible, because God had already defined good and evil, and whatever was made from God's substance carried that knowledge.

**Editor's note.** The Hebrew verb in the sixth commandment, *ratsach*, denotes murder or unlawful killing, and modern translations render it *"Thou shalt not murder"* **(Exodus 20:13)**. Cain's knowledge of his guilt appears in his evasion, *"Am I my brother's keeper?"* **(Genesis 4:9)**. "No shadow of turning" is from **(James 1:17)**.

## 8. Why God defined good and evil: love requires another

Michael observed that an "everything" which prefers some things and calls them good, and disprefers others and calls them evil, has something like an ego, which seemed a non-everything kind of thing to do. He said he could work with an everything that has consciousness, and asked whether unconsciousness is also part of everything. He then pressed the point that God did not have to make evil; He chose to.

Thomas said that if God was going to make a creation, He needed something to relate to. Scripture reveals that God is love. What Thomas has been calling the definition of good and evil is another way of saying this: to say God is love requires defining love, and the things that are good are what we call love. It is a matter of nomenclature.

Michael said God could have defined only personal preferences, or set things up so that evil did not exist; He is the Author. Thomas said not if He was to be love. Without a definition of good and evil, God would be a God with no standards, where anything goes, no different from a random universe.

Michael suggested that God cast Himself out of His own Garden of Eden: He had only good, was at one with it all, and then created the categories of what He likes and does not like and made them manifest in a play below. Thomas added a qualifier on love. Love cannot exist without another. If God is love, there had to be a lover capable of reciprocating, of relating to Him as one wishes to be treated. If God had enough articulation within Himself to recognize these concepts, He would ask how to embody love, which required creating an other that could freely choose to relate to Him in a way that satisfied His desire to be appreciated and loved.

## 9. "God is love" and "God is everything": the Shirley MacLaine fallacy

Michael cited "God is love" as one of his favorite verses and noted the implication: if God is love and God is everything, then everything is love. Thomas said that conclusion is false, and Michael asked him to reconcile the two statements.

Thomas said the resolution is the same as before: existence has striations. There is the Father; the Son, His complete image yet distinct; and the whole creation, which the Son imaged. Love is the experience of persons relating to each other in a way that satisfies. It requires an other, free will, and a standard of what does and does not satisfy. Reasoning "God is love, God is everything, therefore everything is love" is the same error as "Everything is God, therefore I am God." He called this the Shirley MacLaine fallacy, recalling the scene of her on the beach shouting "I am God" after her book *Out on a Limb*. The New Age movement called it radical and enlightened. Thomas said she was mistaken: she is a point of God's perspective looking at part of His creation, not God, who holds the perspective of the whole. Everything is God, but connecting the two statements that way is fallacious.

**Editor's note.** Michael cited the verse as John 4:16; "God is love" appears in the First Epistle of John, twice: **(1 John 4:8)** and **(1 John 4:16)**: *"God is love; and he that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God, and God in him."* MacLaine's book *Out on a Limb* was published in 1983; the beach scene is from the ABC miniseries adaptation, aired in January 1987, not 1986.

## 10. Love without another, and the question "Who am I?"

Michael said he did not know where to draw the boundary of "I." If he is his mind, that leaves out his hands and feet. If he is his body, that fails too, because he must breathe constantly or die, so the boundary must include the air and everything around him. Doing the same with God, there is a problem calling God anything but the entire whole.

On love, he said love has vastly different manifestations. Loving Jenny, or loving the group, involves the other Thomas described. But his greatest love does not need another. He loves being alive. Looking at the nature outside his window, he loves not this or that thing but simply being, and he is part of being, which brings him back to "all is one." That love involves no free will and no will at all; it is an acknowledgment of being part of it all, part of God, as Thomas might say. It needs no standard.

Thomas said Michael had moved from one layer to the next until he reached the largest, where God simply exists and Michael is still in a place of love. Each layer is valid as an answer to "Am I this?" but each is a different frame. He walked through them. Am I my two feet, legs, and eyes? If amputated, would I still be me? Then perhaps I am the way I feel inside. Under anesthesia I feel nothing, so perhaps I am awareness. Knocked unconscious, do I still exist? If I were only the brain chemistry that lets me see pictures and hear sounds, that would be the bottom. Thomas contends there is another layer. Drawing on near-death experiences, in which people report a self outside the physical boundary, he posits (and says others may accept or reject) that the self which travels through the tunnel, meets Jesus, and is told to go back is the "I" that he is. That same I is also part of the all that God is.

Michael said this reminded him of a child who asked him what death is like. After saying that nobody knows, he suggested imagining oneself as a raindrop falling and then landing in the ocean: it still has all its substance but is part of something bigger, no longer defined by a boundary. He said he was making it up. He then had to leave, saying it is always a pleasure to ponder with the group.

## 11. Reflections on the conversation with Michael

Armond summarized for Charlie, who had arrived late, that the exchange began with the origin of everything and turned on the creation of the universe and the source of God and all we experience. Charlie asked how Thomas thought it had gone, and whether anything was causing Michael to rethink his views.

Thomas said he thought it went well. In his view, Michael is defending and speaking from a worldview he has held for 71 years: a physical world arising from randomness, with no God, only everything, in which people compete over what should and should not be done. Thomas said he is not sure Michael is trying to incorporate God, but he is being confronted with the idea, and Thomas is showing him the limits of his perspective. Asked whether Michael feels challenged, Thomas said he believes Michael is trying to understand another worldview that is unfamiliar to him.

Armond said the difficulty in discussing with Michael is that every rebuttal he offers rests on physical experience, which can largely be demonstrated. How does one argue with the physical world? Thomas's remark that he has never seen God struck Armond as profound, because it raises the question of how to prove God to someone when we have never seen Him. Charlie answered that if God is everything, we see Him all the time.

### Love without another, and what hell might be

Susan returned to Michael's claim that his greatest love needs no other. She suspected that if he actually lived without another person, he would feel lonely and dislike it; he was describing an abstraction he has never experienced. Thomas said that was exactly his point: aloneness is the state God was in and wished to leave, which is why He created another capable of free will and choice.

Susan proposed asking Michael next time whether he could imagine being entirely alone, stranded like a castaway, with no one to relate to. Armond quipped that there is always Wilson. Thomas thought Michael would probably say he would be fine, precisely because he has never endured it for a lifetime with no hope of company. As a positivist, he would make the best of it and marvel at the palm trees, coconuts, sand, and seashells. Thomas recalled a story in which Michael, finding himself in a prison, responded with fascination at watching the people around him. Michael takes every experience as fully as he can, and if everyone lived that way life would be fairly good. But life is not like that for most people. Someone who is beaten up does not say, "What an interesting experience."

Thomas recalled Michael telling the group of a five-day headache at a pain level of ten that never relented, until he finally surrendered into it and it became "just whatever." The experience apparently shaped his sense of what life could be. Thomas said it may say something about hell: people accommodate. Those who pursued something all their lives and receive it may simply settle at that level, accepting that "this is what you get around here." He added that we do not know what hell really is.

Susan made two points. First, in this life even thieves, murderers, and gang members have some access to love: a wife, children, a brotherhood. Because God is love, she believes hell would contain no love at all and would be nothing like this life. Second, Michael has largely embraced the values of doing right by others. Even his long-term relationship with his girlfriend, though they are not married, reflects a commitment she considers the essence of marriage; formal vows and witnesses exist because many people are not genuinely committed. She believes Michael is unknowingly living in accordance with much of God's law and is being blessed for it. Because he does not recognize God in this, he concludes he does not need God. But he has God now, and if he were to die and find himself in a hell without love or God, she does not think he would find it comfortable. Thomas said he agreed with all of it.

**Editor's note.** "Wilson" is the volleyball companion of the castaway in the film *Cast Away* (2000).

## 12. Leonard: the Father, the Son, and the playground

Leonard apologized for being drowsy after a late night. He said he goes to Scripture to understand who God is, because God cannot be discerned apart from His own revelation, which He has given through the ages to people He chose. He has long asked who it is we worship and why God requires worship.

He read from John 5, where the Jews sought to kill Jesus for breaking the Sabbath and for calling God His Father, making Himself equal with God. Jesus answered that the Son can do nothing of Himself but what He sees the Father do; that the Father loves the Son and shows Him all things; that as the Father raises the dead, so the Son quickens whom He will; and that the Father has committed all judgment to the Son.

From this Leonard draws that Christ is God incarnate, bearing the title Son, and that the being He calls Father wants us to know Him by that name. The realm where God lives is therefore familial: a Father and a Son. Like any good father, God wants the best for His children. The Son emulates the Father by doing what He has seen the Father do. Leonard said he believes, as his own view, that Christ literally witnessed His Father do all that He did in coming to this creation. The Son was given all power to create, and He made this earth so His children could have experiences and learn: seeking, asking, knocking, and coming to understand who God is.

Leonard said he differs from the idea that God needs to enjoy anything. God already has the fullness of joy; He is the fullness of joy. He did not create us so He could enjoy our existence. Leonard cited Joseph Smith's teaching that God's work and glory is to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man, and Jesus' command to be perfect as the Father is perfect. We cannot do that by our own power, but the Father can make us perfect if we are willing. We make mistakes and hurt people, try to make things right, and He forgives, if in our hearts we want to be like Him.

He offered an analogy: creation is God's playground. Some children love the swings, others the merry-go-round. A child who wants a swing when they are full may push another child off and take it, when he could have asked nicely and probably been given a turn. Children make good and bad choices on the playground. It is His playground, not anyone else's, and in it we show our true hearts. If we play nicely and live by the Sermon on the Mount, we grow to understand who God is, who we want to be, and with whom we want to be after we leave the playground.

Leonard said that, in his own view, God is still progressing: when we glorify His name by doing what He wants, He progresses further, as we do. God created these things for us, not for His own pleasure; he regards the "for His pleasure" idea as sectarian dogma that came in through pagan influence. God created the world so we could find the fullness of joy in His presence, and the key is being comfortable in the presence of a perfect, all-powerful being. Leonard believes God has gone through what we are going through, which he said Joseph Smith tried to teach shortly before he was killed.

### Thomas's response

Thomas cited the verses behind his own position, Revelation 4:11 and Romans 11:36. He said Leonard's view is integral to Joseph Smith's worldview and at odds with the one he is proposing; he uses the Bible as his standard, not Joseph Smith. He added that much of his account (God is everything and created all things for His pleasure, and the structure he built around that) is his own revelation, just as Joseph Smith had his. He cannot claim it reflects truth; it is the story he has made. Leonard has developed a theory he is comfortable with about who God is and His motivation. All of it is backstory to how we live, and the two worldviews may have downstream implications neither can yet identify. That realm is opaque to us. Each cites a verse or a prophet, and Thomas said he does not see either as primary.

Leonard said he thinks the two corroborate each other despite differences in language: God is everything and created the playground for His children to play in. It is His and no one else's.

### The church's wealth, family, and the state of the playground

Leonard said he should not go there, but the LDS Church is one of the wealthiest institutions in the world, at over a trillion dollars, and explains holding and acquiring its assets as preparing to hand them to the Lord when He comes. He asked who the Church thinks owns them in the first place. It is sad to see, he said, and all churches tend that way. This world is a bad place, yet there is beauty and joy in it. Family can be a section of hell or of heaven. He spoke of his daughters, raised in California and now living their own lives, and the joy he sees in their faces. This, he said, is the only place we can have such experiences as mortals. The playground can be wonderful or nasty, because nasty people take it over. She asked Thomas, as a physician, what the body does to heal a small wound such as a thorn in the foot. Thomas explained the acute phase reaction: inflammation, swelling vessels, and white blood cells arriving to digest and carry away the debris. Healing cannot happen until the acute phase is complete. It has gone chronic. To heal it, one must re-stimulate the area with heat, massage, electrical stimulation, and similar means, reinitiating the acute phase so the tissue is actually cleansed.

Asked to translate this to the Christian community, Thomas said that when someone hurts us and we simply breathe deeply, walk it off, hit a punching bag, sleep on it, watch a movie, or play sports, we have bypassed the acute phase. Charlie asked whether Tyson regained the title and whether he retired on top; Thomas said he did regain it but had not finished the series. In a bad environment, a person can nurture the animal and let it run him, or he can refuse to act on it and act on the spirit. With the sword of the Spirit dividing soul from spirit, good from evil, a person can ally with the spirit against the soul and control the animal inside.

## 25. Knowing God's ways by doing them

As the group looked for a way to wrap up, Thomas asked Armond for his insights. Susan cited the parable of the wise man who built his house on the rock and the foolish man who built on sand. ## 26. Implementing *The Law*: transition, taxes, and who is King


### How hard the change would be

Thomas said it had been a good discussion. Susan suggested taking up Bastiat another time, but Thomas thought they had covered the important issue. For anyone interested in continuing, the follow-up essays (four, five in all with the original) address how to implement the system, which would radically change our world; the two systems are not compatible. Susan said he is writing so much that he is making their heads spin, and Charlie said it was making his whole body spin. Nobody can read it all, Charlie said, but the writing is helping Thomas get things straight in his own mind, and Thomas agreed. Thomas said he would probably bring a different topic next time.

Thomas said ideas are the easy part; the question is how to turn them into a program that works. *The Law* is admirable, but Bastiat died in 1850 and nobody has implemented it since. Making a campaign of it is like turning oneself into a pretzel, because it runs completely counter to how the system works and how people think, as Charlie added. One can use it as a platform describing how things should and could be, but then one must face today's reality, in which many people depend on welfare. Stopping it abruptly would leave millions literally dying, because we have become addicted to a satanic system, and going cold turkey would kill people. Susan called it general plunder, general crime.

Susan said this could never be implemented without teaching the gospel, so that people see how they can turn to Christ and immediately begin to be different. What usually drives people to call out to God is crisis, and a crisis is coming. People can afflict themselves now through fasting and prayer, or wait for the affliction to arrive; God can help us prepare if we turn to Him. The choice is to change now, the less painful way, or have it forced on us. Thomas added that we would deserve it for falling away from God: He lets the locusts come, with famine, disease, and pestilence. The pestilence might be a bomb from Iran, a leaked biolab from Wuhan, or Muslims outpopulating us and imposing Sharia law in 100 to 500 years. We do not know what it will be.

### Harmonious transformation

Susan said the fear that people would starve overlooks that believers in Christ would reach out and help, organized through churches, charities, and families, with accountability. A helper might say, "I'll help you for three months; then let's reassess what you are doing to get into a better position," weaning the person off help until he is stable. Thomas agreed and said he should have qualified his point. Chinese medicine distinguishes harmonious from inharmonious transformation. He had been describing the inharmonious kind: passing a law that ends Social Security, Medicare, welfare, and WIC overnight, telling people the Christians will take care of them, which would be chaos. Weaning is harmonious transformation. As Susan spoke, he pictured laws granting tax reductions to those who organize or give to relief agencies, so that people gradually shift their giving there and get credit for it, building a patchwork of such arrangements.

Susan said she would go as far as a person not having to pay taxes at all. Thomas said there will still be taxes, because Romans 13 tells us to render tribute; giving to government is biblical. Government is to judge evil and protect the population, so taxes are fine in their proper proportion. The present system has destroyed people's ability to be charitable by taking their money and giving it to others. The recipient does not feel given to, because it is an entitlement; the giver does not feel virtuous, because the money was taken from him, so he is resentful. Everything bad follows from that, and it must change. Susan agreed it must change.

### Taxes and kingship: where Susan and Thomas differ

Susan said she disagrees with Thomas about taxes, though it is a long subject. Thomas asked her to read the passage in his essay. After some confusion over whether it was Romans 1 or 13, he found Romans 13:4–7 and concluded that taxes are not optional.

Susan explained her view through the cycle in the Old Testament. God's people disobey; God brings foreign rulers over them; they suffer under those rulers until they cry out to God; God raises a deliverer and frees them; then the cycle repeats. Thomas said that in the times they were ruled by God they still had kings, apart from the period of the judges. After the judges came kings from then on, with good and bad cycles and periods under other nations and under their own, but never a return to rule by God alone. Susan agreed that the cycle of foreign domination continued, and that Israel chose kings, but noted that this was not God's original desire: He wanted to be their King. Thomas agreed, but said there is no example of that happening again; it was kings from then on, and Romans tells us what to do when we have a king.

Susan said that just as God sent deliverers to free His people from foreign, secular rulers, she believes He is providing a deliverer to free us from other rulers so He can again be our King. Thomas said he sees that happening when Christ comes: we move from earthly kings, to Christ as King, to all things placed under the Father. God's original plan was to be under God alone; we fell from it and are now in the cycle; Christ will deliver us all under God, and not before. Susan said that is a point of contention to work through. Her essay is about returning to the rule of Christ even before He comes, through a way she believes Scripture provides.

Thomas said she is correct if the claim is that each person comes to Christ and bows to God. Christ is the mediator, and through Him we may enter boldly into the Father's presence. That is the change he wants in every heart. But until the nations unite under Christ and all things are subdued, we each remain under some king. Susan asked whether he thinks direct allegiance to Christ operates within subjection to a king or ruler, and he said he does.

Susan said they agree on much and differ on something significant, and that the conversation had clarified where the difference lies. She agreed every individual, whatever his circumstances, can make Christ his King, but she still thinks something more is possible before Christ returns, though she might change her mind. Thomas said he does not rule it out, but he is practical and does not see it happening in the world except in individual hearts. The programs discussed earlier (relief societies replacing taxes, with taxes paid only for defense) seem practical to him. He sees a world without a king as the outcome once the nations have beaten their swords into plowshares, not before. Until then a sword is needed, and the state, not the individual, bears it; one does not raise a private army to fight or defend against the Iranians. Until the world has come under the Father, giving up the sword is unsafe and foolish. Susan thanked him for helping her understand their difference, while not agreeing. Thomas said they are all on a journey, trying to figure this out, and Armond called it exciting.
