# 261116 - Psychedelics and Spiritual Experience

# The Chemical Door:


### Psychedelics, Mystical Experience, and the God Who Is Already Near

*Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · *16 November 2026

## A Good Friday in a Basement

On Good Friday, April 20, 1962, twenty Protestant divinity students gathered in the basement of Boston University's Marsh Chapel. Upstairs, the Good Friday service was under way, piped down to them over loudspeakers. Before it began, each student swallowed a capsule. Ten capsules held 30 milligrams of psilocybin, the active compound in "magic" mushrooms. The other ten held niacin, a vitamin that flushes the skin and makes a person feel that *something* is happening.

The experiment was designed by Walter Pahnke, a physician and minister working on his doctorate at Harvard under the supervision of Timothy Leary. Pahnke wanted to know whether a drug could reliably produce a mystical experience in religiously prepared people. Most of the students who received psilocybin reported that it did. One of them ran out of the chapel convinced he had been chosen to announce the return of the Messiah, and had to be restrained and sedated.

I recently watched a video from the YouTube channel *Religion for Breakfast* that uses the "Good Friday Experiment" as its doorway into a larger question: what the last sixty years of research tell us about psychedelics and religious experience. The video is careful, fair, and well researched. I want to summarize what it reports, and then do what the video, as an academic survey, deliberately does not do: judge the claims by the standard of Scripture.

The question under the question is simple. People who take these substances often say they met God. Did they? And if what they met was not God, what was it, and why does it feel so much like Him?

## What the Video Reports

When psychedelics are used in a religious setting, scholars call them *entheogens*, a word coined in the 1970s from Greek roots meaning roughly "God generated within." The term was chosen to escape the baggage of "hallucinogen" and "psychedelic." The substances are the same; only the frame is different.

**The history.** Ritual use is ancient and widespread. Initiates of the Greek Eleusinian Mysteries drank a brew called *kykeon*, which some scholars believe contained an ergot fungus with LSD-like compounds. Peyote, a mescaline-bearing cactus, has been used in Mesoamerica for millennia. A thousand-year-old ritual bundle found in Bolivia in 2008 held traces of psilocybin and DMT. Today the Native American Church takes peyote as a sacrament and speaks of it as "Mother Peyote" or "Father Peyote," a watching, parent-like presence. In the Amazon, more than seventy indigenous groups use ayahuasca, and the Brazilian religion Santo Daime blends ayahuasca ritual with Christianity.

**The chemistry.** The four best-studied substances are mescaline, psilocybin, DMT, and LSD. All of them act on one type of serotonin receptor in the brain, the 5-HT2A receptor. Ayahuasca works only because one plant in the brew blocks the stomach enzyme that would otherwise destroy the DMT supplied by the other.

**The experiences.** The research the video surveys clusters around four themes.


 	- *Unity.* Depressed patients given psilocybin described merging with the universe. One said he had become everybody, with six billion faces; another felt himself becoming the Hindu god Shiva. Researchers measure this with a scale for "oceanic boundlessness," a phrase that traces to the French writer Romain Rolland's letter to Sigmund Freud.

 	- *Awe.* Users describe vastness, and the need to rebuild their mental framework to take in what they are seeing. The video notes that this is why visionary books like Revelation are sometimes (wrongly, the narrator says) attributed to drug experiences.

 	- *Changed beliefs.* A 2021 study of 866 people attending psychedelic ceremonies found that beliefs shifted away from strict materialism, the view that only physical matter exists, and the shift lasted at least six months. The proposed explanation is the "REBUS" model (Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics): the drug loosens how tightly the mind holds its convictions.

 	- *Entity encounters.* In a 2020 survey of 2,561 people who had taken DMT, most met a being they called a guide, spirit, alien, or helper. Fewer called it an angel or an elf; a handful said a gnome, a monster, or a dead person.


**The comparison.** A 2019 Johns Hopkins survey of 4,285 people compared God encounters that came without drugs with those that came on psychedelics. Both groups reported voices, visions, and deep emotion. But 41 percent of the non-drug group said the best word for what they met was "God," against 18 percent of the psychedelic group, who more often spoke of an impersonal "ultimate reality."

**The ethics.** Finally, the video notes that psilocybin is now the center of a multi-billion-dollar wellness industry with hundreds of patents, and that the indigenous communities whose knowledge started it all rarely share in the benefit.

## Where I Stand

I believe these experiences are real, and I believe they are not a safe road to God. Both halves matter.

They are real in the sense that the people having them are not simply making things up, and in the sense that the experiences point at something true: the physical world is not the whole of reality, and God is far nearer to every one of us than the materialist imagines. On that point the psychedelic user and the Christian agree, against the secular consensus of the last century.

But a chemical opens a door from the wrong side. It can lower the walls of perception without changing the heart of the one who perceives. It can give a person the *feeling* of oneness with God without the *fact* of reconciliation with God. And it places him, unguarded and impressionable, in a spiritual territory that Scripture says is inhabited by more than one kind of being. I therefore stand on the side that a Christian should not seek God through these substances, and that what is met under their influence should not be received as the voice of God unless it passes the tests Scripture gives.

There are honest limits to what I can claim. No one outside a person's experience can say with certainty what he encountered. The survey data on which the video relies are self-reports, not observations. And the medical question, whether a physician may use these compounds to treat depression or addiction under supervision, is a separate question from the spiritual one. I address it briefly below, but it is not my main subject. My subject is the claim that a drug can bring a person to God.

## What the Experiences Get Right

**Materialism is false.** The 2021 belief study found that people moved away from the idea that reality consists only of physical objects and processes. I count that as a movement toward the truth. The Bible never treated the physical world as the whole: *"For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead"* **(Romans 1:20)**. My own physics work begins from the conviction that consciousness is not an accident of matter but is woven into the foundation of reality. A person who loses his confidence in strict materialism has lost a false belief, however he lost it.

**God is near.** Paul told the philosophers of Athens that God made men *"that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: For in him we live, and move, and have our being"* **(Acts 17:27–28)**. The overwhelming sense of presence that people report is not, in itself, an illusion. God really is that close.

**The oneness points at something real.** Here I want to say something that I believe is missing from most Christian responses to this subject. At the center of my teaching is the conviction that God does not merely watch our lives from outside. He inhabits and experiences every person's life from within. In a real sense, we are God's experience of the world. Every joy and every wound, every kindness and every cruelty, is felt by Him from the inside. This is why His law is not arbitrary. He asks us to love one another because He feels what we do to one another.

When a patient on psilocybin says, "I was everybody, one life with six billion faces," or "I was swimming in the sea, and the sea was me," I believe he is catching a distorted glimpse of that truth. There *is* one Life present in every life. The person has sensed the nearness of the One who shares every experience. What he has not grasped is who that One is, or what stands between him and that One.

## What the Experiences Get Wrong

**Oneness is not reconciliation.** If God lives inside every life, then He is near even to the man who hates Him. Nearness is not the problem. The problem is the separation that sin creates: *"But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you"* **(Isaiah 59:2)**. God feels our choices from within, and that is exactly why our wrongs grieve Him and why they must be dealt with. The word *atonement* means "at-one-ment": what sin divided is rejoined. Scripture names one way that happens: *"But now in Christ Jesus ye who sometimes were far off are made nigh by the blood of Christ"* **(Ephesians 2:13)**. A drug can make a man *feel* at one with God. It cannot make him at one. The feeling arrives without repentance, without forgiveness, and without any change in the record of what he has done. It is the sensation of home without the road home.

**The arrow points the wrong way.** The true teaching is that God lives in me. The psychedelic version, again and again, is that *I am God*. The patient who felt himself becoming Shiva, the one who was "everybody," the one for whom "the sea was me": in each case the self expands until it swallows the divine. That is the oldest offer in the world: *"ye shall be as gods"* **(Genesis 3:5)**. The unity Jesus prayed for is different. It keeps the persons distinct and joins them in love: *"That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us"* **(John 17:21)**. In that union, I do not dissolve into God, and God does not dissolve into me. We know one another.

**The God of Scripture is a Person.** The Johns Hopkins comparison is telling. People who met God without drugs were more than twice as likely to say they had met *God*, a Someone. People who met "God" on psychedelics more often described an impersonal "ultimate reality." But the God of the Bible has a name, speaks, makes covenants, and gives commandments. A presence that loves everyone, asks nothing, and has no name is far easier to welcome than the One who said *"I AM THAT I AM"* **(Exodus 3:14)** and then told Moses what to do.

**Awe without holiness.** The researchers define awe as vastness plus the need to rebuild one's mental framework. Biblical awe has those elements, but it has another that the research does not measure: the sudden sight of one's own sin. When Isaiah saw the Lord, his first words were *"Woe is me! **for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips"* **(Isaiah 6:5)**. When John saw the risen Christ, *"I fell at his feet as dead"* **(Revelation 1:17)**. In none of the accounts quoted in the video does anyone fall on his face in conviction. Bill Richards describes love, beauty, and peace beyond imagining. That is a lovely experience. It is not the experience Isaiah had.

## Testing the Spirits

The entity encounters deserve the most care, because they are where the experience stops being a feeling and starts carrying a message. In the 2020 DMT survey, nearly all respondents described the being they met as conscious and intelligent, most called it benevolent, and about two-thirds said they came away with a message, task, mission, or purpose from it.

Scripture does not tell us to dismiss such encounters as hallucination. It tells us something more sobering: *"Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world"* **(1 John 4:1)**. And it gives the test: *"Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God"* **(1 John 4:2)**. The question to put to any being met in any state is not "Was it kind?" or "Did I feel loved?" It is "What did it say about Jesus Christ?" Scripture warns that kindness and light are exactly the costume deception would wear: *"for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light"* **(2 Corinthians 11:14)**.

The beings in the survey were called guides, spirits, aliens, helpers, elves, gnomes, and the dead. Very few of those names belong to anything the Bible tells us to seek. Some of them, contact with the dead in particular, belong to practices the Bible expressly forbids. A message from such a source, received while one's ordinary judgment has been chemically loosened, is not a revelation to obey. It is a claim to be tested, and most users have no framework for testing it.

The New Testament also has a word for this territory. Paul lists *"witchcraft"* among the works of the flesh **(Galatians 5:20)**, and Revelation says of Babylon, *"for by thy sorceries were all nations deceived"* **(Revelation 18:23)**. In both places the Greek word is *pharmakeia*, from which we get "pharmacy." In the ancient world it meant sorcery practiced through potions and drugs. I do not read it as a condemnation of all medicine; I am a physician, and I do not believe Scripture forbids healing. But I do read it as a plain warning that using substances to reach into the spiritual world is something God has already named, and not kindly.

Finally, Scripture repeatedly asks for a clear mind in spiritual matters: *"Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour"* **(1 Peter 5:8)**. The REBUS model says psychedelics work by relaxing the grip of one's beliefs. That may be helpful for a man stuck in despair. It is also, precisely, a description of lowering one's guard.

## The Strongest Case on the Other Side

I want to state the opposing view at its best, because it is not foolish.

**The fruit looks good.** Jesus said, *"Ye shall know them by their fruits"* **(Matthew 7:16)**. By the measures researchers use, the fruit is impressive. In the Johns Hopkins survey, about three-quarters of respondents rated their encounter among the most meaningful experiences of their lives, and more than two-thirds of those who had called themselves atheists no longer did afterward. In the DMT survey, most reported lasting gains in well-being and life satisfaction. When Rick Doblin tracked down the Good Friday participants about 25 years later, those who had received psilocybin still counted it among the high points of their spiritual lives. The religion scholar Huston Smith, who took part in a session of the experiment, called his own experience the most powerful "cosmic homecoming" he had ever known.

**God is not confined to our categories.** Paul was not seeking God on the Damascus road; God met him anyway. If God can speak through a donkey, the argument goes, why not through a mushroom? And the Native American Church and Santo Daime are communities of sincere believers, some of whom name Jesus, who have used these sacraments for generations.

**My answer.** I take the fruit seriously, but I notice two things about it.

First, the Johns Hopkins study found that the non-drug group reported the *same* kinds of lasting benefit, and was more likely to meet a personal God. If the good fruit comes from both roads, the chemical road is not giving access to anything the other road withholds. It adds risk without adding anything unique.

Second, Jesus' fruit test was given to judge prophets, and the fruit He meant is the fruit of the Spirit and faithfulness to the truth, not only improved mood. Leaving atheism for a nameless "ultimate reality" is a real change, but it is not yet conversion. By the test of 1 John 4, the question is whether the experience leads a person to confess Jesus Christ come in the flesh. The surveys do not ask that, so the surveys cannot answer it.

As for God meeting a man where he is: I believe He does, and I would never tell someone that God could not have reached him during a drug experience. God has met men in prisons, gutters, and battlefields. But a meeting in a ditch is grace in spite of the ditch, not a recommendation of the ditch.

## Why the Concern Remains Plausible

Even a reader who sets aside every Scripture I have quoted has reasons to be wary.

**A God on demand.** There is something instinctively wrong about a God who arrives on schedule, thirty minutes after a measured dose. Every idol in history had this in common: it could be operated. The living God comes when He wills: *"And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart"* **(Jeremiah 29:13)**. The condition is the heart, not the dose.

**The impressionable are moved most.** The 2021 belief study found that how far a person's beliefs shifted depended on how impressionable he was beforehand, and on how emotionally in sync he felt with the others in the ceremony. The shift was strongest in first-time users. Its authors went so far as to suggest that people offered psychedelics should be warned, as part of informed consent, that their worldview may change. When the researchers themselves say a substance can rewrite beliefs most powerfully in the most suggestible people, in a group setting led by someone else, a Christian should ask who is doing the writing.

**Money is in the room.** Psilocybin is now the center of a multi-billion-dollar industry with hundreds of patents. Spiritual language sells: a retreat that promises an encounter with the divine commands a higher price than one that promises a serotonin receptor effect. That does not prove the claims false, but it means the people promoting them are not disinterested witnesses.

**A physician's view.** I am a naturopathic physician, and I do not dismiss the clinical research. Depression and addiction destroy lives, and if a supervised treatment helps, I want to know about it. But the researchers themselves warn of impaired judgment and of harmful psychological effects in vulnerable people, especially without qualified guides. The Good Friday student who ran into the street to announce the Messiah is a reminder that the same loosening that frees one person can unmoor another. Medicine for a diagnosed illness, under a physician, is one question. Seeking God through a drug is another, and I do not think the first answer settles the second.

## The Door God Opened

The video closes by saying that entheogens sit at the crossroads of chemistry and mysticism, and that is a fair description. My conclusion is that they are a door, but a door forced from the wrong side. They show a person that the walls of the material world are thinner than he thought, and that Someone is very near. They do not tell him who that Someone is, and they cannot remove what stands between them.

Jesus described Himself in exactly these terms: *"I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved"* **(John 10:9)**. And again: *"I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me"* **(John 14:6)**. The door He opens does not lower our guard; it changes our heart. It does not dissolve the self into an ocean; it brings a person, still himself, into fellowship with the Father who has been living his life alongside him all along. Paul set the two kinds of filling side by side: *"And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit"* **(Ephesians 5:18)**.

If you know someone who has had one of these experiences, I would not begin by mocking it. Begin where it is right. Agree that the material world is not all there is, and that God is closer than he imagined. Then ask the question Scripture asks: what did the presence he met say about Jesus Christ? The longing that sent him looking was real. The One who answers that longing has a name, and He is already near.

**Limits.** My sourcing leans on the mainstream academic record, and that record has blind spots. The surveys recruited volunteers online, and people with good experiences are more likely to answer a survey about them than people with frightening ones. Much of the research is done by scientists who favor psychedelic therapy, and the field depends on funding from an industry with a commercial stake. Firsthand accounts of terrifying encounters, lasting spiritual oppression, or deliverance afterward are common in Christian pastoral experience but rarely reach the journals; I treat those reports as leads that deserve study, not as proof. I relied on published summaries of the studies rather than reading every paper in full. The transcript of the video was machine-generated, and I have corrected several names it garbled (for example, *kykeon*, Leor Roseman, and Romain Rolland).
