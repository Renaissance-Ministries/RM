# 261023 - Reincarnation Fact or Fantasy

# Remembered Lives and the Appointed Death


Oct 23, 2026 · @Thomas Abshier


## A Pattern in the Archive


No one returns to this world for a second life, and yet some children speak as though they remember one. A new study from the University of Virginia presses that tension harder than any before it, and the Christian should neither wave it away nor follow it where its popular readers want to go.


Since the 1960s, the University of Virginia’s Division of Perceptual Studies has collected what it calls “cases of the reincarnation type”: young children, usually between two and six years old, who describe the life of someone who died before they were born. In the strongest cases, investigators located a real deceased person whose name, family, home, and manner of death matched what the child said. The archive is the largest of its kind.


In August 2026, researchers Saeedeh Sadeghi, Philip Cozzolino, and colleagues published an analysis of every case in that archive with an identified deceased person, 1,678 in all, in the American Psychological Association journal *Psychology of Consciousness*. They measured what they call the “intermission”: the time between the remembered person’s death and the birth of the child who seems to remember him. Among the 1,319 cases with usable dates, the median intermission was 16 months.


The striking finding is that the intermission depended on how the person died. When the death was fully expected, as with a long illness in old age, the median interval was about 21 months. When the death was sudden and unforeseen, by accident, violence, or abrupt medical crisis, the median was about 12 months, a reduction of roughly 43 percent. The more abrupt the death, the shorter the wait.


## Why the Easy Dismissals Do Not Hold


The skeptic’s first answer to any single case is that someone embellished it. A grieving family hears what it wants to hear, a story grows in the retelling, and a child absorbs details from adult conversation. These explanations are often correct for individual cases, and the researchers say so. Their point is that a consistent statistical pattern across more than a thousand cases is much harder to manufacture by accident than a single good story.


The second answer is culture. Many traditions teach that a person who dies suddenly leaves unfinished business and returns sooner. If families who hold that belief were shaping the stories, we would expect the pattern to be strongest among them. The researchers found the opposite: the effect held after controlling for the quality of the verification, the accuracy of the child’s statements, prior acquaintance between the two families, social status, and religious belief, and it was *stronger* in families whose religion did not teach reincarnation.


The researchers are careful about what this does and does not show. The relationship is modest: the manner of death accounts for only about two to three percent of the variation in intermission time. Their claim is not that the cause of death strongly predicts the interval, but that it predicts it at all, when no known ordinary mechanism connects the two. Sadeghi describes herself as agnostic about the mechanism, and says the pattern is a reason to keep looking. That is an honest posture, and I share the conviction that the right response is to look harder, not away.


## What the Finding Does to Materialism


Before asking what the cases mean, it is worth noticing whom they trouble most. The materialist holds that a person is his brain, and that when the brain dies, everything it held is gone. On that view, no information about a dead man’s life can reach a child except through ordinary channels: someone told him, he overheard it, or the investigators were fooled.


If even a small fraction of the verified cases are what they appear to be, that view fails. A three-year-old in one village names the relatives, the house, and the manner of death of a stranger from another village, and the details check out. Something carried that information across a gap that matter alone does not cross. The timing pattern adds a second difficulty: the circumstances of a death somehow bear on when the information surfaces. Nothing in the materialist picture connects the two.


The Christian has no stake in defending materialism. Scripture has always taught that the person is more than the body, that the spirit survives death, and that unseen persons act in the visible world (Ecclesiastes 12:7; Matthew 10:28; Ephesians 6:12). Data that embarrass the materialist are not a threat to the faith. The question for the believer is narrower and more important: if these children are receiving real information about the dead, *who is delivering it?*


## Why Scripture Closes the Door on Rebirth


The plainest answer is that the remembered person is not coming back. “It is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27). The writer makes the point to show that Christ’s sacrifice, like a man’s death, happens once and is complete. A single life followed by judgment is the frame for the whole argument; a cycle of returns would dissolve it.


The rest of Scripture speaks with one voice. Job says that a man who goes down to the grave “shall return no more to his house, neither shall his place know him any more” (Job 7:9–10). David, mourning his infant son, says, “I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me” (2 Samuel 12:23). In the account of the rich man and Lazarus, the dead man asks that someone be sent back to warn his brothers, and he is told that a great gulf is fixed and that the living have Moses and the prophets (Luke 16:19–31).


Two passages are often offered on the other side, and both fail on inspection. Jesus said John the Baptist was the Elijah who was to come (Matthew 11:14; 17:12), but John himself denied being Elijah (John 1:21), the angel said he would come “in the spirit and power of Elias” (Luke 1:17), and Elijah never died in the first place (2 Kings 2:11). The prophecy concerned a ministry, not a returning soul. When the disciples asked whether a man born blind had sinned before his birth, Jesus rejected the premise outright: neither he nor his parents had sinned to cause it (John 9:2–3).


The doctrine matters because of what it carries. Rebirth goes hand in hand with karma: the soul works off its debt across many lives. The gospel says the debt is paid once, by Christ, and offered freely. Reincarnation is not a neutral hypothesis about timing; it is a competing account of salvation.


## A Better Reading: Who Is Doing the Remembering


Here is the position I take. The best cases are real, in the sense that the children are receiving information about dead persons that they could not have learned by ordinary means. But the dead person is not the one delivering it. The information comes from a living spiritual agent who knew the deceased, and the child mistakes that agent’s knowledge for his own memory.


Scripture names such agents. The law forbids consulting a “familiar spirit” (Leviticus 19:31; Deuteronomy 18:10–12). The familiar spirit was the spirit who seemed to know the dead and to speak for them through a living medium. Paul met a girl in Philippi who spoke true things through a “spirit of divination,” and he cast it out in the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 16:16–18). Her statements were accurate; their source was not God. Paul also warns that Satan presents himself as an angel of light (2 Corinthians 11:14). Accuracy is not the test of a spirit; its origin and its fruit are.


This reading explains the features of the cases at least as well as reincarnation does:


- **The information is specific and verifiable.** A spirit present in a person’s life would know his name, his relatives, his house, and how he died.

- **The child is very young.** The statements typically come between two and six years old, when a child’s sense of self is still forming and he is least able to tell his own thoughts from another voice. The reports commonly fade as he grows into his own identity.

- **The deaths are disproportionately sudden and violent.** Investigators have long noted that violent deaths are overrepresented in these cases. Scripture locates evil spirits where murder, terror, and despair are concentrated, since the devil “was a murderer from the beginning” (John 8:44).

- **The timing follows the manner of death.** If a spirit attached to a violent death seeks a new host, a shorter interval after a sudden death is what one would expect: the spirit moves while the circumstances are fresh, rather than waiting for a life to wind down.


The last point deserves care. The researchers note that many traditions see an abrupt death as leaving unfinished business that drives a quicker return. That intuition may be tracking something real, but it has misidentified the traveler. What lingers after a violent death is not the dead man’s soul, which has gone to judgment, but the spirits who were party to his death or drawn to it.


There is one other candidate. Some researchers have proposed that the child somehow draws information from living minds, the grieving family of the deceased. That view keeps the information real and removes the reincarnation. It explains the specificity but does less well with the timing, since the pattern is tied to how the person died, not to the grief of the survivors. Either way, the remembered person is not the speaker.


## Why the Concern Stands, and Where the Uncertainty Lies


I stand on the side of Scripture: no one returns for a second life, and the verified cases are best explained as spiritual contact that impersonates the dead. I hold that position open to a better argument, and the reader should know where the uncertainty lies.


**What we cannot know from this study.** The archive rests on interviews, sometimes taken months or years after the child first spoke. Investigators worked hard to rule out contamination, but testimony is never as clean as measurement. The effect is small, and a statistical association, however consistent, does not reveal its cause. A conventional explanation may still emerge. For example, a sudden or violent death is more talked about in a region than a quiet death in old age, and a recent, well-known death may be easier to match to a child’s statements; that could shorten the apparent intervals without anything anomalous at work. The researchers’ controls for prior acquaintance and verification quality reduce this concern, but I cannot say they eliminate it.


**What we cannot know from Scripture alone.** The Bible does not describe these cases, and it does not tell us the mechanism by which a spirit might supply a young child with another person’s history. The familiar-spirit reading is an inference from what Scripture says spirits do, applied to a phenomenon it does not discuss.


**Why the concern remains plausible all the same.** First, there is an intuitive sense of wrongness in a small child grieving for a wife he never had or terrified of the way a stranger died; that is not the innocence God gave children, and many parents in these cases have felt it. Second, reincarnation belief is spreading in the West, and ScienceAlert reports that over 30 percent of Americans now hold it; each new headline about “evidence for past lives” moves that number in one direction. Third, the doctrine travels with a system. Where reincarnation is believed, it supports karma, works-based salvation, and in some cultures a social order that treats suffering as deserved. Where it is believed about a particular child, families sometimes encourage him to take up the dead person’s identity, relationships, and even name. Ideas about the soul do not stay in the journals; they shape how parents raise children and how societies treat the suffering.


## Counsel for the Believer


The right response to this research is neither fear nor ridicule. Ridicule fails because the data are not foolish; serious people gathered them carefully over sixty years, and the pattern they found is real enough to deserve an explanation. Fear fails because Christ has already “spoiled principalities and powers” and made a show of them openly (Colossians 2:15). Nothing in these cases is beyond His authority.


For a parent whose child begins to speak of another life, the counsel is simple. Do not encourage the child to take on the identity of the person he describes, and do not seek out the other family to confirm it. Keep telling him who he is: your son, made by God, with one life of his own to live. Pray over him in the name of Jesus, and ask a mature pastor or elder to pray with you. The reports usually fade with time; help them fade.


For everyone else, these cases are a reminder of what is at stake in the doctrine of one life. Reincarnation offers another chance; the gospel offers something better. It offers a debt paid in full, a judgment already borne by Christ for those who trust Him, and a resurrection in which each person rises as himself, not as someone else (1 Corinthians 15:42–44; 1 John 3:2). The child who seems to remember a stranger’s death needs what every one of us needs: not a return to this world, but the life that death cannot touch.
