# 261120 - The Mask of Satan - Commentary

# The Mask of Satan - Commentary:


### Margo's Case That the Adversary Was Made, Not Fallen

by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 20 November 2026

## The question

My wife, Margo Diann Abshier, ND, has written a short book called *The Mask of Satan*. Its thesis runs against nearly two thousand years of Christian teaching: Satan is not a fallen angel. He was never Lucifer, never a glorious being who rebelled in heaven before the world began. He is a creature that God made for a purpose, on a leash God holds, and the "Shining One" of Isaiah and Ezekiel who fell from glory was not Satan at all. It was Adam.

I want to set out her argument as clearly as I can, follow it through the Scriptures she uses, and say where I think it is strongest and where it will meet the most resistance. I write as someone who has watched her hold this view for as long as I have known her, and who has come to think it answers questions the traditional story leaves hanging.

The question underneath the question is this: is God in charge of this world, or is He managing a rebellion He did not foresee? Margo's answer is that He has been in charge from the first word He spoke.

## The theory in brief

Margo's case rests on six claims, each built from Scripture:


 	- **Satan was made, not fallen.** Everything in heaven and earth, including the principalities and powers we wrestle against, was created through Christ. Satan was a murderer and a sinner "from the beginning," so he was never a good angel who later went bad.

 	- **Satan is under God's control.** In Job, God raises the subject, sets the limits, and Satan obeys them. He is an accuser and a tester working within boundaries God draws.

 	- **The Shining One was Adam.** Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 describe a man who dies, rots, and is turned to ashes, who was in Eden on the day he was created, and who wore the stones of the high priest's breastplate. That is Adam, the first king and priest of the earth, not a spirit.

 	- **"Angel" means messenger.** The Hebrew *malak* and Greek *aggelos* are used of prophets and priests as well as heavenly beings. The "angels who sinned" in Peter and Jude can be read as false human messengers, like Balaam.

 	- **Evil exists so that we can overcome.** God placed the tree in the garden to give real choice, and He subjected creation to futility "in hope." Earth is a school for overcomers, and Satan is the opponent the training requires.

 	- **The story ends in restoration.** Because God created the adversary and holds the keys of death and hell, the end of His plan is that every knee bows and God becomes all in all. Hell is real and the last penny will be paid, but it is not endless.


She closes with a more speculative chapter about the "sons of God" in Genesis 6 and the possibility that Adam's line mixed with earlier human-like creatures. I treat that separately, because the main argument does not depend on it.

## Nothing evil at the end of the sixth day

Margo begins where Scripture begins. *"And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good. And the evening and the morning were the sixth day"* **(Genesis 1:31)**. If everything was very good when Adam and Eve were created, there was no rebel angel lurking in the cosmos. The traditional story, which places Lucifer's fall in an age before Genesis, has to read a gap into the text that the text does not show. Margo notes that this pre-creation drama is told most vividly by Milton, not by Moses.

She then sets two of John's statements beside each other. Jesus said the devil *"was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him"* **(John 8:44)**. John wrote that *"the devil sinneth from the beginning"* **(1 John 3:8)**. If Satan had once been a faithful angel who later rebelled, those statements would be false. He would have been truthful once, and sinless once. John says neither.

Next she asks who made the powers we struggle against. Paul names them: *"For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places"* **(Ephesians 6:12)**. And Paul names their maker: *"For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him"* **(Colossians 1:16)**. The same words, principalities and powers, appear in both. Christ made them.

Finally, God says it of Himself: *"I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things"* **(Isaiah 45:7)**. Most modern translations render the Hebrew *ra* as "calamity" or "disaster." Margo points out that *ra* is the same word used for the tree of the knowledge of good and evil **(Genesis 2:9)**, and if it means evil there, it can mean evil here. She pairs it with *"I have created the waster to destroy"* **(Isaiah 54:16)**.

She tentatively suggests that the dark powers were made early on the seventh day, after God had pronounced the sixth day good. Genesis says God "ended his work" on the seventh day but does not call that work good. I hold this timing loosely, as she does. The core point doesn't depend on it: whenever the adversary was made, he was made for a purpose.

## Job: the adversary on a leash

The clearest picture of how God and Satan relate is in the first two chapters of Job. It is God, not Satan, who brings up Job's name: *"Hast thou considered my servant Job, that there is none like him in the earth?"* **(Job 1:8)**. Satan asks God to act: *"put forth thine hand now, and touch all that he hath"* **(Job 1:11)**. God sets the boundary: *"only upon himself put not forth thine hand"* **(Job 1:12)**, and later, *"but save his life"* **(Job 2:6)**. Satan goes out and does exactly what he is permitted, and nothing more.

There is no war in this scene. There is a sovereign and a servant with a hard job. Satan plays the role Revelation gives him, *"the accuser of our brethren"* **(Revelation 12:10)**, but the accusation is filed in God's court, on God's docket, under God's rules.

Margo then asks why God allowed it, and draws four answers from the book itself:


 	- **Refining.** *"When he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold"* **(Job 23:10)**. Peter says the same of every believer's faith, *"though it be tried with fire"* **(1 Peter 1:7)**.

 	- **Self-righteousness.** Job's friends stopped answering him *"because he was righteous in his own eyes"* **(Job 32:1)**.

 	- **Fear.** *"For the thing which I greatly feared is come upon me"* **(Job 3:25)**. Job sacrificed for his children out of dread, not trust in a rewarder **(Job 1:5)**.

 	- **Forgiveness.** *"And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends"* **(Job 42:10)**.


The result was not merely restoration but sight: *"I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee"* **(Job 42:5)**. On Margo's reading, the adversary was the instrument by which Job came to know God for himself. The trial was not God losing a round to Satan. It was God finishing Job.

## The Shining One was Adam: Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28

The whole fallen-angel doctrine rests on two passages, and this is where Margo does her most careful work. Both are addressed to earthly kings, Isaiah's to the king of Babylon **(Isaiah 14:4)** and Ezekiel's to the prince and king of Tyre **(Ezekiel 28:2, 12)**. Both reach behind those kings to an earlier figure who fell from great height. The tradition says that figure is Satan. Margo says it is Adam.

### The name

*"How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!"* **(Isaiah 14:12)**. "Lucifer" is Latin, not Hebrew. The Hebrew *helel* means "shining one" or "day star," and its Greek equivalent, *phosphoros*, is applied to Christ: *"until the day dawn, and the day star arise in your hearts"* **(2 Peter 1:19)**. Jesus calls Himself *"the bright and morning star"* **(Revelation 22:16)**. The Amplified Bible's own footnote on Isaiah 14:12 concedes that the identification of this figure with Satan dates from the third century and rests on reading Luke 10:18 as a commentary on Isaiah, which some scholars doubt.

If the morning star is a title of Christ, and Christ is called the last Adam **(1 Corinthians 15:45)**, and the first Adam is *"the figure of him that was to come"* **(Romans 5:14)**, then the first morning star may well be the first Adam.

### A man who dies

Margo's sharpest observation is that Isaiah's figure has a body. *"Thy pomp is brought down to the grave… the worm is spread under thee, and the worms cover thee"* **(Isaiah 14:11)**. Onlookers ask, *"Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?"* **(Isaiah 14:16)**. He is *"cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch… as a carcase trodden under feet"* **(Isaiah 14:19)**. Spirits do not have carcasses.

Ezekiel says the same of his figure: *"yet thou art a man, and not God"* **(Ezekiel 28:2)**, and *"thou shalt be a man, and no God, in the hand of him that slayeth thee"* **(Ezekiel 28:9)**. The end is *"I will bring thee to ashes upon the earth"* **(Ezekiel 28:18)**, which echoes the sentence on Adam: *"for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return"* **(Genesis 3:19)**.

### A man in Eden, on the day he was created

*"Thou hast been in Eden the garden of God… in the day that thou wast created"* **(Ezekiel 28:13)**. Eden was planted for Adam and Adam was placed in it **(Genesis 2:8)**. It was his home. Satan entered it as an intruder, through the serpent. Margo's point is simple: you do not describe a house by naming the thief who broke into it. You name the owner.

*"Thou wast perfect in thy ways from the day that thou wast created, till iniquity was found in thee"* **(Ezekiel 28:15)**. The verb for "created" is *bara*, the same verb used for the creation of man **(Genesis 1:27)**. And "perfect" is the condition Genesis assigns to everything at the end of the sixth day, before the fall. By Jesus's own words, Satan was never in that condition.

### The king-priest's breastplate

*"Every precious stone was thy covering"* **(Ezekiel 28:13)**. Margo shows that the stones listed are stones of the high priest's breastplate **(Exodus 39:10–13)**, set in gold. In her reading, Adam was the original king and priest of the earth, given dominion **(Genesis 1:28)** and walking with God in the cool of the day **(Genesis 3:8)**. The same stones return as the foundations of the New Jerusalem **(Revelation 21:19–20)**, the bride of the last Adam, whose people are made *"kings and priests unto God"* **(Revelation 1:6)**. What the first Adam lost, the last Adam restores to His body.

### The anointed cherub that covers

**Ezekiel 28:14** is the verse most often taken to describe an angel: *"Thou art the anointed cherub that covereth."* Margo works through the Hebrew word by word. The word rendered "anointed" is *mimshach*, meaning expansion or outspreading, close in form to *mimshal*, dominion. "Cherub" can mean one grasped or held fast. "Covereth" is *sakak*, to entwine, screen, and protect, the word used of the cherubim's wings over the mercy seat. Read this way, the verse describes Adam's commission to spread out over the earth, subdue it, and shelter it, as a hen covers her brood. She notes that Lamsa's Aramaic text reads "you were *with* the anointed cherub," which would make the figure a man set beside the guardian, not the guardian himself.

The verse goes on: *"thou wast upon the holy mountain of God; thou hast walked up and down in the midst of the stones of fire."* Margo connects "walked up and down" (*halak*) and "in the midst" (*tavek*, between the halves) to the covenant ceremony of Genesis 15, where God passed between the divided animals, and to the elders on Sinai who saw God standing on a pavement of sapphire **(Exodus 24:10)**. Covenant is cut with those made in God's image. Scripture records no covenant between God and an angel.

### What Adam wanted

Isaiah's figure says, *"I will exalt my throne above the stars of God… I will be like the most High"* **(Isaiah 14:13–14)**. Margo notes that the serpent's temptation was exactly this, offered to the man and woman: *"ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil"* **(Genesis 3:5)**. The serpent did not say "you will be like me." Being like God was a live temptation only for creatures already made in God's image. God's own verdict afterward was, *"Behold, the man is become as one of us"* **(Genesis 3:22)**.

And the figure *"weakened the nations"* and *"made the world as a wilderness"* **(Isaiah 14:12, 17)**. Adam did that. Through him the ground was cursed and grew thorns **(Genesis 3:17–18)**, and *"in Adam all die"* **(1 Corinthians 15:22)**. He is the one who *"opened not the house of his prisoners"* **(Isaiah 14:17)**: no one he condemned to death could return home, until the last Adam led captivity captive.

## Messengers and fallen messengers

The second pillar of the tradition is the doctrine of fallen angels who followed Lucifer. Margo answers it by asking what the word "angel" means. The Hebrew *malak* means messenger. The King James translators rendered it "messenger" 98 times and "angel" 111 times. The Greek *aggelos* is the same. Prophets and priests are called messengers: *"Then spake Haggai the LORD's messenger in the LORD's message unto the people"* **(Haggai 1:13)**. Whenever we read "angel," Margo asks us to read "messenger" and see whether the passage changes.

The two texts used for a heavenly rebellion are 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6. *"God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment"* **(2 Peter 2:4)**. Margo makes two observations. First, both chapters address false teachers and false prophets within the church, and both compare them to Balaam, a prophet for hire. The subject is false messengers. Second, these messengers are already *in chains* until the judgment. Satan and his powers are not in chains. Paul says we still wrestle with them **(Ephesians 6:12)**, and Peter says the devil still prowls *"as a roaring lion"* **(1 Peter 5:8)**. Whoever is chained in Jude is not the active adversary.

She then gives two examples of human messengers who fell. Saul was anointed king, received the spirit of prophecy, rejected the word of the Lord, and died *fallen* (*naphal*) on Gilboa, beheaded, his armor hung in a pagan temple **(1 Samuel 31:8–10)**. John the Baptist, whom Jesus called *"my messenger"* **(Luke 7:27)**, doubted from prison, was beheaded, and his body is called a *ptoma*, a "fallen thing" **(Mark 6:29)**. Margo is careful here. She does not judge either man's eternal state. Her point is only that Scripture shows human messengers falling, and shows no clear case of a heavenly one doing so.

She does not deny that the devil has messengers. Jesus speaks of *"everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels"* **(Matthew 25:41)**. She suggests that the devil's messengers, like God's, are both spirits and men.

## Satan falling like lightning: Luke 10

The tradition links Isaiah 14 to Jesus's words: *"I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven"* **(Luke 10:18)**. Margo reads the verse in its setting. The seventy had just returned from preaching and healing in pairs, saying, *"Lord, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name"* **(Luke 10:17)**. Jesus's answer is about what they had just done, not about an event before the creation of the world. The Gospels record Him saying it only once, and it follows the first time His followers did His works without Him present.

Where did Satan fall from? Margo points to Jesus's own definition: *"the kingdom of God is within you"* **(Luke 17:21)**, and she shows from parallel passages that Jesus used "kingdom of God" and "kingdom of heaven" interchangeably. When a demon is cast out of a person, Satan loses ground in the heavenly realm that person was made to be. *"Heaven"* in this verse is the territory of human lives, and the battle for it is fought one person at a time.

This also fits Paul's present tense. Satan still holds a place *"in high places"* **(Ephesians 6:12)**. His final expulsion is still future **(Revelation 12:7–9)**. A fall completed before Adam would leave Revelation with nothing to describe.

## Why God would make an adversary

The first objection anyone raises is moral: if God created the evil one, is God not the author of evil? Margo's answer has three parts.

**Choice requires a real alternative.** God placed the tree in the garden so that love could be chosen. You cannot choose to love and follow God unless there is something else to choose. God did not loose evil on the world; Adam did, by eating what he had been told not to eat. Eve was deceived, but *"Adam was not deceived"* **(1 Timothy 2:14)**. He chose with open eyes.

**Overcoming requires an opponent.** *"For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope"* **(Romans 8:20)**. The promises of Revelation 2 and 3 are made *"to him that overcometh,"* and the last is a seat on Christ's throne **(Revelation 3:21)**. There is no overcoming without something to overcome. Margo calls this world the Overcomers Through Faith in Jesus School. The tests are fitted to the student: *"God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able"* **(1 Corinthians 10:13)**.

**Redemption was planned before the test.** Christ is *"the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world"* **(Revelation 13:8)**. God did not create an adversary and then scramble when things went wrong. The Savior was in the plan before the serpent was in the garden.

Margo adds another distinction. The Father begot the Son, and the Son made everything else, including the adversary. *"No one is good but One, that is, God"* **(Mark 10:18)**. The Father is kept pure from any contact with evil. The Son took on Himself the creation, control, and defeat of evil, and He did it by obeying the Father in everything.

I would add a thought from my own teaching, which I think strengthens hers. I hold that God lives inside every human life and experiences it from within. If so, God is not a distant designer who built a hazard and stepped back. He walks through every trial the adversary is permitted to bring, in the person who suffers it, and He feels every choice we make in it. A God who made the adversary and then experiences its work from the inside is not the author of our suffering in the sense the objection fears. He is its fellow sufferer, and the Cross is where that becomes visible.

## The end of the story: the keys of death and hell

If God made the adversary and holds his leash, the story's ending changes. Margo follows that logic all the way. God's stated pleasure is that all be saved: He is *"not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance"* **(2 Peter 3:9)**. God's purpose cannot fail: *"My counsel shall stand, and I will do all my pleasure"* **(Isaiah 46:10)**. Put those two together, and the conclusion follows. The plan is *"to reconcile all things unto himself… whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven"* **(Colossians 1:20)**, and Jesus said, *"I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me"* **(John 12:32)**.

She addresses the obvious objection, eternal punishment, through the words translated "eternal." The Hebrew *olam* and the Greek *aion* and *aionios* mean an age, or age-lasting: a vast and indefinite span, not necessarily an endless one. Robert Young, of *Young's Analytical Concordance*, held this view. On Margo's reading, the lake of fire is real and terrible, but it is a refiner's fire. *"For every one shall be salted with fire"* **(Mark 9:49)**. The Lord comes *"like a refiner's fire"* **(Malachi 3:2)**. And Jesus's own words about the prison are *"Thou shalt by no means come out thence, till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing"* **(Matthew 5:26)**, a sentence with an end.

Her most interesting textual point is in the last two chapters of Revelation. The list of those in the lake of fire **(Revelation 21:8)** and the list of those *"without"* the city **(Revelation 22:15)** are nearly the same: sorcerers, whoremongers, murderers, idolaters, liars. Those outside the city are still within reach. The gates *"shall not be shut at all"* **(Revelation 21:25)**, the leaves of the tree are *"for the healing of the nations"* **(Revelation 22:2)**, and *"the Spirit and the bride say, Come…** And whosoever will, let him take th**e water of life freely"* **(Revelation 22:17)**.

Her confidence rests on Christ's own word: *"I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death"* **(Revelation 1:18)**. She puts it in her own plain terms. She believes in sickness, but she believes Jesus is bigger than sickness. She believes in death, but He raised Lazarus. She believes in hell, but He has the keys, and He means to use them.

She does not let this become an excuse. Justice will be done, and the last penny paid. She warns anyone tempted to take his chances that hell is worse than anything on earth, and that time there may feel far longer than time here. She also gives two reasons to evangelize: love of neighbor and the watchman's duty, *"his blood will I require at the watchman's hand"* **(Ezekiel 33:6)**.

## Where the argument meets resistance

I stand with Margo on her central claim: Scripture does not teach that Satan was a glorious angel who fell before the creation of Adam, and it does teach that the adversary operates entirely under God's authority. But a reader should know where the argument is strongest, where it is a minority view, and where it is speculation.

**Strongest: Isaiah 14 is not about Satan.** Here Margo has a good deal of scholarly company. Most commentators read Isaiah 14 as a taunt against the king of Babylon, using the image of the morning star that rises bright and is quickly swallowed by the sun. Reading it as Satan's biography is a later Christian addition, and the Amplified footnote she cites makes that clear.

**Strong: Ezekiel 28 describes a man in Eden.** Many scholars also read Ezekiel 28 as drawing on a story of the first man in God's garden. The Greek Septuagint, like the Aramaic she quotes, reads verse 14 as "I placed you *with* the cherub," which supports her reading over the King James. The step from "a primal man" to "Adam specifically" is hers, and it is a natural one. The detail that fits least well is *"By the multitude of thy merchandise"* **(Ezekiel 28:16)**, which plainly describes Tyre's trade. Margo's explanation, that Adam's line traded with other peoples, is possible but not established.

**Contested: the war in heaven.** The strongest text against her is this: *"the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan… and his angels were cast out with him."* **(Revelation 12:9)**. Traditional readers take this as the primeval fall. Margo places it in the future, at the fifth trumpet. Her reading fits Paul's present tense in Ephesians 6, but readers should know this is the passage they will be asked about.

**Contested: the meaning of "eternal."** The age-lasting reading of *aion* has ancient advocates, including Origen and Gregory of Nyssa. The objection she will hear most is the verse where the same word describes both punishment and life: *"these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal."* **(Matthew 25:46)**. If one is endless, the critic says, so is the other. Her answer would be that *aionios* describes the quality of the age, the life of the coming age and the correction of the coming age, not its length.

**A departure from the creeds: the begetting of the Son.** Margo reads Genesis 1:3, "Let there be light," as the moment the Father brought forth the Son. The Nicene Creed holds that the Son is eternally begotten, and readers will point to *"In the beginning was the Word"* **(John 1:1)**, which says the Word already *was*, and to Jesus's prayer for *"the glory which I had with thee before the world was"* **(John 17:5)**. Her central argument about Satan does not depend on this point. It depends only on Colossians 1:16, that the powers were made through Christ.

**Speculation, offered as questions.** The book also raises several ideas Margo herself presents as possibilities, not doctrine: that Adam built the Great Pyramid as an empty tomb; that the "coats of skins" God made in Genesis 3:21 were the bodies of earlier human-like creatures, which would explain pain in childbirth and the wives of Cain; and that some disciples who heard Matthew 16:28 may still be alive. I read these as she wrote them, as questions to test against Scripture. The case that Satan was made, not fallen, stands without any of them.

## Why it matters

Margo takes her title from Paul: *"for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light"* **(2 Corinthians 11:14)**. The Greek word means to disguise oneself. If Satan really had been a glorious angel of light, Paul would not call it a disguise. In her reading, the mask is the whole story of a great rebel who nearly matched God. That story serves the adversary well. No one follows a loser, and a Satan who once fought God to a standstill is a Satan worth fearing and worth serving.

The difference the truth makes is practical. If Satan is a loose cannon from a cosmic war, then God is cleaning up after him, and our trials are collateral damage. If Satan is a creature on a leash, then every trial passes through God's hand first, and *"all things work together for good to them that love God"* **(Romans 8:28)** is a statement of fact, not a hope.

It also puts responsibility where it belongs. "The devil made me do it" has no standing in this view. The adversary may accuse, test, and tempt within his limits, but we make our choices, and *"by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned"* **(Matthew 12:37)**. The prescription follows: *"Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you"* **(James 4:7)**. Margo also warns against the opposite error of treating the adversary lightly or reviling him, since even Michael *"durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee"* **(Jude 9)**.

And it restores Adam to his place in the story. The great fall in Scripture is not an angel's. It is the fall of the man made in God's image, crowned king and priest of the earth, who wanted to be like God on his own terms and brought death on everyone after him. That makes the rescue a human story too. *"For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive"* **(1 Corinthians 15:22)**. The last Adam did what the first would not: He obeyed. The morning star the first Adam lost, Christ now offers to those who overcome: *"And I will give him the morning star"* **(Revelation 2:28)**.

Margo closes her book with an invitation I want to close with too: test this against the Scriptures yourself, and do not accept or reject it because of whom you admire. She came to it through study with her mother over many years, and she holds it because the pieces kept fitting. I have found the same.

**Subject:** This Sunday: Margo’s case that Satan was made, not fallen


Dear friends,


For this Sunday I’ve written an essay on Margo’s book, *The Mask of Satan*. Its thesis runs against almost two thousand years of Christian teaching. Satan was never Lucifer, a glorious angel who rebelled before the world began. God made him for a purpose and keeps him on a leash. And the “Shining One” who fell in Isaiah and Ezekiel was Adam. Here is an outline of the essay’s points:


**1. The question underneath.** Is God in charge of this world, or is He managing a rebellion He didn’t foresee? Margo says He has been in charge since the first word He spoke.


**2. Nothing evil at the end of the sixth day.**


 	- God called everything “very good” when He created Adam and Eve, so no fallen angel was already in the picture.

 	- Jesus said the devil was “a murderer from the beginning,” and John wrote that he “sinneth from the beginning.” Neither statement fits an angel who was once faithful.

 	- Paul says Christ made the principalities and powers (Colossians 1:16), the same powers we wrestle against (Ephesians 6:12).

 	- God says of Himself, “I make peace, and create evil” (Isaiah 45:7).


**3. Job: the adversary on a leash.**


 	- God raises the subject of Job, sets the limits, and Satan obeys them. Nobody is at war in that scene.

 	- The trial refined Job’s faith, exposed his self-righteousness and his fear, and taught him to forgive.

 	- It ended with Job seeing God for himself.


**4. The Shining One was Adam (Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28).**


 	- “Lucifer” is a Latin word. In Hebrew, it means “day star,” and “morning star” is also a title of Christ, the last Adam.

 	- The fallen figure has a body: worms, a carcass, ashes. He is called “a man, and not God.”

 	- He was in Eden “in the day that thou wast created.” Eden was Adam’s home, and Satan was the intruder.

 	- He wore the stones of the high priest’s breastplate, which marks Adam as the first king and priest of the earth.

 	- “I will be like the most High” is the very temptation the serpent offered Adam and Eve.


**5. Messengers and fallen messengers.**


 	- “Angel” means messenger, and Scripture also uses the word for prophets and priests.

 	- The “angels that sinned” in Peter and Jude appear in passages about false teachers. Those angels are already in chains, while Satan is still active.

 	- Saul and John the Baptist are examples of human messengers who “fell.”


**6. Satan falling like lightning (Luke 10).** Jesus said this when the seventy came back from casting out demons. It describes the ground Satan loses in human lives, not a fall before creation.


**7. Why God would make an adversary.**


 	- Choice needs a real alternative, and overcoming needs an opponent.

 	- The Lamb was slain “from the foundation of the world,” so God planned redemption before the test.

 	- The Father is kept pure from evil. The Son made the adversary, controls him, and defeated him.

 	- My own addition: God lives inside every life and suffers each trial with us from within.


**8. The end of the story: the keys of death and hell.**


 	- God wills that all be saved, and His purpose cannot fail.

 	- The words translated “eternal” mean “age-lasting.” Hell is real, and the last penny will be paid, but it is a refining fire.

 	- The people in the lake of fire are the same people listed outside the New Jerusalem, whose gates are never shut.

 	- Christ holds the keys, and He will use them.


**9. Where the argument meets resistance.**


 	- Strongest: many scholars agree that Isaiah 14 is not about Satan.

 	- Strong: Ezekiel 28 describes a man in Eden.

 	- Contested: the war in heaven in Revelation 12, and the meaning of “eternal” in Matthew 25:46.

 	- Her reading of the Son's begetting departs from the Nicene Creed.

 	- Speculative ideas, which she offers only as questions: Adam and the pyramid, the “coats of skins,” and the possibility that some disciples are still living.


**10. Why it matters.**


 	- The “mask” is the myth that Satan is a great rival to God, and that myth serves him.

 	- If Satan is on a leash, every trial passes through God’s hand first, and “all things work together for good” is a fact.

 	- “The devil made me do it” no longer works as an excuse. We answer for our own choices.

 	- The great fall in Scripture was Adam’s, and the last Adam restores what the first one lost.
