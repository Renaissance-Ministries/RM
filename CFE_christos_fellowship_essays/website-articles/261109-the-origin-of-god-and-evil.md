# 261109 - The Origin of God and Evil

## God, Evil, and Whether Satan Can Be Saved


*Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 9 November 2026*


**Plain Language summary.** On the morning of Monday, 9 November 2026, Thomas and Isak spent the first ninety minutes of their call on a question Susan had raised after Sunday’s fellowship: can Satan repent and be saved? Thomas answered that it depends on who Satan is. If Satan’s existence is integral with evil, he cannot repent and leave his post. Evil is not a rebel who might come home. It is the whole collection of everything God has declared He is not. The boundary between who God is and what evil is makes existence and experience possible; without it, goodness would have no existence to stand in. This essay follows how Thomas reached that answer, how Isak tested it, and what it means for the claim that Christians worship three gods.


### How the question arose


At Sunday’s Christos Fellowship, Thomas had described God as one, in the sense that God is everything. The idea came up in a discussion of marriage: its purpose, and what gives it satisfaction. Leonard and Michael both rejected the idea as nonsense. Michael offered a satirical version. In his version, God was alone with nothing but Himself, and He made creation into a kind of movie so that the story would have a plot. Thomas said the parody was cartoonish rather than accurate, but that it did not catch him off guard. He has worked on that very problem for a long time.


After the meeting, Susan asked Thomas two things. The first was whether Satan can repent, or be saved. Thomas had answered then that he did not know. The second was whether he could present the faith some other way than “God is everything”: simply by showing people how God is good in their lives. Thomas answered the second question near the end of the call with Isak. He answered the first in what follows.


### The problem of the solitary player


Thomas put the difficulty Michael was satirizing this way: if God is the only player, how can the game mean anything? A man who plays chess against himself cannot be surprised, or excited to win. The problem is that all drama is meaningless if God is fundamentally alone.


The only solution Thomas has found, the only way to give real feeling and meaning to a drama whose players are all the same Person, is to keep the players from seeing the whole game. From where they stand, they must not see or know their true identity. God sees all of it; the players inside it do not. Each player has limited knowledge of the options, of the other player’s motives, and of what the other player knows. Each also models what the other thinks he thinks, and what he thinks the other thinks he thinks, in a loop with no end. At some point no finite mind can predict the other’s move with certainty. “If it isn’t free will,” Thomas said, “it has every marking of it.”


Under that uncertainty, a player decides by what he actually wants to create, not by calculating how to win. God may know what each player will choose from his limited information. The real question is the motive behind the move. Does a player win to dominate, or to build the kingdom? A player of the second kind is concerned to make the other man’s move one that benefits them both. When both players play for that double win, each loves the other. God, who is present on both sides, gets to experience that love from each side. Thomas called that the Kingdom of Heaven: everyone working for everyone else’s well-being. It is the answer to the prayer *“Thy kingdom come, Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven”* **(Matthew 6:10)**.


He recalled a song from his years in Sikh Dharma and the 3HO movement: God is the boat, and God is the boatman; like the rain and like the sun, both fall the same on everyone. The line echoes Jesus’ own words: *“for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust”* **(Matthew 5:45)**. But that truth does not answer the hard question: what to do with an enemy. The aim is to bring him to blessing and redemption, so that his experience of life becomes good. The difficulty is the man who behaves badly and does not see what he needs to see.


### Win-win and win-lose systems


Thomas set two systems side by side. Most religions teach care for the other person’s well-being, Islam included. But in Islam, he said, that care extends more to those inside the system. Its structure toward outsiders is win-lose: if you have not declared allegiance to the prophet and god of Islam, you will be taxed, or worse. In Christ’s system, when someone opposes me, my task is to make his life better. That is the meaning of *“Love your enemies”* **(Matthew 5:44)** and *“Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good”* **(Romans 12:21)**. Thomas said this posture pleases God on both sides and produces a world God finds pleasant to live in.


### Righteous war and the verdict of conscience


What, then, is a righteous war? Two warriors may face each other, each believing his cause is right. From God’s point of view, one of them may be wrong. Thomas thinks a man who is wrong but sincerely acts on what he believes is right may receive a lighter punishment. But he will certainly not receive the blessing of living the truth. Jesus drew the same line: *“And that servant, which knew his lord’s will, and prepared not himself, neither did according to his will, shall be beaten with many stripes. But he that knew not, and did commit things worthy of stripes, shall be beaten with few stripes”* **(Luke 12:47–48)**. God has given a standard, the Bible, which reveals what pleases Him and what does not. Going against a revealed standard has consequences.


The hardest case turns out to be the easiest. Hitler’s regime believed, or claimed to believe, that its cause was good. Yet its shame and its efforts to hide the camps show that its conscience had already convicted it. Paul describes that inner witness in those who have the law *“written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another”* **(Romans 2:15)**. What Hitler and his company actually served was a lesser god: power, or a sense of purity or beauty. Their condemnation stands because their own conscience condemned them first.


### Why God partitions Himself


That raised a deeper puzzle. If everything arose from one primal existence, from which God Himself arose, then the condemned are made of the same substance as God. Condemnation would then seem to be God condemning God, and sealing off part of the whole so that He is not polluted by it. That is really the question of what hell is: a place where God says, in effect, “I will not include that in My experience.”


To keep the argument clear, this essay uses two terms. The **primal existence** is the whole: everything that is, including what God has rejected. **God** is the One who arose within it, said “I am,” and defined Himself as good. Scripture shows that act of division at the start of creation: *“And God saw the light, that it was good: and God divided the light from the darkness”* **(Genesis 1:4)**.


Thomas resolved the puzzle this way. God is committed to love, and love is what gives Him joy. *“God is love”* **(1 John 4:8)**. If some aspect of the whole has chosen to oppose what gives God joy, and actually enjoys the opposite, then there must be a war against that part. The God who is love is the One we are allied with when we love and serve Him. Thomas said that he has long been reluctant to attribute the arising of any negative trait to God. But the fact that all things arose from the primal existence requires a more specific framing of whom we are calling God.


### “I am” and “I am not”


Thomas admitted that he cannot explain how God arose. No one created Him, nothing created Him, and yet before God there was nothing. He has no concept, and sees no possibility, for explaining how God arose from literally nothing. So he asked instead what the first thing present would have been when God arose.


His answer is a sequence of distinctions:


- The first was between what was conscious and what was not: the awake and the asleep.

- The next was higher: “I am” and “I am not.” The “I am” perspective loves life, and acknowledges and embraces existence. It is the name God gave Moses: *“I AM THAT I AM”* **(Exodus 3:14)**. The “I am not” perspective also requires awareness. It is a step above sleep, yet it denies that it exists. The declaration “I am not” is therefore a lie: it denies what is. The pole that is aware of itself and declares its own non-existence is committed to sleep, unconsciousness and death. To stay, live and be in the “I am not” amounts to a declaration against existence: *I hate creation; creation has pain and suffering in it; all life is suffering; I stand on the side of non-existence.* Jesus described its head in exactly these terms: *“He was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it”* **(John 8:44)**.

- Each time God rose to a higher level of awareness, He assigned the opposite pole to the dark side and said, “I am not that.” I am love; I am not hate. I am not pain, not death, not darkness. With each refinement of who God says He is, and who He is not, the “I am not” side gains more character and more existence.

- Good and evil, in the developed moral sense, came much later. They arose among complex beings in relationship, with feelings rising from bodily sensation, and with ways of treating one another.


The dark side was filled up, pole by pole, with everything God is not. In that sense God “created” all of it, but only in the sense that He was aware of it and disowned it. Isaiah records God saying as much: *“I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things”* **(Isaiah 45:7)**. So hell is not a stain in God’s character. God did not create hell as a thing He is. He created heaven, and with it “not heaven.” Hell arose as the natural opposite pole and populated the realm of the “I am not.” Each time God defined Himself by another entity, trait or principle, its opposite took form there. Once He defined and chose good, the possibility of the not-good came into existence at once. He rejected it, and all of the not-good gathered in the “I am not” realm. Thomas said that existence itself needed that polarity: existence is a thing, and non-existence became a thing because of existence. The not-thing went to the dark side.


As God filled His “I am” kingdom, the “I am not” kingdom was filled and completed to match. The kingdom opposing God is as complete and full as the Kingdom of Heaven, because everything in heaven has its opposite: for every beauty, a not-beauty. Its titular head, whether we call him Satan, Lucifer or Evil, can pitch his realm as more interesting, more fun and more rewarding. The spirit of the “I am not” says that harps, clouds and rainbows are boring: come take a walk on the wild side. Polarity, he argues, is what real enjoyment, satisfaction and fullness of life require: the freedom to do every possible thing and to take real risks.


God’s answer is that the polarity exists on His side too, but as something rejected. The not-beauty is always there and visible. It will exist for eternity, because God will exist for eternity and will go on rejecting it. That takes away every selling point Satan has. Light has darkness as its comparison, and that comparison gives the light its texture. Yet the light itself holds no darkness: *“God is light, and in him is no darkness at all”* **(1 John 1:5)**. So no one needs to fear being lost in a featureless oneness of pure white light.

>
**Editor’s note.** Thomas referred to C. S. Lewis’s novel *That Hideous Strength* (1945). He also wondered whether the English word *hell* might come from the Hebrew *El*, “God,” negated. It does not. *Hell* comes from the Old English *hel*, from a Germanic root meaning a covered or hidden place. The Hebrew Bible’s word for the realm of the dead is *Sheol*. His theological point does not depend on the etymology.


### Can Satan be saved?


Therefore, Thomas said, if Satan is the head of the “I am not” realm, the personification of evil itself, he cannot be saved. Satan, or whatever the head of that realm is called, exists because God exists. He cannot fail to exist, because he is the sum of all that God has rejected as not Himself. God cannot save it and still have a universe; existence depends on it. God continues to hold it as not who He is. His call to us is to turn away from the wild side and walk on the side of godliness, goodness, cleansing and forgiveness. (If Satan is instead understood only as a created angel who fell, the question is the one Origen and Aquinas debated; see the note below.)


Isak put it in one phrase: evil is “a structural, foundational piece.” Thomas agreed and offered an image. Saving Satan would be like wanting the house without the beams and the siding, because there would be more room without them. Isak extended the image. We live in the space inside a house, but that space exists only because walls separate it from the outside. Darkness, in the same way, exists to show the light.


Isak added that the question itself shows a good heart: nobody worries about how the bad guy feels. “No one knows what it’s like to be the bad man,” Thomas replied.

>
**Editor’s note.** The question has a long history. Origen (third century) speculated that all rational beings, the devil included, might finally be restored. The Second Council of Constantinople (553) condemned that teaching. Thomas Aquinas held that an angel’s choice is made once, with full knowledge, and so the fallen angels’ wills stay fixed in evil (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 64, a. 2). Thomas’s answer reaches the same conclusion by a different route: the fixity comes from the structure of existence, not from the nature of angelic knowledge.


### Isak’s reading: Eden, choice, and the evil of the attractive


Isak offered his own reading of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. He takes it as a metaphor. The fruit itself did not matter, and it did not chemically make Adam and Eve wiser. What mattered was that a choice was set before them, and they crossed the line God had drawn. The fruit was good to eat: *“And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat”* **(Genesis 3:6)**. But in eating it they learned that one can act against God, that it can feel good, and that afterward one is ashamed in His presence: *“I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself”* **(Genesis 3:10)**. So Isak sees good as what God has commanded. He sees evil as the options that look and taste good, and that can consume a life dedicated to them. The choice is not evil itself; it is the freedom to choose either.


He then asked how Thomas’s account fits. Is evil still inside the body of God, as stomach acid is inside ours and has a function? Or is it simply what God has declared He is not? If so, God also has a choice. He chose one way and not the other, and everything off that way is what He deemed evil. Isak pictured God as the trailblazer who saw the patterns of life first and marked the path, and human beings almost as His exploration of it.


Thomas agreed that the choice was made before us. God made a template: this is My way, this goes on this side, that goes on the other. The revealed word says, in effect, “Choose My way.”


Isak drew a further conclusion. Other gods can be real, as reflections of other parts of the whole. A man can dedicate his life to an idol, a person or a way of life. Beneath any of these he may in fact be worshiping the darkness, and that darkness is real. It is part of God’s world, “but you’re just in the trash compactor.” Evil, then, is rejected by God and yet exists within the primal existence. Thomas confirmed this. There is the whole, and there is the God who has said of Himself, *this is who I am, and I will never be other than this.* He is the God of whom James writes: *“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning”* **(James 1:17)**. Scripture says the same of Christ: *“Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever”* **(Hebrews 13:8)**.


Isak said this helped him reconcile something he had struggled with: how evil can exist while being in God. Unlike Satan, he noted, a human being can be redeemed. But redemption requires a change of direction, at least inward. *“Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out”* **(Acts 3:19)**. The thief on the cross had no lifetime left, yet he turned: *“Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom. And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise”* **(Luke 23:42–43)**. One cannot keep walking the same road and call it good because God is now along. Having God means turning to follow Him, not bringing Him wherever you were already going.


### Monotheism, the Trinity, and Islam


Isak raised the objection he hears most from Muslims: they are monotheists, and Christians cannot be, because Christians worship at least three gods. For anyone hoping to reach Muslims, he said, this has to be answered.


Thomas said the answer is the one they had just worked out. If “one God” means the bare whole, the primal existence, then that “one” contains both good and evil. But within the whole, God has separated what is wholly good from what is wholly evil: what He is from what He is not. To worship the bare “one” would be to worship a god that includes both. That is precisely the god Christians reject. The God they worship is wholly good and does no evil “on the weekends.” So monotheism, held as a formula without content, can itself become an idol.


Thomas added that the Muslim conception of God’s oneness is rarely spelled out. He recalled his years as a driver and host for visiting Sikh dignitaries in the 3HO community. They would often say “One God” almost as a slogan. Yet none could have said what “God” meant, what evil is, or where creation’s material came from. So the right reply to the charge is a set of questions. Tell us about your one God. Does He live in a place that is not Him? Is He everything? If so, does He include evil, and if not, how is evil separated from Him? Thomas said philosophy and theology alike answer these questions poorly. Later, with Joelle, he noted that Judaism raises the same objection to the Trinity, and that Mormonism’s position on one God or many is hard to pin down. The question is central to all of them.


Thomas’s answer, then, is yes: there is one God. But “one” must be used carefully. The primal existence is the whole, including everything God has adopted as good and everything He has rejected as evil. Thomas does not call that whole God, and no one worships it. The one God is the God of the Bible: the One who said “I am,” who is wholly good, and in whom there is no darkness. Moses taught it: *“Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD”* **(Deuteronomy 6:4)**.


Within that one God are three persons: not three gods, and not three aspects or masks of one person. They are the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Paul holds the oneness and the persons together in a single sentence: *“But to us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we in him; and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him”* **(1 Corinthians 8:6)**. Jesus sent His disciples to baptize in one name, shared by three: *“in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost”* **(Matthew 28:19)**. The three persons differ in role:


- **The Father** is the head.

- **The Son** was given the task of creating the universe and all that was created: *“All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made”* **(John 1:3; see also Colossians 1:16)**. He is worshiped with the Father: *“That all men should honour the Son, even as they honour the Father”* **(John 5:23)**.

- **The Holy Spirit** was given as the personal Comforter and Counselor after Jesus, having lived as a man, returned to the Father: *“And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever”* **(John 14:16; see also John 16:7)**.


For a Muslim or Jewish listener, then, the historic Christian answer is that the Trinity is one God, one divine essence, in three persons. Father, Son and Spirit are not the good and evil halves of a larger whole. Thomas’s argument addresses a different question: whether God’s oneness includes evil. It does not. Evil must exist as the opposite of all that God is, but God has rejected it as any part of His character. There is no second God. Thomas offers his account of “I am” and “I am not” as a postulate: a reasoned derivation of how the one good God may have arisen from the primal existence, not a description of a second god.


The first commandment points the same way: *“Thou shalt have no other gods before me”* **(Exodus 20:3)**. Thomas reads the commandment as evidence that other gods exist as real rivals for worship. Refusing them is the first and foundational commandment, the prerequisite on which all the others depend. Paul supports that reading: *“But I say, that the things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to devils, and not to God: and I would not that ye should have fellowship with devils”* **(1 Corinthians 10:20)**. Thomas takes the commandment to refer above all to the “I am not” god, which competes for mankind’s worship, service and loyalty.


### Why a philosophy site is needed


The conversation convinced Thomas that a philosophy site is needed, which he called *Dr. Philosopher*. Philosophy, as he describes it, is love of knowledge and understanding, not religion as such. But he sees philosophy as one of Satan’s main tools for confusing people, as Paul warned: *“Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ”* **(Colossians 2:8)**. It asks: What about the Jews in the attic? What if this is the best of all possible worlds? Can an all-powerful God make an immovable object, then move it? He wants each of these handled at the level of pure logic, so that he can say that Kant, Hegel and Berkeley have been answered. The goal is a full toolkit showing why it is reasonable to believe that Jesus is Lord. Isak liked the idea of laying out each philosopher’s argument in turn, followed by the response.

>
**Editor’s note.** Thomas’s reference to “the best of all possible worlds” is to Gottfried Leibniz’s thesis, made famous by Voltaire’s satire in *Candide*.


### Thomas’s ministry and the “whatabouts”


Thomas then answered Susan’s second question: could he not simply show people God’s goodness? He said that ministry is good and must be done, but it is personal. Each person must witness to his neighbor and minister healing and direction in God’s way, one life at a time. There are too many situations for one man to cover them all. Two of his sites attempt it. [mycounselor.com](https://www.mycounselor.com) offers advice on how to live life and solve its problems. [renaissance-ministries.com](https://www.renaissance-ministries.com) explains in finer detail how God applies to the different areas of life, and how Scripture is consistent with itself. Thomas sees his own primary message as connecting every part of life, its large problems and its small ones, to the oneness of God.


The gentler story is one almost anyone can tell: God’s goodness, how He has changed one’s life, and counsel on what to do and not do, believe and disbelieve. But in Thomas’s experience, it takes a lifetime to persuade anyone that the Bible is true and to live a godly life by its precepts. Every testimony and explanation meets a “yes, but.” That, he said, is the right response for someone trying to understand everything, to fit all of life’s experience into one connected chain of cause and effect. Anyone who thinks deeply about life sees that parts of the story are not told in the Bible. People know there is more to the story, and they do not know what it is, so they remain undecided and uncommitted. Many cannot accept the simple version.


He said he knows this because that was him. Faced with the mystery of life, he took part in or studied some forty religions, philosophies and teachings. He used psychedelics as a vision quest to see into the spirit world. He developed Conscious Point Physics to explain the interface between God and the physical universe. He found what he was looking for. He doubts that most people who followed the same path would have done so; most, he thinks, would have ended up under the Burnside Bridge. He credits a miracle of grace. He compared himself to the persistent widow in Jesus’ parable, whom the judge finally answered just to be rid of her **(Luke 18:1–8)**. Jesus told it to teach *“that men ought always to pray, and not to faint”* **(Luke 18:1)**. God answers that kind of desire to know, he said: *“Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you”* **(Matthew 7:7)**. Isak has that desire too.


Even after all that study, the connection between God and the physical world is not fully explained. It is explained well enough to convince Thomas that the Bible points in the direction of truth, and the gap must be filled with faith. *“For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known”* **(1 Corinthians 13:12)**. People need different degrees of certainty. Thomas was a Doubting Thomas, and like his namesake he needed evidence. Eventually the evidence was sufficient. Christ’s words to the first Thomas apply: *“be not faithless, but believing”* **(John 20:27)**, and *“blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed”* **(John 20:29)**. Each person must decide when he is willing to say, “That is sufficient.” Most people do not need such extreme analysis to be convinced that the Bible agrees with their own experience and with the evidence of experiment, but Thomas did. He recommends that a person keep studying, knowing there is no end to the mysteries of God. The deepest of them is His existence, the subject of this essay.


What Thomas had been waiting for, he said, was assurance that he would be well enough prepared to answer the objections raised. Now, when someone throws a “whatabout” at him, he trusts that he will find the answer in time, because every earlier objection was eventually answered. That is the readiness Peter asks of every believer: *“be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear”* **(1 Peter 3:15)**. The answers gathered so far will serve people whose questions go that deep. For others there may have to be fresh manna **(Exodus 16:4)**: new revelation, needed to answer their questions.


Isak spoke of his own path. He knows a good deal of the Bible and its concepts. He finds it hard to speak with full conviction because he is still sorting out small parts. Some days he is sure he has the whole picture; the next day he sees that it was close but not quite right. He is reading Scripture more for wisdom, not only for ideas. He described a lifelong wish to be wise and to have answers. Once that wish pushed him away from faith, because he could not learn everything. Now it pushes him to use the capacity he has. His personal goal is to teach and to counsel. He credits much of his growth to Thomas and this project. Getting past the generalizations about Christ and the church, past people’s hurts, to the question “What is God?” has helped him most. It points him toward a higher power he wants to align with, even if he never fully knows it.
