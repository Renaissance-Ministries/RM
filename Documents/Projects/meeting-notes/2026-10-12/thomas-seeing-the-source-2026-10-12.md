## 1. The Problem of Categorizing a Body of Work

Isak shared a working interface that sorted the president-site articles several ways: by intention (eight of them — explain, defend, teach, review, record, exhort, frame, Bible-verse analysis), by date, by keyword (CPP appeared in 13 articles, AI in 7, consciousness and faith in 5 each, superposition in 4), by subject, and by length. The subject list drew on the categories Thomas had assigned in each post's front matter.

Thomas said those categories are close to useless: nearly everything he had tagged as a "fellowship essay," because he found it very difficult to call anything else. That difficulty was one of his reasons for wanting a mind-map approach in the first place. He suggested ignoring his categories, or using them only as hints, and letting Claude find a better scheme.

Isak proposed cross-categorization: keep "fellowship essay" as a format label but also file each essay under every subject it touches (physics, life, apologetics), so that a reader browsing a subject sees the fellowship essays that bear on it. He noted the deeper difficulty: physics, for instance, is arguably present in every category, because the same patterns and movements show up everywhere in life. No single category is ever the category.

## 2. Why Mapping Knowledge Is Hard

Thomas observed that mapping a whole body of knowledge is rarely done in practice, and he thinks the reason is simply that it is too hard. Partial versions exist — the cell-metabolism diagram with arrows showing what flows where — and they help because they show a flow of reality.

He has always sensed that life is connected in sequential, branching ways. A recent example: an article he had Claude write about a man who read 500 self-help books and wrote a book about them. Reading it, Thomas saw it as a flowchart — a few top-level ideas, each with its parts underneath — and thought how useful an overview in that form would be. This is the original intent behind the name Hyperphysics: everything is related to everything, and the work is to discover how one thing maps to another.

So he suggested a smaller target: rather than mapping every topic across all the writing, turn each essay into its own mind map — a central idea with branches — as learning-technique practitioners recommend. But he noted the catch: to take notes as a mind map, you almost have to understand the material beforehand.

Isak agreed and applied it to CPP: you have to recognize which parts are building blocks and what the true sequence is. For teaching, does one start with origins? Build upward from the quarks and the conscious points (eCPs, qCPs)? Work downward? Or present several levels side by side and show that they behave alike because one fundamental principle drives them all — as in linking gravity and conscious points within a single article?

## 3. Isak's Backyard Reflection: Seeing Toward a Bush

Isak described an experience from the day before. Sitting in his backyard, he watched light pass through hanging glass light-catchers. The light was redistributed in many directions, yet it did not seem to lose its power; unlike a body that blocks light, the glass simply relocates it, much as a mirror redirects it.

He then looked at a bush in the corner of the yard and broke down what was happening. The light reaching his eyes is sunlight; the bush is what redirects it to him. The bush absorbs some of it, so it is not a perfect mirror like glass or metal. A person standing next to him also sees the bush, but receives different photons at a slightly different angle — same source, same object, different photons.

This brought him to the double-slit experiment: a photon passing through is bent and guided by something along the way; a long stream of photons shows up as a wave pattern, a single one as a single point.

Abstracting further: people all over the Earth are lit by one unifying source, its light spread and bounced into countless places. The rules are consistent — our eyes have similar receptors, and we seem to see the same things — yet each of us sees from a different angle depending on where we stand.

From there he moved to the spiritual parallel. Suppose there is a Source sending out not only photons but something arriving from every direction at once and leaving in every direction at once. In CPP terms, that constant motion still allows clusters of particles to "decide" to behave together, forming DNA, following its rules, and repeating them through death and new birth. Like the sun, this Source is taken for granted precisely because it lights everything else; it is effectively invisible until we trust that it is there. We are receiving waves and particles of information constantly, without having asked for them.

He posed the question this raises: is faith a matter of training the eyes to see certain things, or of trusting that we move through a world full of what we cannot see? Or is what we can see already enough to justify belief in God — a deeper resonance beneath what we observe, with a consistent ebb and flow? His conclusion: "I'm not looking at a bush. I'm looking toward a bush. The only reason I'm seeing it is because something else is showing it to me." And he asked whether we can read all of life that way — as we already do spiritually.

*Note (Claude): On the physics of the two observers: each person receives distinct photons, never the same ones, as Isak said. Glass prisms and light-catchers refract and partly reflect light; a small fraction is still absorbed or scattered, so the redistribution is very efficient but not lossless. This does not change the point of the reflection.*

## 4. Thomas's Response: Every Vector Ends at God

Thomas characterized Isak's reflection as a reductionist track: pick any vector, follow it down — the bush, to photons, to conscious points, to God — and you arrive at God. The question is whether that tracing works in other directions too, and his answer is yes. His whole project, he said, has been showing that you can find God everywhere you look. That one can go in any direction and find God at the end of it is itself an indication that God underlies everything — that God exists.

Having established that, he said, the conclusion is in a sense trivial — hidden, but elementary. The next step is to ask what more useful work can be done with the knowledge that God is in everything.

## 5. Sensation Implies an Observer

Thomas turned to another feature of life: it has sensation — hot and cold, red and blue, pain and pleasure. These can also be traced back. Each sensation produces a particular pattern of brain activation, felt in particular parts of the body, and different sense organs correspond to different conscious-point configurations.

But to perceive a configuration there must be a perceiver. So the observer — the "I" — has a conscious-point nature of its own. He was careful on this point: he does not know whether the observer is a conscious point, but there is certainly a conscious-point nature associated with our perception of pain, heat, color, and the rest. Since every sensory pattern produces a particular neural pattern, we can say there is an observer who looks at those arising patterns — the "I" that observes them all.

## 6. The Conscious Entity

He described a second, more gestalt way to see the same thing. Each pattern — conscious points configured with nerves, configured in turn with more conscious points — has an identity of its own. When a sufficient critical mass of consciousness is oriented in a certain way, it forms its own pattern and becomes its own conscious entity.

This, he said, is where his concept of the conscious entity came from: the idea can be applied to anything. There is a beingness associated with any concentration of conscious points together with the diffuse space around it. Space plus the entity together give distinction; that distinction is made of particular conscious points; it has some degree of awareness; and it stands in relationship to other things.

## 7. Identity Depends on the Frame the Observer Brings

He illustrated with a rock. A rock emerges in a canyon, from under a glacier, or out of a volcano. In itself it is just an "it," with no particular character — until someone comes along and says, "This will make a great doorstop." Now it has the identity of a doorstop; it is no longer just a rock.

Another person picks up the same collection of conscious points and says, "This has a fossil in it. It needs to be cut — it's a lapidary project, it belongs in a museum, and you'll be famous." Same object, different identity, because the two people brought different frames to it.

Thomas concluded that identity is a very important principle, and the simplest account of how it arises is this: a seer sees a scene and says, "In relationship to me, this is a doorstop." Identity depends a great deal on what we bring. If we bring nothing, we likely do not notice the thing at all — we trip over it as a stumbling stone and forget it, because there are so many and we have no relationship with them.

He tied this back to the starting problem: all of this is about how to categorize something. (The discussion broke off here as Joelle joined the meeting.)
