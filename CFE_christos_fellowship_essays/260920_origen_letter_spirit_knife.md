# The Letter, the Spirit, and the Knife: Weighing the Case Against Origen

Fellowship Discussion Essay | 20 September 2026

> Who also hath made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life. — 2 Corinthians 3:6

**Occasion.** On September 17, Scott Lively published an essay arguing that Origen of Alexandria, the third-century teacher most associated with the allegorical reading of Scripture, cannot be trusted on doctrine, because as a young man he castrated himself in obedience to a literal reading of Matthew 19:12 and never afterward renounced the way of reading that Mr. Lively believes produced the act. From that one wound he traces a line through Augustine to Rome, to much of Protestant teaching about the last things, and finally to the modern reinterpretations of the Bible's teaching on sex. The essay raises a question every Christian has to answer whether or not he has heard of Origen: how does a reader know when the Bible is speaking plainly and when it is speaking in figure, and who gets to say? That question is worth a Sunday. This essay summarizes the argument, grants what is sound in it, tests what is not, and then offers a set of working rules for the fellowship to argue over.

---

## I. The Argument in Brief

Mr. Lively's case runs in five steps.

First, the history. The church historian Eusebius records that the young Origen read the Lord's saying about men who have "made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake" as an instruction and carried it out on his own body. Mr. Lively treats this as reliable, and he points to the first canon of the Council of Nicaea (A.D. 325), which removed from the clergy any healthy man who had castrated himself, as evidence that the practice was real and known.

Second, the later Origen. In his commentary on Matthew, written late in life, Origen rejected the literal reading of that verse and condemned the act. Mr. Lively holds that this was not true repentance, because Origen kept and expanded the interpretive system: Scripture has a bodily, a soulish, and a spiritual sense, the literal is the lowest, and the spiritual is the highest and is often hidden. Mr. Lively says that scheme came from Plato and not from Moses.

Third, the diagnosis. Origen lacked the grounding in the Old Testament that would have told him when Jesus was speaking in hyperbole. He treated the Sermon on the Mount's standard as something to be reached by willpower, which Mr. Lively calls Pelagianism before Pelagius. The youthful literalism was the error; the later allegory was the escape hatch that let him avoid naming its cause.

Fourth, the pipeline. Origen taught the Church that the concrete, Hebrew sense of Scripture is optional. Augustine inherited the habit, spiritualized the thousand-year reign of Revelation 20, and recast the place of the Jews. Rome made it an institution, and the Reformers kept more of it than they knew.

Fifth, the present. The same move that turns a plain text into a "higher" meaning is, he says, what modern revisionists do with Romans 1, Leviticus 18, and Genesis 1:27. The remedy is what he calls the Hebraic reading: the New Testament read through the Old as written, with metaphor and hyperbole recognized for what they are, and with the spiritual sense understood as the sense the letter was written to carry.

---

## II. The Record

The story is ancient and the source is friendly. Eusebius was the pupil of Pamphilus, who was Origen's devoted defender, and the sixth book of his Ecclesiastical History is largely a tribute to Origen. An admirer has no motive to invent such a story, and Eusebius tells it with evident discomfort. That is a fair point in Mr. Lively's favor, and many historians accept the account.

Three cautions belong beside it. Eusebius was born a few years after Origen died and writes from the tradition of Origen's circle, not as a witness. Epiphanius, writing in the following century, reports more than one version of how Origen was supposed to have achieved his continence, which shows the matter was already rumor-laden in antiquity. And the Nicene canon cannot be pinned on Origen. Justin Martyr, writing in Alexandria's orbit a generation before Origen was born, tells of a young Christian who petitioned the Roman governor for permission to be made a eunuch. The practice was in the air among zealous ascetics; the canon addresses a pattern, not one famous man.

---

## III. What the Article Gets Right

A good deal.

The reading of Matthew 19 is sound. The disciples have just heard the Lord restore marriage to its creation pattern, and they answer that on those terms "it is not good to marry." Jesus replies, "All men cannot receive this saying, save they to whom it is given," and then names three kinds of eunuchs. The third is the man who gives up marriage for the sake of the kingdom's work, as Paul did. It is a calling received, not a surgery performed.

The reading of Matthew 5 is also sound. "If thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out" is hyperbole, and the proof is in the sentence itself: a man with one eye lusts as well as a man with two.

The central principle is sound: the spiritual sense of a passage stands on its letter and does not replace it. "That Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4), and yet a real rock gave real water to real people.

And the criticism of Origen has a real target. In On First Principles, Origen says plainly that some passages have no true literal sense at all. His test for which passages those are is whether the letter seems impossible or unworthy of God. That test is the weak joint. "Unworthy of God" by whose measure?

---

## IV. Which Method Held the Knife?

The act was committed by the literal reading. The allegorical reading was the correction. If the wound is an exhibit against a method, it is an exhibit against taking a figure of speech as a command, which is the opposite of allegory.

A reader steeped in the Law would have had Deuteronomy 23:1 before him and would have known that the God who gave that statute was not now commanding the thing it describes. Scripture would have checked the reading of Scripture.

And the logic cuts both ways. If a bad act discredits the method that led to it, then the knife discredits literalism. A method of reading is tested by one question: does it recover what the Author meant? The wound does not answer that question either way.

---

## V. The Teacher and the Teaching

The title of the article makes a further claim: that the man, having done this, cannot be trusted. This is the form of argument called ad hominem. Scripture does not reason that way. Moses killed a man. David took a wife and arranged a death. Peter denied the Lord. Paul held the coats at Stephen's stoning.

The Lord Himself separated the two questions: "All therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works" (Matthew 23:3).

What the record shows is a man who did a rash thing at about eighteen, said in later life that those who do it act without understanding, labored for decades over the text of Scripture, and at the end was imprisoned and tortured under the emperor Decius without denying Christ, dying not long after from what he suffered.

---

## VI. Allegory Before Origen

Origen was the most systematic Christian practitioner of allegorical interpretation, but he did not begin it. Philo the Jew had read Moses allegorically two centuries earlier. More to the point, the practice is in the New Testament itself.

Paul writes of Sarah and Hagar, "Which things are an allegory" (Galatians 4:24). Hebrews reads the tabernacle as "the example and shadow of heavenly things." The Lord made Jonah's three days and the serpent on the pole into signs of Himself.

The useful distinction: **Typology** reads a real person, event, or institution as a God-designed pattern of something greater to come, with the original history left fully standing. **Allegory**, in the sense that deserves criticism, treats the narrative as a code whose surface may be discarded once the hidden meaning is extracted. The apostles practice the first.

---

## VII. The Pipeline Examined

Does the line run from Origen through Augustine to Rome and into the Reformation? In part it does. Augustine tells in the Confessions that what freed him to believe the Old Testament was the preaching of Ambrose of Milan, who expounded it spiritually, and Ambrose had learned that manner from Origen and Philo.

Three facts complicate the picture:

1. The disagreement over the millennium is older than Origen's influence. Justin Martyr, around A.D. 160, held firmly to an earthly thousand-year reign yet told his Jewish conversation partner that many Christians of pure and godly faith thought otherwise. Origen was not yet born.

2. Rome did not enthrone Origen. Jerome turned against him. Augustine rejected his best-known speculation. The Second Council of Constantinople in 553 listed him by name among the condemned.

3. The Reformers rejected Origen's allegorizing in the strongest language yet both remained amillennial. Men who threw out Origen's method with both hands still did not arrive at Mr. Lively's eschatology.

---

## VIII. The Modern Application

Those who reinterpret Romans 1 and Leviticus 18 do not, as a rule, allegorize. They argue history and vocabulary: that Paul had only exploitative practices or idol-temple rites in view, that a Greek word has been mistranslated. In form, these are appeals to the literal and historical sense. They must therefore be answered on that ground.

What this shows is that the grammatical-historical method can be bent just as the allegorical method can. The wresting Peter describes (2 Peter 3:16) is done by the reader's will, and the method is only the instrument.

The Lord states the real safeguard: "If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God" (John 7:17).

---

## IX. Is the Standard Impossible?

It is true that the flesh cannot keep the Sermon. It is not true that the Sermon is merely a mirror held up to make us give up. It ends with a man who "heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them."

Origen's mistake was not that he took the Lord's demand for purity seriously. His mistake was the location and the instrument. The apostolic reading is given by Paul: "Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection" (Colossians 3:5). The "members" to be put to death are the sins, and the power is the Spirit (Romans 8:13).

---

## X. Working Rules for Rightly Dividing

1. **Begin with the letter.** Ask first what the words meant, in their grammar and setting, to those who first heard them.
2. **Let Scripture check the reading of Scripture.** A reading of one verse that requires what another verse forbids is a wrong reading.
3. **Build the spiritual sense on the history, never in place of it.** The rock was Christ, and the rock was also a rock.
4. **Read each kind of writing by its own signals.** Narrative presents itself as narrative, law as law, and proverb as proverb.
5. **Let the apostles show how it is done.** Where the New Testament interprets a passage, that interpretation governs ours.
6. **Read in company.** Origen acted young, alone, and in secret. "In the multitude of counsellors there is safety" (Proverbs 11:14).
7. **Come willing to obey.** John 7:17 stands over all the rest.

---

## XI. Conclusion

Mr. Lively's warning has a sound core. The letter of Scripture is God's own control on every higher reading, and a rule of interpretation that lets the reader drop the history whenever it offends the age will in the end let him drop the Bible altogether.

But the argument from the wound does not carry the weight placed on it. The knife was in the hand of the literal reading. The apostles themselves read Scripture spiritually. Rome condemned Origen as well as borrowing from him, and the Reformers who rejected him most loudly did not reach the conclusions the article says must follow.

Neither a lower view of the spiritual sense nor a higher view of the literal will fix that by itself. The remedy is the whole counsel of God, read in the fellowship of the saints, by men who intend to do what they find there.

---

## Discussion Questions

1. If a bad act committed under a way of reading Scripture discredits that way of reading, which way does Origen's act discredit? Is there any sound version of the argument from the wound?
2. Matthew 23:3 separates what the scribes teach from what they do. When, if ever, does a teacher's conduct rightly count against his teaching?
3. Paul writes, "Which things are an allegory" (Galatians 4:24). How is what Paul does with Sarah and Hagar different from what Origen claimed the right to do?
4. Origen's test for setting aside the letter was that it seemed "impossible" or "unworthy of God." What passages does our own age find unworthy of God?
5. Luther and Calvin rejected allegory and yet did not expect an earthly millennium. What does that tell us?
6. Section VIII argues that modern revisionist readings use historical arguments, not allegorical ones. If that is right, what is the most effective answer to them?
7. Is the standard of the Sermon on the Mount meant to be kept?
8. Origen acted alone and in secret. Has anyone here acted on a private reading of Scripture that the counsel of other believers would have corrected?
