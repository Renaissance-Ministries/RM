# 261115 - Recap - God Defines Good and Evil

# The Author and the Raindrop:


# God - Good and Evil - A World for His Pleasure

15 November 2026 · Thomas Lee Abshier, ND

At the Christos Fellowship meeting of 15 November 2026, Michael and Thomas spent the better part of an hour on the oldest questions in theology: why God made the world, whether we are free, where evil comes from, and whether goodness can exist without God. Michael came as a self-described learner of the rules of an unfamiliar game, testing each premise for weakness; Thomas defended a picture in which God creates in order to experience Himself through His creatures, defines good and evil, and grants real freedom so that He can be loved.

This essay sets the conversation aside as a conversation and examines the arguments themselves, adding commentary on each. Its conclusion, stated here at the outset, is that Thomas's central claims hold: without a God who defines the good, morality reduces to preference, and love requires both freedom and a standard. Several of Michael's objections, however, land on real pressure points that Thomas's account answers only in part, and those are named plainly below.

## 1. Was the world made for God or for us?

Michael's opening reading was blunt. If the world was made for God and not for us, then God is an author who wanted a story with villains so He could punish them, and whatever happens is His preferred script. He likened this to the Muslim resignation, "It is the will of Allah," and pointed to conquest, colonization, and religious war as scenes the Author apparently chose.

Thomas accepted the criticism of his own phrasing and supplied the missing qualification: God created the world to experience Himself, so what God experiences is our experience. That is a significant correction, not a retreat. It turns Michael's picture of a detached author watching his characters suffer into a picture of an author who lives inside every character. If the Native Americans are slaughtered, God is not enjoying a story about their slaughter; He is undergoing it from inside them. On this view the Great Commandment is not an arbitrary demand but the operating rule of a shared life: love God, because you are dealing with the One who feels your choices, and love your neighbor, because God is also living your neighbor's life.

**Commentary.** The correction removes the sharpest edge of Michael's critique. A God who experiences creation from within every creature cannot be indifferent to cruelty, because cruelty is done to Him. Christians will recognize a version of this in Jesus' words, *"Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me"* **(Matthew 25:40)**. Thomas's account takes that identification further than most traditional theology does, but it is consistent with the text's direction.

The premise does leave one tension that should be faced rather than avoided. If God experiences every side, He also experiences the satisfactions of the wrongdoer. Thomas answered this directly: God does not enjoy everything people enjoy. Adultery may please the adulterer, but it is not what God enjoys, because His experience is not merely the sum of His creatures' pleasures; it is filtered through what He has defined as good. That answer works, but it means God's experience of creation is not simply our experience. It is our experience as judged by His own character. The premise is best stated with that qualification attached: God lives our lives from within, and He feels them as He is, not as we are.

## 2. Free will, or a scripted play?

Michael's strongest philosophical move came early. Granting that God made free will, he said, God still sets its exact dosage: enough freedom that some will refuse Him, so He has someone to punish. On that view freedom is just another stage direction, and the play remains scripted. Thomas called this the key point and conceded its force conditionally. If everything simply runs out from God's setup, there is no free will, the drama means nothing, and it is God doing everything. His account requires that freedom be real, and he affirmed that it is.

**Commentary.** Thomas was right to treat this as the crux, and right not to soften the conditional. Determinism, whether theological or physical, empties moral language of meaning. Praise, blame, love, and repentance all presuppose that a person could have done otherwise. If he could not, the words name nothing.

But affirming freedom is not the same as explaining how a sovereign Creator can make it. Michael's objection has a sharper form that the conversation did not reach: an omniscient God who creates a world knowing how each person will choose seems to have chosen the outcome by choosing the world. Philosophers have offered several answers. One is that God's knowledge of a choice does not cause the choice, any more than knowing that the sun rose this morning caused it to rise. Another is that God, being outside time, sees choices as they are made rather than predicting them in advance. A third, closer to Thomas's framework, is that God has deliberately limited Himself, veiling His own knowledge and power in the layers of creation so that genuine others can exist. Thomas's later remark that God "implemented the system by layering and disguising" points toward this third answer, and it would strengthen his case to connect the two explicitly: the disguise is what makes the freedom real.

Michael's second jab, that God sets up freedom so He can have His revenge, mistakes the purpose. In Thomas's account freedom exists so God can be loved, and love that cannot refuse is not love. Hell is then not revenge but the consequence of a refusal, which leads to the next question: where the thing refused came from.

## 3. Where evil comes from

Michael pressed the obvious inference: if God made everything, He made the things He dislikes. Of the two couples who agree to swap partners, he said, God must have wanted it, or He would not have made it. Thomas answered with a structured account. The Father divided all possibilities into what He loves and what He rejects; that definition is prior to creation. The Son then made a world of potentialities, including the potential to choose against the Father's definition. Nothing evil was made. Evil appears only when a conscious creature chooses what God has said not to do. Animals cannot do evil; a snail darter kills because it was built to. In Eden, humans first knew only good; once they gained the knowledge of good and evil, they saw that the world allowed theft, adultery, and murder, and they could choose them.

**Commentary.** This is the strongest part of Thomas's case, and it has deep roots. Augustine argued that evil is not a substance God created but a privation, a turning away from the good by a will that was made good. Thomas's "potentialities" make the same move in a different vocabulary. It answers Michael's inference directly: God made the possibility of the foursome, because He made beings free enough to love, and the possibility of refusal comes with that freedom. To make the possibility is not to want the act. A parent who teaches a child to drive makes a car crash possible without wanting one.

The account invites a question that the conversation did not reach, and that Thomas's critics will raise: is something good because God loves it, or does God love it because it is good? Plato posed this dilemma in the *Euthyphro*. If goodness is whatever God happens to choose, it looks arbitrary; God could have declared cruelty good. If God loves the good because it is good, the good seems to stand above God. Thomas's phrasing (God "divided" possibilities into what He likes and dislikes) sounds like the first horn. The classical resolution, and the one that fits Thomas's framework best, is that the good is neither arbitrary nor external; it is God's own nature. God did not pick love as a preference. *"God is love"* **(1 John 4:8)**, and the definition of good and evil is that nature expressed. Stating it this way would close a door that a skeptic like Michael will otherwise walk through.

Two smaller points deserve comment. First, Thomas's axiom that God "popped" into self-awareness out of nothing is unnecessary and costs him something. He noted that an eternal God is just as mysterious, which is true, but the two mysteries are not equal. Something arising from nothing invites the question of what caused it; a being that exists necessarily and without beginning does not. Scripture's own starting point is the second: *"from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God"* **(Psalm 90:2)**. Second, Thomas's description of hell as the place people chose, where they get the life they wanted but without God, is both scripturally defensible and persuasive to modern hearers. C. S. Lewis made the same argument in *The Problem of Pain*, where he wrote that the doors of hell are locked on the inside, and again in *The Great Divorce*, where those in hell are shown to have chosen it. It also answers Michael's charge of divine revenge. A God who gives people what they insist on is not taking vengeance.

## 4. Can there be order, and goodness, without God?

Michael asked why God should be added to the universe at all. The universe might simply have always been here; God looks like an unnecessary middleman. Thomas replied that a universe without a defining mind must produce all its order by accident, making randomness its god, and that such a world has no moral standard except each person's preference. He invoked Aleister Crowley's "Do what thou wilt" as that world's honest motto, with his Kabbalah teacher's addition: take what you want, and pay for it.

Michael's rejoinder was his statistics professor's coin-flip lesson. Real randomness contains runs and clusters; a list without them is a fake. Random processes therefore produce structure. Thomas agreed and said he had acknowledged self-organization from chaos already. His claim was not that a godless world cannot produce order, but that it cannot produce an obligation.

**Commentary.** On the narrow question, Michael was right, and Thomas was right to concede it. Randomness does generate pattern, and physics and biology are full of order that arises from simple rules without design. A theist gains nothing by denying this. Whether undirected processes are sufficient to produce the particular order we observe (life, consciousness, the fine-tuning of physical constants) is a separate and much harder question, and the coin-flip lesson does not touch it. A run of six heads is expected in 100 tosses; a working cell is not.

On the moral question, Thomas's point is decisive, and it is worth stating precisely. A description of how things are does not by itself yield a conclusion about how they ought to be. David Hume made this observation in the eighteenth century, and no naturalist has since closed the gap. Patterns from chaos, however rich, are still facts. If nothing stands above the facts, then "good" is a word for what a person or a group prefers, and Crowley is simply candid about it.

Michael's own example illustrates this. He described ten weaker tribesmen banding together to stop a bully, and called it the origin of government and of shared morality. As history, that is plausible. But it explains how people came to believe the bully was wrong; it does not show that he was wrong. Had the bully gathered eleven allies, the same process would have produced the opposite "morality." The account describes power, not justice.

## 5. Whose good? The category error

Michael's next objection was that even with God there is only personal interpretation. The German army wore *Gott mit uns* on its belt buckles, Christians have fought Christians for centuries, and cultures that never heard of the Bible have developed their own codes. He concluded that warring Christian nations killing each other for God is "not a very good good."

Thomas called this a category error. There are two domains: what God has defined as good, and what humans, individually and in groups, believe is good. Human claims do not make anything good for God; to think otherwise is to treat the claimant as God. Humans are trying to discern an absolute and may get it wrong. Both men agreed the Nazis got it wrong.

**Commentary.** Thomas's distinction is the standard one between what is true and what people believe, and it is correct. Disagreement about a question does not show that the question has no answer. Physicians disagreed for centuries about the cause of infection; germs were real throughout. The existence of false claims to God's endorsement no more disproves God's standard than counterfeit bills disprove money; counterfeiting presupposes a genuine article.

The most telling detail is in Michael's own verdict. To call the Christian wars "not a very good good" is to appeal to a standard by which both warring parties fall short. Michael was not saying that each side's good was valid for that side. He was saying that both were wrong, and that judgment requires a measure that belongs to neither. That is Thomas's "absolute perspective," admitted from the other direction.

Michael's follow-up question, borrowed from a comedy routine (who made you the sheriff?), is the honest residue. Granting that there is a true good, who has access to it, and how would anyone check? Thomas pointed to revelation: the Ten Commandments and Scripture. That answer is sound but incomplete, because revelation must be read and applied, and the warring Christians also read Scripture. The fuller answer has two parts. First, the core of the moral law is not obscure; Paul says the Gentiles *"shew the work of the law written in their hearts"* **(Romans 2:15)**, which is why Cain hid Abel's body. Second, Jesus gave a public test that does not depend on anyone's self-report: *"Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them"* **(Matthew 7:20)**. Wars waged under His name while ignoring His commands fail that test. Susan made the same point later in the meeting, when she said that taking the Lord's name in vain is claiming Him without following Him. Michael's best evidence against Christianity is, on this reading, evidence of Christians breaking the third commandment.

## 6. God as everything, yet distinct from His creatures

Michael found a contradiction: Thomas says God is everything, yet speaks of humans as separated from God by free will. Either God is not everything, or there is something beyond everything. Thomas answered that nothing is apart from God, but God hid Himself in layers. Built up from conscious points through particles, atoms, cells, and a nervous system, a person sees only what is around him. He learns good and bad from pain, pleasure, and Scripture, and is not privy to God's own perspective. It is all one, but from inside it does not look like one. When Michael concluded that God must then be the evil too, Thomas distinguished the Father, wholly good, who defined good and evil, from the Son, whom the Father created and who made a world of potentialities.

**Commentary.** The layering answer is coherent and resolves Michael's verbal puzzle. A whole can contain parts whose view is partial without the parts being outside the whole. Paul expresses the same idea: *"For in him we live, and move, and have our being"* **(Acts 17:28)**. Philosophers call this panentheism (everything is in God, while God is more than everything) to distinguish it from pantheism (everything simply is God). Thomas's position is panentheist, and saying so would help, because Michael kept hearing pantheism, and pantheism really does make God the author of evil.

One part of the answer needs care. Thomas described the Son as created by the Father and as a "subset" of Him. Historic Christian teaching, from the Gospel of John through the Nicene Creed, holds that the Son is not created: *"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God"* **(John 1:1)**, "begotten, not made." Thomas's division of labor (the Father defines, the Son makes) has support in Scripture's description of creation through the Son, but its wording places the Son below the Father in a way most Christian readers will notice and object to. The argument against Michael does not need that wording. It works as well if the Father and Son are one in nature and distinct in role.

## 7. Killing, murder, and the amount of evil

Thomas argued that there was no evil before humans: animals follow their nature, and everything nature does is good. Michael objected that venomous animals kill, and asked whether "thou shalt not kill" applies only to humans. Thomas corrected the commandment to "thou shalt not murder": murder is the knowing, unrighteous taking of a life, and justified killing, such as shooting a hostage-taker, is not murder. Michael asked why God permitted so much evil rather than a tiny amount. Thomas said God made no quantity of evil; He made a platform for choice, and the amount is what people choose. When Michael suggested that choices are conditioned by upbringing, as B. F. Skinner held, Thomas agreed that people are imprinted, by a crack house or a Mafia household for example, but said that God also gave each person a conscience. Cain knew murder was wrong and hid the body.

**Commentary.** The correction on the commandment is right; the Hebrew verb in Exodus 20:13 denotes murder, not all killing. That distinction underlies the ethics of self-defense, just war, and lawful punishment, and Michael's quip that it is "the creed of every soldier" points to a real tradition of thought, not a loophole.

Thomas's answer on the quantity of evil is, in substance, what philosophers call the free will defense, given its rigorous modern form by Alvin Plantinga: if God wants creatures who can freely love, He cannot also guarantee that they will never choose wrongly. The amount of evil is then set by the choices, not by a dial God turns. That answer is strong for moral evil, the wrong that people do.

It is weaker for suffering that no one chose. Before humans there was predation, disease, and pain over a vast span of time, by the account of the same physical science Thomas uses elsewhere. Calling all of that "good" because animals cannot sin settles whether animals are guilty; it does not settle why the world contains so much pain. Scripture itself does not call this suffering good; it says *"the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now"* **(Romans 8:22)**. A complete answer to Michael would distinguish moral evil, which Thomas explained well, from natural suffering, which remains the harder question, and which Christianity answers less by explanation than by the promise of redemption.

On conditioning, Thomas's appeal to conscience is the right move, and it can be sharpened. Upbringing affects how clearly a person sees, and Scripture accounts for that: *"For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required"* **(Luke 12:48)**. The child raised in a crack house is not judged as one raised in a church. But no one is wholly without light, which is why Cain hid the body.

## 8. Why define good and evil at all? Love requires another

Michael's persistent question was why God did not simply refrain from creating evil, or define everything as good. Thomas's answer was that God is love, and love requires three things: another to love, the freedom of that other to return or refuse love, and a standard of what does and does not satisfy. Without a standard, God would be "God with no standards," indistinguishable from a random universe. Michael offered a vivid image in reply: God cast Himself out of His own Garden of Eden when He divided reality into what He likes and does not.

**Commentary.** The argument that love needs both freedom and a standard is sound and is the best answer in the conversation. Love that cannot refuse is mechanism, and "love" that accepts anything, including cruelty toward the beloved, is indifference. A parent who has no standard for how his children treat one another does not love them more than one who does; he loves them less. Michael's Eden image is clever but mistaken for the same reason. God's standard is not a loss of innocence. It is the shape of His love, which was never indifferent to begin with.

Where the argument needs a qualification is in its picture of God's motive. Thomas said God created because He was alone and wished to leave that state, and Susan later supported this, observing that Michael would find real aloneness lonely. Leonard objected from the other side, saying God already has the fullness of joy and needs nothing from us. Historic Christian theology agrees with Leonard on this point, and does so precisely through the Trinity Thomas invokes. Jesus prays, *"for thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world"* **(John 17:24)**. Love already existed in God, between Father and Son, before anything was made. On that view God did not create to escape loneliness; He created out of love's overflow, to share what He already had.

The two pictures are not as far apart as they first look. Thomas's deeper claim, that God wants to be loved freely by creatures who could refuse, does not require that God was lonely. The love of a free creature is a distinct good that the Trinity's inner life does not contain, and it is reasonable to say God desired it. Stated that way, the argument keeps everything Thomas needs (God's desire for reciprocated love, and the necessity of freedom and a standard) without implying that God lacked something.

## 9. "God is love, God is everything, therefore everything is love"

Michael drew this syllogism from two of Thomas's statements. Thomas rejected it as the same fallacy as "Everything is God, therefore I am God," which he associated with Shirley MacLaine shouting "I am God" on a beach. He argued that reality has levels: the Father, the Son who images Him, and the creation the Son imaged. Love is a relation between persons who choose to relate well. MacLaine is a point of God's perspective, not God.

**Commentary.** Thomas is right that the inference fails, and the reason can be stated briefly. It moves from a property of the whole to a property of each part, which logicians call the fallacy of division. A symphony can be beautiful though a single dropped cymbal is not. The syllogism also shifts the meaning of "is." "God is love" describes God's character; "God is everything" (in Thomas's panentheist sense) describes everything's dependence on God. Joining them as if both were simple identities yields nonsense in either direction: everything is love, or I am God.

The point matters beyond this exchange because the MacLaine move is common in popular spirituality, and it flattens exactly the distinction that makes love possible. If I am God, there is no other; if there is no other, there is no love. Thomas's levels are the condition for the relationship he was describing in the previous section.

## 10. Who am I? Michael's love of being, and the raindrop

Michael's last contribution was the most personal. He could not locate the boundary of "I." Not the mind alone, which leaves out the body; not the body alone, which dies without the air it constantly exchanges. Drawn honestly, the boundary keeps widening until it includes everything, so God too should be called simply the whole. He added that his greatest love needs no other and no will: it is love of being itself, of being alive and part of it all. Asked once by a child what death is like, he had answered that it is like a raindrop falling into the ocean, keeping its substance but losing its boundary.

Thomas answered that each of Michael's answers to "Who am I?" was valid at its own level and each was a different frame: limbs, feeling, awareness, brain chemistry. He contended there is a further level. Drawing on near-death experiences, he posited that the self which travels outside the body and returns is the "I" he is, and that this same I is also part of the all that God is.

**Commentary.** Michael's love of being deserves respect rather than rebuttal. Wonder at existence itself, at the plain fact that there is something rather than nothing, is one of the most universal religious experiences, and the psalmists made it the beginning of worship: *"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork"* **(Psalm 19:1)**. What Michael describes is very close to that, with one element missing: someone to thank. Thomas's remark later in the meeting, that Michael is in awe of the creation but not of the Creator, captures this precisely.

But his claim that this love needs no other trades on the same ambiguity examined above. Delight in being is real, but it is appreciation, not love in the sense under discussion, which is a relation between persons who can answer each other. No one is wronged if Michael stops enjoying the view; someone is wronged if he stops keeping faith with Jenny. Susan's observation that Michael would find real, lifelong aloneness unbearable is the empirical test, and most human experience supports her.

The widening-boundary argument also proves less than it seems. Dependence does not erase distinction. An infant depends on its mother for every breath, yet it is not its mother, and the relationship between them is possible only because they are two. Michael is right that a human self cannot be cut off from its surroundings, and Thomas is right that God holds everything in being. Neither point makes the self unreal.

The raindrop is a lovely image, and as comfort to a child it was kindly meant. As a picture of what follows death, it is the Eastern answer rather than the Christian one, and it cuts against the rest of Michael's argument. A drop that merges into the ocean loses itself. If death dissolves the person, there is no one left to love or be loved; the relation Thomas described ends with the self. The Christian hope is not absorption but communion, persons preserved and fully known: *"then shall I know even as also I am known"* **(1 Corinthians 13:12)**.

Thomas's appeal to near-death experiences is reasonable as long as its weight is stated honestly. The reports are numerous and strikingly consistent, and some include perceptions that are hard to explain as hallucination. Researchers also dispute them, and they do not settle the question. Thomas treated the conclusion as a postulate that others may accept or reject, which is the right posture: the reports are testimony that points toward a self not reducible to brain chemistry, not proof of it.

## 11. Where the argument stands

On the central question, Thomas's position holds. Michael's world without God can produce order but not obligation, and his own most sincere judgments (that the Christian wars were "not a very good good," that the bully in the tribe should be stopped) appeal to a standard his worldview cannot supply. Thomas's account of love explains why such a standard must exist and why freedom must exist with it: love needs another who can refuse, and a measure of what love is. Evil, on this account, is not something God made but something creatures do with the freedom love requires.

The conversation also exposed places where the case would be stronger with sharper statement:


 	- **Freedom and foreknowledge.** Connect the claim that God hid Himself in layers to the reality of freedom: the veiling is what makes genuine choice possible.

 	- **The source of the good.** State that the good is God's own nature, not a preference He selected, so that it is neither arbitrary nor above Him.

 	- **God's motive.** Ground creation in love's overflow within the Trinity, and God's desire for the free love of creatures, rather than in loneliness.

 	- **The Son.** Avoid calling the Son created or a subset; the argument works as well with the historic confession that He is begotten, not made.

 	- **Natural suffering.** Distinguish the evil people choose, which the free will argument explains, from the suffering no one chose, which remains the harder question.


None of these concessions weakens Thomas's thesis; each removes a foothold a skeptic would otherwise use.

Michael's position also deserves to be taken seriously for what it gets right. He is right that claiming God's authority proves nothing, and that history is full of people who used God's name to sanctify their own cause. He is right that randomness produces structure. And his wonder at being is closer to faith than his arguments are. Armond named the difficulty of these conversations precisely: every rebuttal Michael offers rests on physical experience, and God is not an object among objects to be pointed at. Thomas admitted as much when he said he has never seen God.

That is why the most persuasive answers to Michael may not be arguments at all. Susan noticed that Michael already lives largely by the law he questions, and is blessed by it without naming its source. Armond observed that no one verifies God's promises by debate, only by trying them. Jesus made the same offer: *"If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself"* **(John 7:17)**. Michael has spent seventy-one years asking questions with unusual cheerfulness and honesty. The arguments in this exchange can show him that his questions point beyond the creation he admires. Whether he follows them is, as Thomas insisted from the start, a free choice, and the only kind that would mean anything.
