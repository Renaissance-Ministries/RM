# 261031 - Japan A Nation Without a Religion

# 261031 – Japan’s Unnamed Religion


Oct 31, 2026 · @Thomas Lee Abshier, ND


## A Nation Without Religion?


By the usual measures, Japan is one of the least religious nations on earth. Pew Research estimated in 2012 that 57 percent of the Japanese are religiously unaffiliated, and Japan’s own long-running national surveys put the share who say they hold no personal religious faith near 70 percent. Ask a man on a Tokyo train whether religion matters to him, and he will very likely say no.


Yet the same man will visit a shrine at New Year, bury his parents with Buddhist rites, bow to his superiors by a precise and unspoken rule, and give his health, his evenings, and sometimes his life to the company that employs him. He keeps a demanding moral code he never consciously chose and could not write down. He makes sacrifices that look, from the outside, very much like devotion.


I recently watched a [documentary-style video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y1AEzmcLJiQ) that traces how this came to be. It tells the story of a nation whose spiritual life was built over a thousand years, rebuilt by its government as a weapon, shattered in 1945, and then quietly reassembled into something with no name, no clergy, and no scripture. The video ends with a question: is our definition of religion large enough to hold what Japan actually has?


I want to answer that question directly, because the answer is this essay’s thesis. **There is no such thing as a man without a god, and there is no such thing as a nation without a religion.** Only people worship what they do not name. Japan is not an exception to that rule. Japan is the clearest modern demonstration of it, because its gods were built and torn down in plain sight, within living memory, and we can watch what each one cost.


Scripture says this plainly. God “hath set the world in their heart” (Ecclesiastes 3:11; the Hebrew carries the sense of *eternity*), and when men will not glorify the true God, they do not stop worshiping. They exchange Him for something made — and they “worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator” (Romans 1:21–25). The question for Japan, as for America, is never *whether* a people will serve something as ultimate. The question is *what* they will serve, and whether that thing loves them back.


That last phrase is where this essay is going. The true God is not one more demanding master in a line of demanding masters. He is the One who lives inside each human life and experiences it from within. That changes everything about what His law is, why we keep it, and what any substitute god costs.


## The Three Layers


Before any organized system reached the islands, the people of Japan already lived in a world they experienced as charged with spirit. Rivers, mountains, waterfalls, great trees, storms, and dead ancestors were *kami*—powers or presences worthy of reverence. Shrines marked the places where that presence seemed strongest. This web of practice later came to be called *Shinto*, “the way of the kami,” but only because something arrived that it needed to be distinguished from. It had no founder, creed, or systematic theology. It was a relationship with the land.


The density of that relationship is still visible. Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs counts roughly 80,000 registered Shinto shrines and about 77,000 Buddhist temples; estimates that include small unregistered shrines run past 100,000. Either way, there are more shrines than convenience stores.


Buddhism arrived from Korea and China in the sixth century. It did not displace Shinto; it settled on top of it. Over centuries, the two blended so thoroughly (the Japanese call it *shinbutsu-shūgō*, the fusion of kami and buddhas) that Buddhist priests served at shrines and explained the kami as local appearances of buddhas. Families went to the shrine for protection and blessing, and to the temple for death and the afterlife.


Confucianism came as the third layer. It was less a religion than an ethic of relationships: hierarchy, loyalty, duty to parents and superiors, the obligations each station owes the others. It asked for conduct rather than belief, so it fit beside the other two without friction.


The result was a division of labor. Shinto handled the sacred places of this life, Buddhism handled death, and Confucianism handled social order. No one designed it. It grew.


A Christian reader should be fair here. Romans 1:20 says that God’s “eternal power and Godhead” are “clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made.” The ancient Japanese sense that the mountain and the waterfall are not merely matter, that the dead are owed honor, and that we owe one another duties by our stations — these are not nothing. They are real perceptions of a real Creator and a real moral order, received without the revelation that would tell them Whose glory they were seeing. The error was not in sensing the sacred. The error was in locating it in the created thing rather than in the One who made it and lives within it.


## The Manufactured God


In 1603, the Tokugawa shogunate took power and held it for more than 250 years. It closed the country to nearly all foreign contact and froze the social order. Under it, the ruling class adopted a refined Neo-Confucianism as its official philosophy. The scholar Hayashi Razan, adviser to the early shoguns, argued that the hierarchy of warrior, farmer, artisan, and merchant was not merely practical but moral: to stay in one’s place was itself a virtue. In this period, the samurai code later called *bushidō* took its clearest shape, drawing reverence for land and lord from Shinto, acceptance of death from Buddhism, and loyalty and discipline from Confucius.


Meanwhile, scholars of the *Kokugaku, or “National Learning,” *school argued that Shinto, as Japan’s native way, should be stripped of Buddhist and Chinese additions and restored to its supposed original purity. It was an academic movement. It would not stay academic.


In 1868, the Meiji Restoration overthrew the shogunate and restored rule to the emperor. The new leaders faced two problems at once: a country of some 260 feudal domains with separate loyalties, and Western empires already carving up Asia. They needed one nation, quickly, and they built a religion to make it.


The government ordered Shinto and Buddhism separated after more than a thousand years together. They removed Buddhist images from shrines and expelled priests. In places, this became the violent movement called *haibutsu kishaku*, “abolish the Buddha, destroy Shakyamuni”: temples were demolished, scriptures burned, bells melted down. The state brought shrines under its administration and ranked them in a national hierarchy. The emperor was proclaimed a living descendant of the sun goddess Amaterasu, of a line unbroken for 2,600 years. Schoolchildren bowed daily toward his portrait. The Imperial Rescript to Soldiers and Sailors (1882) made his command absolute, and in 1900 Inazō Nitobe’s *Bushido: The Soul of Japan* recast the warrior code as the moral character of the whole nation.


The officials even insisted that this State Shinto was *not a religion* but simply national morality. That allowed them to make it compulsory while claiming to honor religious freedom. It is a maneuver worth remembering, because it is not peculiar to Japan. Any state that wants total loyalty will try to occupy the place of God while denying that it is doing so.


The fruit was what Scripture would predict. A god who is a man, backed by the state, demands the lives of the young. Japan’s armies swept across East Asia in the emperor’s name, and by 1944 young pilots of the special attack units were flying their aircraft into ships. Their letters home speak of duty, honor, and becoming guardian spirits enshrined at Yasukuni. For the most part, they were not despairing men. They were *devout* men, formed from childhood by a faith their government had engineered.


This is the first lesson Japan teaches: **a god you build will be built for someone’s purposes, and when the purposes require it, the god will consume its worshipers.** Every idol eventually asks for blood, and it never gives its own.


## The God Who Resigned


Japan surrendered in August 1945. The American occupation understood that State Shinto had driven the war, and the Shinto Directive of December 1945 cut every tie between shrine and state. Shinto was not outlawed; it was disestablished. Shrines became private institutions supported by their own communities, and Shinto instruction left the schools.


On January 1, 1946, Emperor Hirohito issued what historians call the Humanity Declaration. He disavowed the idea that he was a living god, saying the bond between throne and people did not rest on the false conception that the emperor is divine.


Consider what that meant for a teacher who had led children in bowing toward the palace every morning, or for a veteran who had watched his friends die for a divine emperor. The god had resigned. His divinity had been a policy, and the policy was over. This came on top of defeat, the loss of the empire, the firebombing of Tokyo (roughly 100,000 dead in the single night of March 9–10, 1945), and the atomic destruction of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.


The Japanese learned, in the hardest way a people can learn anything, what happens when the state owns their faith. The lesson burned in so deeply that organized religion of every kind has been suspect ever since. The 1995 sarin attack on the Tokyo subway by the Aum Shinrikyō cult — which killed more than a dozen people and injured thousands — only confirmed the suspicion.


But a people cannot simply stop worshiping. The throne was empty. Something had to sit on it.


## The Religion With No Name


What filled the throne was neither a new church nor atheism. It was a set of social practices that perform every function religion performs — belonging, meaning, ritual, moral law, sacrifice — without ever being called religion.


Its first commandment is *wa*, harmony. Wa is not a pleasant mood. It is an obligation: the group comes before the individual. One does not disagree in public, stand out, or make a scene. “The nail that sticks up gets hammered down.” To break wa is treated not as rudeness but as moral failure. The anthropologist Chie Nakane, in her study *Japanese Society* (1967 in Japanese; English 1970), showed that a person’s place in the vertical structure of his group was a stronger source of identity than family or personal achievement.


Its second commandment is *kūki wo yomu*, “reading the air”: sensing, without being told, what the room expects, and doing it. There is no written code. A person who cannot read the air is labeled *KY* (*kūki yomenai*, “can’t read the air”), one of the most damaging social labels a person can carry. In a culture where social fitness is the highest virtue, that label functions as the nearest thing to the word *sinner*.


After the war, the national energy that had gone into the empire went into the economy, and the corporation became the congregation. Ezra Vogel’s *Japan as Number One* (1979) documented how a defeated nation became the world’s second-largest economy in about three decades. Hard work was patriotism. The large companies offered what amounted to a covenant: give us your life, and we will never abandon you. Lifetime employment, seniority pay, company housing, company healthcare, company weddings and funerals. The boss was the elder; his approval was the blessing; after-hours drinking with colleagues was attendance at worship, not optional.


The *salaryman* left before his children woke and came home after they slept. When two salarymen met, conversation waited until each knew the other’s rank, because the depth of the bow, the level of speech, and the seat at the table all depended on it. This was the Tokugawa moral order wearing a suit instead of armor. The sociologist Robert Bellah argued as early as 1957, in *Tokugawa Religion*, that modern Japanese economic life drew directly on the feudal era’s ethical structure. The content changed. The operating system did not.


Again, fairness requires an honest accounting of the goods in this system. Wa produces a society where lost wallets are returned, streets are clean, crime is low, and strangers are considerate in crowded spaces. Reading the air is, at its best, attentiveness to one’s neighbor. The salaryman’s diligence rebuilt a ruined country. These are real fruits, and a Christian should recognize them as the common grace that God extends to all men (Matthew 5:45). The problem is not that the Japanese care about one another. The problem is what sits at the top of the system and what it asks for in return.


## What the Unnamed God Costs


In the late 1970s, Japanese physicians began using a new word for something they were seeing in otherwise healthy working men: *karōshi*, death from overwork. Strokes and heart attacks were killing men in their forties and fifties who had worked brutal hours for years. A related word, *karō-jisatsu*, names suicide driven by overwork.


The official numbers are modest only because the definitions are narrow. In fiscal 2024, Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare compensated 241 cases of overwork-related stroke and heart disease, 67 of them fatal, and 1,055 cases of work-related mental disorder, including 88 suicides. The government’s own recognition standard treats roughly 80 hours of overtime a month as the danger line. Advocacy groups for victims’ families have long argued that the true toll is far higher, because most such deaths are recorded simply as heart failure or suicide.


The wider picture is starker. Japan’s total suicides exceeded 30,000 a year for fourteen consecutive years, from 1998 through 2011. Not all of these were work-related, but the economic collapse of the 1990s — when the corporate covenant began to fail and men who had given everything to their companies were cast off — is part of that story.


And the culture often answered these deaths not with reform but with a sorrowful admiration for the effort. Japanese tradition has long honored what the scholar Ivan Morris called “the nobility of failure”: the sincere man who gives everything and falls. Transferred from the samurai to the salaryman, it made the loss look noble rather than tragic.


Call it what it is. When a system asks for a man’s whole life, rewards his self-destruction with honor, and treats departure as betrayal, it is functioning as a god. **The kamikaze pilot and the karōshi victim are two forms of the same martyrdom, offered to two forms of the same idol: the collective that stands in the place of God.** The emperor-god asked the young to die quickly. The company-god asked the middle-aged to die slowly. Neither one died for them.


## The Inward Turn


The corporate faith is now cracking too. Younger Japanese workers are leaving lifetime employment behind. Changing jobs, once a kind of betrayal, has become ordinary. The shrines and temples remain busy at New Year and at funerals, but for most people under fifty they are cultural furniture rather than living faith.


In the space opening up, a different idea has gained ground: *ikigai*, one’s reason for getting up in the morning. Ikigai points inward. It asks what makes *this* life worth living. Japanese researchers have studied it as a factor in health and longevity, and most Japanese locate their ikigai not in work but in family, hobbies, and community.


The video calls this the spiritual fault line of modern Japan: the collective on one side, the personal on the other. I think that is right, and I think the inward turn contains a true intuition. Each life *does* have a reason, and the individual is *not* merely a part to be used up by the group.


But ikigai cannot finish the thought by itself. A man cannot be his own reason for living; if he tries, he becomes one more small idol, worshiping his hobbies or his comfort, and he is left alone with himself when those fail. The collective was too large a god, and the self is too small a one. Japan has moved from the first to the second. What it has not yet found is the God who is neither the crowd nor the self, but who lives within every self and joins them into one.


## The God Who Lives Each Life


Here is the heart of the matter, and the center of everything I teach.


God is not an emperor standing above His people, demanding service from outside. He is not a corporation that consumes its members and calls it loyalty. He is not the watching crowd whose eyes enforce the unwritten rules. **God lives inside each human life and experiences it from within.** Scripture says that in Him “we live, and move, and have our being” **(Acts 17:28)** — there is nothing outside Him, nowhere else to be. Put plainly: *we are God’s experience of the world.* Every joy you feel, He feels. Every wound you take, He takes. Every wound you give another, He receives in that other.


This is why Jesus could say, *“Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me”* **(Matthew 25:40)**, and why the risen Christ asked Saul, who was persecuting Christians, *“Why persecutest thou me?”* **(Acts 9:4)**. These are not figures of speech. They describe what is actually so.


Set this beside Japan’s three gods, and the difference is sharp at every point.


**First, the reason for keeping the law.** The Japanese social code is enforced from outside: by the group’s eyes, by shame, by the fear of being the one who cannot read the air. A man keeps it because he is watched. The law of God is kept for a different reason. The One who sees is not outside you looking in; He is inside you, living what you live. You keep His law because He *feels* your choices — what you do to yourself and what you do to your neighbor. Obedience born of shame says, “They will see.” Obedience born of love says, “He will feel it, and I love Him.” The first produces conformity. The second produces holiness.


**Second, the worth of the individual.** A collective god, whether emperor or company, can spend a man because it does not live his life. To the empire, a pilot is one plane among many; to the company, a salaryman is one desk among many. But if God lives each life from the inside, then no life is a unit to be spent. The salaryman’s exhausted midnight was not invisible. God lived it. The young pilot’s last terrified morning was not a statistic. God lived it. *“The very hairs of your head are all numbered”* **(Matthew 10:30)** because the One who counts them is experiencing the head they grow on.


**Third, who pays.** Every idol in Japan’s history asked its worshipers to pay with their lives. And here the logic of the Cross becomes clear. A creature cannot pay for his own wrong; his good works arrive too late to undo what has been done, and to pay in full would cost him the very existence the payment was meant to save. That is exactly the trap of the salaryman who tried to earn his worth by spending himself to death: the payment destroyed the payer. The true God reversed the direction of sacrifice. The Son, the Creator who holds this world within the Father, entered it as a man, innocent, and died unjustly — so that His death was a payment owed by no debt of His own, available to cover ours. Idols demand the blood of their worshipers. God gave His own blood for His. (I have laid out this argument in full, step by step, in the one-page [Premise Change handout](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/10/31/260725-premise-change-handout/) and at length in [Reconciliation of Man with the Father](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/07/25/260725-reconciliation-of-man-with-the-father/).)


**Fourth, freedom.** State Shinto was compulsory. Wa is coercive by design. But love cannot be satisfied by a compelled response. This is why God made creatures genuinely free, able to refuse Him, and why the gift of the Cross is applied only to those who receive it. To impose it would destroy, at the last step, the very freedom creation was built to protect. The God who lives inside you does not force your will; He waits for it, and feels the waiting.


So the answer to Japan’s fault line is not to choose between the group and the self. It is to discover that the One who lives inside each self is the same One who lives inside every other self. Love for the neighbor and the worth of the individual are not rivals; they have one source. Wa at its best is a shadow of that truth — the instinct that we belong to one another. Ikigai at its best is a shadow of the other half — the instinct that this one life matters. Both are fulfilled in Christ, *“in whom all the building fitly framed together groweth unto an holy temple in the Lord”* **(Ephesians 2:21)**.


## Why This Is Not a Stretch


I stand on the side of this claim: modern Japan is not irreligious; it practices an unnamed religion whose god is the collective, and that god has exacted a real and measurable price. The genuine uncertainty is definitional and statistical. Scholars disagree about how far the word *religion* should stretch, and the surveys measure different things — institutional affiliation, personal faith, belief in kami or buddhas — and do not agree with one another. The karōshi figures depend on narrow legal definitions. None of that weakens the central point, for three reasons.


**First,** Scripture defines worship by what a man serves as ultimate and sacrifices for, not by what he calls it. *“No man can serve two masters”*** (Matthew 6:24)** assumes every man serves one. On that definition, the evidence is not subtle: compulsory ritual, a moral code enforced by exclusion, lives given up, and death honored as devotion.


**Second,** Japan’s own history shows the substitution happening on the record. The Meiji government openly built a religion and denied it was one. When it fell, the same Confucian architecture of rank, duty, and sacrifice reappeared in the corporation within a generation. Bellah and Nakane documented the continuity decades ago. This is not a Western Christian reading imposed from outside; it is how Japanese and Western scholars of Japan have described their own society.


**Third,** the motive is plain. A state that wants loyalty without limit, and an economy that wants labor without limit, both gain from occupying the place of God while denying it. Where there is that much power and money in a substitution, we should expect it, and we should name it.


And lest an American reader feel superior: we have our own unnamed religions. The state that would be our provider and conscience, the career that consumes our evenings and our children’s childhoods, the self that has become its own reason for living — these are recognizable from the story above. Japan simply shows the pattern more clearly, because its gods rose and fell in a single lifetime. The remedy for us is the same as for them: to turn from every god that consumes its worshipers to the God who lives our lives with us, feels every choice we make, and gave Himself so that we would not have to be spent.


*“For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we shall be saved by his life.”* **(Romans 5:10)**


## Notes on Sources and Corrections


This essay was prompted by a [documentary-style video on Japanese religious history](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y1AEzmcLJiQ). Where I could check its claims, several needed correction, and I used the corrected figures above.


- **Number of shrines.** The video says over 100,000. Registered shrines number about 80,000 per the Agency for Cultural Affairs (81,158 in 2016; about 80,600 in 2024); the 100,000 figure is an estimate that includes small unregistered shrines. Temples number about 77,000. Both totals still exceed the number of convenience stores. ([nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/features/h00226/))

- **“Unaffiliated” figures.** The video cites both 57 percent (Pew) and 70 percent without reconciling them. They measure different things: Pew’s affiliation estimate versus surveys asking about personal religious faith (e.g., the Institute of Statistical Mathematics found about 72 percent reporting no personal faith in 2013).

- **Decline in belief.** The video says NHK surveys show personal faith dropping from about 35 percent in 1973 to under 20 percent in 2018. The NHK series instead shows belief in kami holding roughly steady at about 30 percent (33 percent in 1973, 31 percent in 2018), with about a third saying they believe in nothing religious. Japanese belief has been low but stable, not collapsing. ([japanese-religions.com](https://japanese-religions.com/en/jrjrtopic/why-do-japans-believer-counts-exceed-the-population))

- **Karōshi figures.** The video’s “348 claims in fiscal 2022” could not be confirmed. I have used the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s fiscal 2024 figures as reported by the Japan Institute for Labour Policy and Training. ([JILPT](https://www.jil.go.jp/english/jli/documents/2026/056-06.pdf))

- **80 hours of overtime.** The video presents this as an average for many workers. It is actually the government’s recognition threshold — the level of monthly overtime above which a stroke or heart attack is presumed work-related.

- **“Mono no aware.”** The video uses this term for the admiration of beautiful defeat. *Mono no aware* means the gentle sorrow of impermanence (the falling cherry blossom). The idea the video meant is better described by Ivan Morris’s phrase “the nobility of failure” (*The Nobility of Failure*, 1975).

- **Aum Shinrikyō attack.** The video says 13 killed and “thousands” injured. The death toll was later counted as 14; roughly 6,000 were injured.

- **Unverified claims omitted.** The video cites a 2019 Cabinet Office survey ranking groups by income and health, and a 2023 Recruit Works Institute survey on graduates’ career intentions. I could not confirm either and have not relied on them.

- **Transcription.** The movement to destroy Buddhism is *haibutsu kishaku*; “reading the air” is *kūki wo yomu*.
