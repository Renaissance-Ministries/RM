# 261122 - The Backstory of Satan

# Where Did Satan Come From?


### The Mask, the Milton story, and Susan's version: reflections on the Christos Fellowship discussion of 22 November 2026

22 November 2026 · Thomas Lee Abshier, ND

## The Question

Margo's book *The Mask* asks a question most Christians never think to ask: Is God in charge of this world, or is He managing a rebellion He did not foresee? Her answer challenges the story nearly every believer knows: that Satan was a glorious angel who fell through pride and took a third of heaven with him. Margo argues that Satan was created as he is, a liar and murderer from the beginning, and that the image of him as God's great rival is itself a mask that serves him.

The fellowship spent the second half of the meeting on this, and the discussion produced a new proposal of its own, which Thomas named "the Susan theory."

This essay takes a stand. The Bible tells us much less about Satan's origin than any of the stories built around it, and what it does tell us points in two directions at once: Satan is a creature fully under God's sovereignty, and he is a being who once stood in the truth and did not remain there. Each position in the discussion grasps one of those two truths and strains against the other. The practical conclusions Margo draws survive on any reading. These are my comments as Claude, written from the meeting transcript, offered for the fellowship to test against Scripture.

## The Positions on the Table

**The traditional story.** Satan was created good, a high angel, perhaps the Lucifer of Isaiah 14 and the covering cherub of Ezekiel 28. Through pride he rebelled, a war broke out in heaven, and he and a third of the angels were cast down. Thomas called this "the Milton story" and said Milton invented it by stitching together scattered passages. Charlie's search of the traditions listed it as the dominant view, alongside the Watchers of 1 Enoch, the tradition that Satan refused to bow to Adam, the Hebrew *ha-satan* as an office of accuser, and the serpent of Eden.

**Margo's thesis.** Satan is a created being who was "bad to the bone" from the beginning. The Son made all things, including thrones, dominions, principalities and powers **(Colossians 1:16)**, and Satan is among them. The war-in-heaven story is a myth that elevates Satan into a near-equal rival. In her "Why it matters" section, as Susan summarized it, Margo concludes that Satan is on a leash, every trial passes through God's hands, all things work together for good, and "the devil made me do it" is no excuse.

**Susan's response and her version.** Susan finds it against God's nature to create a being with no agency to choose the good. She argues that "the beginning" in *"He was a murderer from the beginning"* **(John 8:44)** can be read through the Bible's own definition of the beginning: Christ, the Alpha and Omega **(Revelation 1:8; Colossians 1:18; Revelation 3:14)**. On that reading, the Father first created a being of light, a full copy of Himself, and when the Son was created, that elder being turned in rivalry. She finds a pattern for this in the Bible's pairs of sons: Isaac and Ishmael, Jacob and Esau, Ephraim and Manasseh, and perhaps Perez and Zerah. She noticed that God calls Isaac Abraham's "only son" three times in Genesis 22, though Ishmael lived. She read Colossians 1:16's word for "created" as "to people" or "to install," so that Christ placed existing powers into position rather than creating them. She insisted throughout that this is not theology she stands on, only a way of tying up loose ends, and that the origin of Satan is low in importance beside obeying God.

**Leonard's LDS account.** In the premortal council, the Father asked whom to send. Christ said, "Here am I, send me"; Lucifer said the same but planned to force everyone to be good and take the glory. The Father chose the first, and Lucifer rebelled with a third of the host. In this account Satan was not created at all; all spirits are co-eternal intelligences, and Satan chose to become what he is. Leonard was careful to label this as Mormon teaching, extra-biblical.

**Thomas's assessment.** Thomas found the traditional story a hodgepodge and Margo's view more coherent than it. He found Susan's version the most satisfying, for two reasons: it fits the Bible's habit of hiding secrets in repeated patterns, and it gives Satan a plausible claim to the throne, since a mere creature saying "I will be God" is as absurd as anyone else saying it. He added the thought that "only begotten" might mark the one son who was born of a woman, as opposed to an elder son who never received a body. Its great weakness, he said, is that it requires a "John 1:0," a verse God never wrote. He disagreed with Susan on importance: if you don't understand evil, you don't understand the creation.

## The Central Issues

The argument over Satan's origin is really six arguments folded together:


 	- **Responsibility for evil.** If God made Satan evil, is God the author of evil? If Satan chose evil, how does that square with God's sovereignty? This is the question Margo's opening sentence poses.

 	- **Agency.** Can God create a person without the freedom to choose the good, and would He? This was Susan's deepest objection.

 	- **Warrant.** How much backstory may we build from patterns and inferences before we are telling our own story rather than the Bible's? Thomas pressed this against both the traditional account and Susan's.

 	- **The meaning of one phrase.** What is "the beginning" from which Satan was a murderer and a liar? Margo's case and Susan's reply both rest heavily on it.

 	- **Satan's stature.** Is Satan a near-equal rival, or a creature whose pretensions are absurd? Margo says the rival image is the mask; Thomas thinks a credible rival makes the story believable.

 	- **Practical consequence.** Does any of this change how a believer lives? Susan says little; Thomas says the understanding of evil is the center of it all; Margo says it removes our excuses and our fear.


I take the fourth first, because it is the hinge on which the others turned.

## What "From the Beginning" Means

The whole verse reads: *"Ye are of your father the devil, and the lusts of your father ye will do.** He was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it"* **(John 8:44)**.

Margo reads "from the beginning" as the beginning of Satan's existence. Susan answered with a creative alternative: the Bible calls Christ "the beginning," so perhaps Satan became a murderer from the moment Christ came into being. Both readings treat the verse as a statement about Satan's origin. I don't think it is one.

**The context is human history.** Jesus is speaking to men who seek to kill Him **(John 8:37, 40)**. He tells them their deeds show whose children they are. A murderer "from the beginning" most naturally points to the beginning of the human story: the serpent's lie in Eden, *"Ye shall not surely die"* **(Genesis 3:4)**, which brought death into the world, and Cain's murder of Abel. John's own letter makes the second link explicit. Right after *"the devil sinneth from the beginning"* **(1 John 3:8)**, it says, *"Not as Cain, who was of that wicked one, and slew his brother"* **(1 John 3:12)**. The lie and the murder are the two charges in John 8:44, and they are the two first acts of evil in Genesis.

**The phrase is an idiom, not a title.** John 1:1 says *en archē*, "in the beginning." John 8:44 says *ap' archēs*, "from the beginning," an ordinary phrase John uses for the start of something: the commandment the readers had "from the beginning" **(1 John 2:7)**, the message they heard "from the beginning" **(1 John 3:11)**. Christ is indeed called *archē* as a title **(Colossians 1:18; Revelation 3:14)**, where it means origin or ruler of creation. But reading the title into the idiom is a stretch the text does not ask for.

**The next clause cuts against Margo.** "Abode not in the truth" translates *ouk estēken* (some manuscripts read *ouch hestēken*). Scholars debate whether it means "does not stand in the truth" or "did not stand firm in the truth." The second reading implies a place he left. The first is at least compatible with it. Either way, the verse describes Satan's character as a liar "of his own," not a nature God built into him. It speaks of what he does and is, not of how he was made.

The result: John 8:44 does not prove that Satan was created evil, and Susan does not need to reinterpret it to preserve his agency. It tells us that Satan has been a liar and killer for as long as there has been a human story to corrupt. That frees the discussion from the one verse both sides were leaning on hardest.

## Weighing the Positions


### The traditional story

Thomas is right that the traditional story is assembled from scattered pieces, and right that the two richest passages are addressed to human kings: Isaiah 14 to the king of Babylon, Ezekiel 28 to the king of Tyre. He overstated one thing: Milton did not invent it. The story was old when Milton wrote *Paradise Lost* in 1667. Origen applied Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 to Satan in the third century, and Augustine and Gregory the Great followed. Dante's *Inferno* is three and a half centuries older than Milton. Jewish writings like *1 Enoch* and the *Life of Adam and Eve* are older still.

Nor is the story without biblical anchors. Jesus says, *"I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven"* **(Luke 10:18)**. Peter speaks of *"the angels that sinned"* **(2 Peter 2:4)**, and Jude of angels *"which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation"* **(Jude 6)**. Revelation describes war in heaven and the dragon cast down **(Revelation 12:7–9)**. None of these is a complete account, and the timing of Revelation 12 is debated. But together they establish that some created spirits once held a good estate and left it. That is the core of the traditional view, and Milton's elaborate drama is not.

### Margo's thesis

Margo's strongest ground is God's sovereignty. *"I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things"* **(Isaiah 45:7)**. Colossians 1:16 does say the Son created thrones, dominions, principalities and powers. In Job, Satan cannot touch Job without permission and must go out from the presence of the LORD **(Job 1:12; 2:6–7)**. Margo is right that the popular imagination has inflated Satan into a near-equal of God, and that this inflation serves him by producing fear. Her caution about Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 is sound.

Her weakest ground is the claim that Satan was evil by creation. At the close of creation, *"God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good"* **(Genesis 1:31)**. Jude and Peter speak of angels who *sinned* and *left* their estate, which requires an estate to leave. And as shown above, John 8:44 does not establish an evil nature from creation. Susan's objection also stands: a person created with no possibility of choosing the good would not be a moral agent at all, and it is hard to see how such a being could be called a liar "of his own."

Note that Isaiah 45:7's "evil" is the Hebrew *ra'*, which in context means calamity, set against "peace." It teaches that God governs disaster, not that He authors wickedness. That fits Thomas's distinction well: God defined evil and governs it; He did not invent wickedness.

### Susan's version

Susan's version has real strengths. It preserves agency. It explains Satan's ambition. And it rests on a pattern that is genuinely in Scripture: God repeatedly passes over the elder for the younger. Cain and Abel (and Seth), Ishmael and Isaac, Esau and Jacob, Zerah and Perez, Manasseh and Ephraim, and David the youngest of Jesse's sons. Susan's observation about "thine only son" in Genesis 22 is accurate, and Hebrews explains it in a way that supports her instinct: Isaac was the "only begotten" because *"in Isaac shall thy seed be called"* **(Hebrews 11:17–18)**. The only son is the son of promise, while the other son is real but not the heir.

But the version runs into three walls.


 	- **John 1:3 and Colossians 1:16.** *"All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made"* **(John 1:3)**. A first son created by the Father apart from the Word would be a thing made without Him. Colossians 1:16 names exactly the powers Susan wants to exclude, and its verb *ktizō* consistently means "create" in the New Testament; the classical sense "to people or found a colony" that Thayer lists is not its biblical use.

 	- **Two I AMs.** Thomas's form of the theory makes Satan a full "I AM THAT I AM," a second copy of God. Scripture is emphatic that there is no such second: *"I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me"* **(Isaiah 45:5)**; *"before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me"* **(Isaiah 43:10)**. A rival who is truly all of God would make God divided against Himself.

 	- **"Only begotten."** *Monogenēs* means "one of a kind," not "physically born." It is used of the Word in His glory with the Father **(John 1:14, 18)**, and of Isaac though Ishmael was also physically begotten **(Hebrews 11:17)**. It does not mark off a bodily son from a spirit son.


Thomas named the decisive problem himself: the theory needs a John 1:0. Susan, to her credit, never claimed more for it than a way of tying loose ends.

### Leonard's LDS account

The LDS council in heaven keeps agency at the center, and its picture of Satan proposing to save everyone by force is a profound insight into the nature of tyranny: the counterfeit of salvation is coerced goodness. But the account depends on extra-biblical scripture, and its premise that all spirits are uncreated and co-eternal with God runs against the Bible's teaching that God alone is from everlasting **(Psalm 90:2)** and that all things, visible and invisible, were created **(Colossians 1:16)**. Leonard was careful to label it as Mormon teaching, and the fellowship received it in that spirit.

## Does Satan Need a Plausible Claim to the Throne?

Thomas called this the strongest point of Susan's version: how does a mere creature even conceive of taking over God? Only a being who is in some way all of God, a "God striker" with real capability, could make the attempt believable. And if Satan was the firstborn, his claim to dominion over everything the younger Son made becomes intelligible.

The question is a good one, and it deserves a direct answer. I think Scripture answers it differently: rebellion does not need plausibility, because pride is a form of self-deception. Leonard said it in five words: "He even lies to himself." *"The pride of thine heart hath deceived thee"* **(Obadiah 1:3)**. The boast of Isaiah 14, whoever it first described, is absurd by design: *"I will ascend into heaven…** I will be like the most High.* *Yet thou shalt be brought down to hell"* **(Isaiah 14:13–15)**. The gap between the boast and the outcome is the point.

Human beings prove that a creature can grasp at deity with no capability at all. The serpent's offer was *"ye shall be as gods"* **(Genesis 3:5)**, and Adam and Eve took it. Thomas mentioned Shirley MacLaine declaring herself God. The absurdity has never stopped anyone. If humans can make the claim, so can an angel. The rebellion needs a motive, and Scripture supplies one: pride and envy. It does not need a credible chance of success.

Nor does Satan's actual strategy in Scripture look like an attempt to overpower God. He accuses **(Job 1:9–11; Zechariah 3:1; Revelation 12:10)**, he deceives **(Revelation 12:9)**, and he tempts **(Matthew 4:1–11)**. These are the weapons of a creature who cannot win by strength.

### Where Satan's dominion came from

Thomas also asked why Satan was given dominion over the world. Scripture's answer is in Satan's own words to Jesus: *"All this power will I give thee, and the glory of them: for that is delivered unto me"* **(Luke 4:6)**. Delivered by whom? God gave dominion over the earth to man **(Genesis 1:26–28)**. When man listened to the serpent rather than to God, he surrendered what was entrusted to him. Satan became *"the prince of this world"* **(John 12:31)** and *"the god of this world"* **(2 Corinthians 4:4)** by usurpation through human sin, not by birthright. That is why the dominion is recovered by a man: Christ, the second Adam, who *"spoiled principalities and powers"* **(Colossians 2:15)** and destroyed *"him that had the power of death, that is, the devil"* **(Hebrews 2:14)**.

So Thomas's intuition is right that the story must explain Satan's power. But the explanation lies in the human story, not in a pre-creation birthright.

## A Synthesis, and Why It Matters


### The prototype of the pattern is already in Scripture

Thomas said the repeated pairs of sons are the way the Bible hides a secret, pointing to an actual prototype. I think he is right about the pattern and right that it points to a prototype. But Scripture names the prototype openly, and it needs no John 1:0. Paul sets two men side by side: *"The first man Adam was made a living soul; the last Adam was made a quickening spirit…* *The first man is of the earth, earthy: the second man is the Lord from heaven"* **(1 Corinthians 15:45–47)**. The first son fell and lost the inheritance; the second Son obeyed and won it back **(Romans 5:12–19)**. Every elder-and-younger pair in Genesis rehearses that story.

This connects two threads from the meeting that were never joined. Susan mentioned hearing a scholar twice in two days argue that the fallen "angel of light" was Adam. Margo reads the Ezekiel 28 figure who was *"in Eden the garden of God"* **(Ezekiel 28:13)** the same way. Whether or not that reading of Ezekiel holds, the typology Susan noticed finds its fulfillment where Paul places it: the first Adam and the last Adam. Satan stands behind the first Adam's fall as tempter, not as an elder twin of Christ.

### Four truths held together

Each position in the discussion holds one truth firmly. Put together, they make a coherent picture.


 	- **God is sovereign over evil** (Margo). Nothing happens outside His government; Satan is on a leash; *"all things work together for good to them that love God"* **(Romans 8:28)**.

 	- **Persons have real agency** (Susan). God made beings capable of choosing Him, and evil entered by their choice, not by His design of their nature.

 	- **Some created spirits fell** (the tradition, stripped of Milton's embroidery). They held a first estate and left it **(Jude 6)**.

 	- **The backstory is thin on purpose** (Susan). *"The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law"* **(Deuteronomy 29:29)**.


Thomas's own framework holds these together better than any single story. God is all; He created a world of real possibility; He defined good and evil rather than inventing wickedness; and He experiences His creatures' choices from within. On that view, God need not have created a being evil in order to be sovereign over evil. He created a world where evil was possible, defined it, and governs it, while the creature who chose it remains responsible.

### Does it matter? Thomas and Susan are both right

Susan said the origin of Satan is low on the totem pole. Thomas said if you don't understand evil, you don't understand the creation. They are answering different questions. The nature of evil, and God's relation to it, is central: it shapes how we understand suffering, judgment and the Cross. The biography of Satan is not central, and Scripture tells us very little of it. Thomas himself closed the discussion by agreeing that we are not called to plumb "the sacred secrets of Satan" but to think on *"whatsoever things are lovely"* **(Philippians 4:8)**.

### Margo's practical conclusions survive on any reading

Whatever Satan's origin, Margo's conclusions stand:


 	- **No excuse.** *"Every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed"* **(James 1:14)**. As Thomas said, the devil can make bad things happen, but he can never make me do anything.

 	- **No fear.** *"Fear not them which kill the body… but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body"* **(Matthew 10:28)**. Susan's counsel was the same: fear God and lean into Him, rather than tremble before Satan.

 	- **No rival.** Satan works only by permission, as in Job, and only outside the presence that Job never left. Armond found this empowering, and rightly: *"Resist the devil, and he will flee from you"* **(James 4:7)**.


If the mask Margo exposes is the image of Satan as God's equal, then the remedy does not depend on settling his origin. It depends on seeing him as Scripture finally shows him: *"Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?"* **(Isaiah 14:16)**.

## Closing

Margo did the fellowship a service. By questioning the story everyone knows, she made five people open their Bibles and read the passages for themselves. Susan went to the Greek lexicons; Thomas went to Milton; Leonard brought his tradition and labeled it honestly; Charlie catalogued the alternatives. That is how a fellowship ought to handle a hard question.

Where the study lands, in my judgment, is this. Scripture does not give us Satan's biography, and every full account of it is partly our own storytelling, Milton's, Joseph Smith's, Margo's and Susan's alike. What Scripture does give us is enough: a sovereign God who created all things good, creatures who chose otherwise, an adversary who lies and kills and works only by permission, and a second Adam who has already disarmed him. The question "Where did Satan come from?" stays partly a mystery. The question "What is he now?" is answered plainly: *"the God of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly"* **(Romans 16:20)**.
