# 26810 - Denver Snuffer - Lecture 4

# An Academic Assessment of Denver Snuffer’s 500th-Anniversary Lecture 4


## Historical Accuracy and Biblical Consistency


**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 10, 2026**


[**Source:**](https://restorationarchives.com/blog/index.php/2020/06/02/4th-address-to-christians/) Denver Snuffer Jr., fourth lecture of the eight-lecture series delivered on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of the Protestant Reformation. The transcript provided is the lecture body, without introduction or question period. The series and its supporting materials are published at https://www.christianreformation500years.info and at https://www.learnofchrist.org. Readers are encouraged to hear or read the lecture in full. **Method:** As in the assessments of Lectures 1–3, this essay is an academic evaluation only. Each substantive claim is extracted and assessed on two axes: (1) **historical accuracy** against the documented record and (2) **biblical consistency** against the text of Scripture (King James Version). Claims are paraphrased; quotation is minimal; no judgment is offered on rhetorical importance or persuasive weight. Restoration-scripture provenance is identified where a claim presented on biblical footing originates in a Restoration source. Claims already assessed in earlier essays of this series are cross-referenced rather than re-argued.

## I. Claims Concerning the History of Israel


### 1. The divided kingdom and the Assyrian captivity


**Claims as delivered.** Judaism divided at Solomon’s death into Northern and Southern Kingdoms; the Northern Kingdom was carried captive into Assyria and never returned — the lost ten tribes; there is some reference to their departing Assyria as an organized group led by prophets, turning north into the north countries, after which record of them is lost; prophecies of their return exist, but their own account of themselves is absent.


**Historical and biblical assessment.** The framework is accurate: the kingdom divided at Solomon’s death (c. 930 BC; 1 Kings 12), and the Northern Kingdom fell to Assyria in 722 BC with deportations to “Halah and in Habor by the river of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes” (2 Kings 17:6); the deported population did not return as a body, and prophecies of Israel’s regathering are genuinely biblical (Jeremiah 31:8–9; Ezekiel 37:15–22; Hosea 1:10–11). Two qualifications. (a) The organized-departure-northward account is not biblical: its source is the apocryphal 2 Esdras 13:40–47, in which the ten tribes journey a year and a half to a land called Arzareth; no canonical text, and no Assyrian or other contemporary record, attests an organized exodus, and the documentary trail shows deportees settled in Mesopotamia and Media, where assimilation is the historically attested outcome. The lecture presents the Esdras legend, unnamed, as “some reference” in the record. (b) The terminology “Judaism divided” is anachronistic for the tenth century BC; the entity that divided was the Israelite monarchy, “Judaism” being the post-exilic development the lecture itself later describes. A further datum bearing on §9 below: the northern deportation was not total — Chronicles records remnants of Asher, Manasseh, and Zebulun participating in Hezekiah’s Passover (2 Chronicles 30:10–11) and northern contributions under Josiah (2 Chronicles 34:9), and Anna the prophetess “of the tribe of Aser” stands in the temple at Christ’s presentation (Luke 2:36).


### 2. The name “Jews” and its anachronistic use


**Claim as delivered.** The Southern Kingdom later identified as the Jews; some statements in the Bible identify them as Jews earlier than they were self-identifying by that name.


**Assessment.** Defensible as a redactional observation, with a caveat. The term (Hebrew *yehudi*) first appears at 2 Kings 16:6, in a pre-exilic setting (the reign of Ahaz), within a book compiled later; its frequency belongs to the exilic and post-exilic books (Esther, Ezra–Nehemiah, Zechariah 8:23). Whether the early occurrences are anachronism or simply the natural gentilic “man of Judah” already in period use is not settled; the lecture’s claim is a recognized possibility stated as fact. Minor.


### 3. The Babylonian exile and the portability of the faith


**Claims as delivered.** In Babylon, Judah was dispossessed of land, sacred sites, temple, and functioning religion; the first diaspora was the occasion of making Judaism portable; the Aaronic and Levitical priesthood was literally non-functioning; on the return, they had to resort to genealogical study and Urim and Thummim to declare who could be a priest; Babylonian intellectualism led in turn to rabbinical Judaism.


**Historical and biblical assessment.** The dispossession and portability theses are sound in substance: the exile (597/586 BC) removed temple, land, and cultus, and the reorientation of the faith around text, prayer, and assembly is the standard scholarly account of the period’s significance — with the caveat that the origin of the synagogue in the exile, often asserted, is a hypothesis; the earliest hard evidence for synagogues is centuries later. The priesthood datum is substantially accurate and has an exact biblical anchor: returning priestly families who could not establish their genealogy “were, as polluted, put from the priesthood,” the governor ruling that they not eat of the most holy things “till there stood up a priest with Urim and with Thummim” (Ezra 2:61–63; Nehemiah 7:63–65). One precision: in the text, genealogy was the operative test and the Urim and Thummim adjudication was *deferred to an awaited future priest* — it was not employed as an instrument on the return, as the lecture’s phrasing suggests; indeed Jewish tradition lists the Urim and Thummim among the things absent from the second temple (Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 21b). The rabbinic-Judaism claim compresses chronology: rabbinic Judaism as an institution emerges after AD 70 (the academy at Yavneh), though its scribal antecedents (Ezra “a ready scribe in the law of Moses,” Ezra 7:6) and the later centrality of Babylonia (the Babylonian Talmud) give the long-arc statement a defensible core.


### 4. The second temple as a non-reflection of the first


**Claim as delivered.** There is reason to believe the reconstructed temple was not a reflection of what had been there previously; seventy years removed the generation with continuous knowledge.


**Assessment.** Defensible and biblically supported in substance: the old men who had seen the first house wept at the second’s foundation (Ezra 3:12), and Haggai asks the returned community, “who is left among you that saw this house in her first glory? and how do ye see it now? is it not in your eyes in comparison of it as nothing?” (Haggai 2:3). Jewish tradition enumerates what the second temple lacked (ark, Urim and Thummim, fire, Shekinah, spirit of prophecy — Yoma 21b). The claim as delivered is consistent with these sources.


### 5. The Deuteronomists and the discovered books


**Claims as delivered.** Before the exile, reformers — nicknamed the Deuteronomists by scholars — were trying to fit the faith to a model popular among competing religions; the tension was unresolved at the captivity; the returnees who reconstructed the temple included those persuaded by the Deuteronomists; the books “purportedly discovered when the ruins of the temple were being rummaged through in order to reconstruct the temple site by Ezra,” used “to rebuild the Old Testament that had been lost,” were more influenced by the Deuteronomists than pre-exilic Judaism had been.


**Historical assessment.** Three layers require separation. (a) The Deuteronomist concept is genuine critical scholarship: the identification of a distinctive theological school shaping Deuteronomy and the historical books (the Deuteronomistic History, associated with Martin Noth’s 1943 thesis and its successors) is a standard, though internally debated, position in Old Testament studies. (b) The discovered-book episode is misplaced by nearly two centuries and attached to the wrong figure: the finding of “the book of the law in the house of the LORD” during temple repairs is Hilkiah’s, in the eighteenth year of **Josiah** (2 Kings 22:8; 2 Chronicles 34:14–15), c. 622 BC — *before* the exile, not during the post-exilic reconstruction, and not by Ezra. Critical scholarship’s association of that book with some form of Deuteronomy is precisely what generated the “Deuteronomist” label; relocating the discovery to Ezra’s era conflates 2 Kings 22 with the post-exilic restoration and with the apocryphal legend of 2 Esdras 14, in which Ezra re-dictates the lost scriptures by inspiration — a legend, not a datum, and in any case a different story. The canonical account of Ezra has him bringing and reading the law (Ezra 7:14, 25; Nehemiah 8), not excavating it from ruins. (c) The evaluative frame — that the Deuteronomist party’s prevalence constituted a corruption suppressing an older, richer religion — is the thesis of a particular school of temple-theology scholarship (associated most prominently with Margaret Barker) that has been influential in Restoration circles; it is a contested reconstruction, not an established finding, and the lecture presents it as settled. **Finding:** the scholarly category is genuine; the discovery narrative as delivered is factually wrong on date, agent, and occasion; the corruption thesis is a contested hypothesis stated as fact.


### 6. Daniel 2: the image, the head of gold, and the persistence of Babylon


**Claims as delivered.** Daniel identified the head of gold as Babylon; the succeeding kingdoms — Medes and Persians, Greeks, Romans — swayed Israel in turn; Rome is the legs of iron, under which Christ was born; the whole image, gold included, is to be ground to dust together by the stone cut without hands, which should perplex readers since Babylon fell long ago; therefore Babylon persists as a cultural, religious, and economic influence into the last days.


**Biblical assessment.** The exegetical anchors are accurate: “Thou art this head of gold” (Daniel 2:38); the four-kingdom sequence culminating in Rome is the traditional identification (the principal critical alternative makes Greece the fourth kingdom; the lecture’s is the classical Christian reading and is defensible); and the textual observation on which the argument turns is exact — “then was the iron, the clay, the brass, the silver, and the gold, broken to pieces **together**” (Daniel 2:35), all elements standing when the stone strikes. The inference — that each kingdom’s *influence* persists within its successors until the final destruction — is an interpretation, and a reasonable one, with canonical resonance in the persistence of “Babylon” as the name of the end-time world system (Revelation 17–18; 1 Peter 5:13). Recorded as sound observation plus defensible interpretation, distinguished as such.


### 7. The Luke 2 dating and the Roman frame


**Claims as delivered.** Luke dates the Savior’s birth by the decree of Caesar Augustus and the governorship of Cyrenius; Cyrenius was a Roman puppet appointed by Rome; Caesar Augustus was Octavius, who took the name Caesar after he was the unquestioned head of the empire, having defeated Mark Antony; the dating by Rome shows the birth under the legs of iron.


**Historical assessment.** Luke 2:1–2 is quoted accurately, and the larger point — that the evangelist frames the nativity by Roman administration — is sound. Two corrections and one noted difficulty. (a) “Puppet appointed by Rome” misdescribes Quirinius: a puppet is a client ruler (Herod fits the term); Publius Sulpicius Quirinius was a Roman senator and imperial legate — Rome’s own officer, not its client. (b) The name sequence is reversed: Octavian took the name **Caesar** in 44 BC upon his testamentary adoption by Julius Caesar — seventeen years *before* defeating Antony (Actium, 31 BC); what he received *after* attaining unquestioned headship was the title **Augustus** (27 BC). (c) For completeness: the verse quoted carries a well-known chronological difficulty — Quirinius’s attested Syrian governorship and census fall in AD 6, a decade after Herod’s death (4 BC) under whom Matthew places the birth; the proposed harmonizations (an earlier commission of Quirinius; “first” read as “before”) are debated. The lecture does not engage the problem; it is recorded here because the verse is adduced as fixing the date.

## II. Claims Concerning the Kingdom of God


### 8. Christ did not establish the stone kingdom; the kingdom is future


**Claims as delivered.** Set in the sweep of Daniel’s prophecy, one should not expect the Savior to have established the kingdom that destroys the image; He left those influences intact — ministered, died, rose — and the image continued unimpeded; the Lord’s Prayer petitions a *future* kingdom (“Thy kingdom come”); the stone cut without hands did not happen in Christ’s time, and He knew it, and taught His disciples to pray for that eventuality; Christianity is a step in a process, not an end in itself.


**Biblical assessment.** The materials cited are accurate: Matthew 6:10 is a petition, and its full form (“Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven”) does look to an unrealized state; the image’s final destruction is future in Daniel’s own frame. But the purely-future reading is asserted against a substantial body of text it does not engage. Daniel 2:44 states that “**in the days of these kings** shall the God of heaven set up a kingdom, which shall never be destroyed” — locating the kingdom’s *establishment* within the era of the final (iron) kingdom, which on the lecture’s own identification is Rome, the era of Christ’s advent; the classical Christian reading takes precisely this clause as fulfilled in the first advent, with the stone’s growth into a mountain filling the earth (2:35) as the kingdom’s subsequent history and its grinding of the image as the consummation. And the New Testament asserts the kingdom’s inaugurated presence in Christ’s ministry in direct terms: “the kingdom of God is at hand” (Mark 1:15); “if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then **the kingdom of God is come unto you**” (Matthew 12:28); “the kingdom of God is within [among] you” (Luke 17:21); the Father “hath translated us into the kingdom of his dear Son” (Colossians 1:13); “a kingdom which cannot be moved” already received (Hebrews 12:28). The mainstream synthesis — the kingdom inaugurated at the first advent and consummated at the second (the petition of Matthew 6:10 belonging to the not-yet) — accounts for both sets of texts; the lecture’s either/or (“it did not happen in Christ’s time, and he knew it didn’t happen”) accounts for one set and contradicts the other, including the in-the-days-of-these-kings clause of the very prophecy on which the argument is built. **Finding:** the future consummation is biblically secure; the denial of inauguration is inconsistent with Daniel 2:44 and the cited Gospel texts.


### 9. The corruption and truncation of Christ’s milieu


**Claims as delivered.** Christ entered a corrupt milieu significantly disconnected from pre-Babylonian Israelite religion, holding very little content reckoning before Moses and hardly any connection to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; the nation comprised only two of the twelve tribes — the returned remnant of Judah and Benjamin, with “onesies and twosies” of other tribes through marriage; the blood of Israel is gone; Abraham himself was trying to restore an even earlier religion of the first fathers.


**Biblical and historical assessment.** Partially accurate and materially overstated. Accurate: the returned community was dominated by Judah, Benjamin, and Levi (Ezra 1:5), and the Gospels do present the leadership of the period as corrupt by Christ’s own indictment (Matthew 23; Mark 7:6–13, “making the word of God of none effect through your tradition”). Overstated at three points. (a) “Hardly any connection to Abraham”: the Judaism of the period was saturated with Abraham as no other figure — descent from Abraham was the standing boast Christ and the Baptist had to counter (“We be Abraham’s seed,” John 8:33; “think not to say within yourselves, We have Abraham to our father,” Matthew 3:9), circumcision was the Abrahamic covenant sign in universal practice, and Paul’s whole argument in Romans 4 and Galatians 3 presupposes an audience for whom Abraham was the acknowledged fountainhead. A connection distorted by presumption is not a connection absent. (b) “Only two tribes… the blood of Israel is gone”: Luke names Anna “of the tribe of Aser” (Luke 2:36); Paul writes to “our twelve tribes, instantly serving God day and night” (Acts 26:7) and James addresses “the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad” (James 1:1); and the pre-exilic record itself shows northern remnants absorbed southward (2 Chronicles 30:10–11; 34:9). The two-tribe core is right; the gone-blood conclusion is contradicted by the New Testament’s own tribal notices. (c) “Abraham was trying to restore an even earlier religion… of the first fathers”: no biblical text presents Abraham as a restorer of an ancestral religion; the canonical datum runs the other way — “Your fathers dwelt on the other side of the flood in old time… and they served other gods” (Joshua 24:2), Abraham’s significance lying in his call *out* of that inheritance (Genesis 12:1). The restorer-Abraham doctrine is Restoration scripture — Abraham 1:2–4 in the Pearl of Great Price (“the blessings of the fathers… I sought for mine appointment unto the Priesthood according to the appointment of God unto the fathers”) — presented here without identification of its source, continuing the pattern documented across the series.

## III. Claims Concerning the Structure and Typology of Christ’s Ministry


### 10. The three, the twelve, and the seventy


**Claims as delivered.** Christ set apart Peter, James, and John, elevated for events such as the Transfiguration, where they saw Moses and heard the Father; He ordained twelve and sent them as messengers; He called seventy as missionaries; the three are an homage to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; the twelve remember the twelve sons of Jacob; the seventy correspond to the seventy souls who went into Egypt (Exodus 1:5); Christ was thereby reestablishing the family of Israel on a pre-Babylonian model.


**Biblical assessment.** The structural data are accurate: the inner three at the Transfiguration, Jairus’s house, and Gethsemane (Matthew 17:1; Mark 5:37; 14:33 — the Transfiguration account includes Elias alongside Moses, an omission in the retelling); the ordained twelve (Mark 3:14); the seventy sent (Luke 10:1); and Exodus 1:5 does number the souls of Egypt’s immigration at seventy (Genesis 46:27; Deuteronomy 10:22). The typological correspondences stand on unequal footing. The twelve-as-Israel typology is biblically *explicit*: “ye also shall sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel” (Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30), and the New Jerusalem sets the twelve tribes on the gates and the twelve apostles in the foundations (Revelation 21:12–14); that Christ was constituting a renewed Israel around Himself in the twelve is also a well-supported position in mainstream scholarship. The seventy correspondence is plausible but underdetermined: alongside Exodus 1:5, Scripture offers the seventy elders who saw God (Exodus 24:1, 9) and received the Spirit (Numbers 11:16–17, 24–25) — the antecedent most interpreters prefer for a commissioned, Spirit-empowered body — and the seventy nations of Genesis 10, preferred by those reading Luke 10 as prefiguring the Gentile mission; the lecture selects the one antecedent serving its family-restoration thesis without noting the rivals. The three-as-patriarchs correspondence is attested nowhere: no text links the inner three to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and the Transfiguration’s own supplied typology is different — Moses and Elias, the law and the prophets, witnessing to the Son (Matthew 17:3–5). **Finding:** twelve, explicit; seventy, one plausible option among several presented as the type; three, unattested assertion. The meta-claim (a deliberate pre-Babylonian family model) inherits the strength of its weakest members.


### 11. Christ as restorer rather than innovator


**Claim as delivered.** Christ was a restorer, an antiquarian, bringing back something which once had been — the religion of the era between Abraham and the twelve sons of Israel — rather than founding something revolutionary and new.


**Biblical assessment.** The claim has a genuine Pauline foothold and an overstated exclusivity. Foothold: Paul argues that the gospel was “preached before… unto Abraham” (Galatians 3:8), that the covenant with Abraham precedes and is not annulled by the law (Galatians 3:17), and that justifying faith is patterned on Abraham’s, “who is the father of us all” (Romans 4:16) — Christ’s work as the recovery of the Abrahamic promise-faith is authentically biblical. Overstatement: the same New Testament insists on genuine newness — “a **new** covenant” promised (Jeremiah 31:31) and enacted (Hebrews 8:8–13, where the old is “ready to vanish away”); “new wine… into new bottles” (Mark 2:22); “a new and living way” (Hebrews 10:20); “the mystery which hath been hid from ages” now revealed (Colossians 1:26). The biblical picture is fulfillment — continuity of promise with newness of administration (Matthew 5:17, “not… to destroy, but to fulfil”) — not antiquarian repetition; the restorer-only formulation states half of it.


### 12. Alexander, Greek, and the cultural layers


**Claims as delivered.** The Medes, Persians, Greeks, and Romans each brought culture and government to the land; Alexander conquered first in Persia, then Syria, then Egypt, where he was made Pharaoh; the persistence of these influences is shown by the New Testament’s being written in Greek, the predominant language.


**Historical assessment.** The cumulative-influence point and the Greek-predominance point are sound (all twenty-seven New Testament books are extant in Greek — “much of” understates; the koine’s currency in first-century Palestine and the diaspora is the standard account), and Alexander’s recognition as Pharaoh in Egypt is accurate. The itinerary is out of order: Alexander’s campaign ran through Asia Minor (Granicus, 334 BC) and Syria (Issus, 333; Tyre and Gaza, 332), then Egypt (332–331), and only afterward into the Persian heartland (Gaugamela, 331) — Persia was conquered *after* Syria and Egypt, not first. A slip of sequence, not of substance.

## IV. Claims Concerning the Law of Moses and the Atonement


### 13. Moses and the greater religion refused


**Claims as delivered.** The religion Moses was trying to restore was originally significantly greater than the one he wound up restoring; the people were unwilling to accept the earlier version, so those things were broken, destroyed, discarded, and a lesser law of performances and observances was given to point forward to something coming.


**Biblical assessment.** The claim has partial biblical footing and a decisive Restoration substrate. Footing: at Sinai the people did stand afar off and beg mediation (“let not God speak with us, lest we die,” Exodus 20:18–19); the calf apostasy intervened and the first tablets were broken (Exodus 32:19); and the New Testament itself characterizes the law as pedagogically lesser and preparatory — “added because of transgressions, till the seed should come” (Galatians 3:19), “the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ” (3:24), “the law made nothing perfect” (Hebrews 7:19), “a shadow of good things to come” (10:1). To that extent, “a lesser law pointing forward” is soundly biblical. The specific mechanism delivered — that a *greater* revealed religion was withdrawn and the second dispensation diminished — runs against the plain statement of the replacement-tablets text: “Hew thee two tables of stone like unto the first: and I will write upon these tables **the words that were in the first tables**, which thou brakest” (Exodus 34:1; likewise Deuteronomy 10:2, 4). In the Hebrew Bible the second tablets duplicate the first. The diminished-second-tablets doctrine — the higher law and priesthood withdrawn after the calf — is the reading of Joseph Smith’s revision of Exodus 34:1–2 and of Doctrine and Covenants 84:19–27, a Restoration source governing the lecture’s formulation but unidentified to the hearer. **Finding:** lesser-and-preparatory, biblical (Galatians 3, Hebrews 7–10); greater-religion-withdrawn-at-Sinai, contradicted by Exodus 34:1 as it stands and dependent on Restoration scripture.


### 14. The paschal lamb, the sacrificial blood, and the garments


**Claims as delivered.** The Passover lamb, whose blood on the lintel and doorposts saved from the destroying angel, is a type of Christ; the temple sacrifices were propitiation demonstrating that the wages of sin are death, pointing to a great final sacrifice; the prophets’ imagery of blood on garments, and of shaking blood from one’s garments, derives from the sacrificial courtyard.


**Biblical assessment.** The typology is explicit in the New Testament: “Christ our passover is sacrificed for us” (1 Corinthians 5:7); “Behold the Lamb of God” (John 1:29); the unbroken bones (John 19:36 with Exodus 12:46); “the wages of sin is death” quoted accurately (Romans 6:23); the shadow-to-substance account of sacrifice is that of Hebrews 9–10 (“without shedding of blood is no remission,” 9:22). The garments imagery has biblical instances (Isaiah 63:1–3; Revelation 19:13; the innocence idiom at Acts 20:26 and 18:6, “your blood be upon your own heads; I am clean”); the specific formula “shaking the blood off of your garments” is characteristically the Book of Mormon’s development of that idiom (2 Nephi 9:44; Jacob 1:19; 2:2), noted for provenance; the courtyard derivation is a plausible, unprovable homiletic etiology. No inconsistency in the typological substance.


### 15. Foot-washing as removal of tracked temple blood


**Claim as delivered.** When Christ washed the disciples’ feet, one thing foot-washing accomplished in that culture was cleansing off blood tracked from near the temple courtyards, showing the disciples unaccountable for sin — removing the guilt the blood exhibited.


**Historical and biblical assessment.** Unattested on both axes. Culturally, foot-washing in the sources is the hospitality of removing road dust, performed for guests by themselves or by servants (Genesis 18:4; 19:2; 24:32; Judges 19:21; 1 Samuel 25:41; Luke 7:44; 1 Timothy 5:10); no ancient source known to this assessment connects the practice with blood tracked from the temple precincts, and the Last Supper’s upper room (Luke 22:12) is not placed adjacent to the courts. Biblically, John’s narrative supplies its own two interpretations from the Lord’s mouth: cleansing — “he that is washed needeth not save to wash his feet, but is clean every whit” (John 13:10) — and example — “I have given you an example, that ye should do as I have done to you” (13:14–15); neither mentions sacrificial blood. The unaccountable-for-sin gloss, in the specific form “clean from the blood of this generation,” is the language of Doctrine and Covenants 88:74–75, 138–139, where washing of feet carries exactly that declared meaning — a Restoration ordinance-theology read back into John 13 without identification. **Finding:** historically unattested, exegetically displaced by the text’s own stated meanings, and Restoration-derived in its distinctive content.


### 16. The necessity of remaining on the cross


**Claims as delivered.** The mockers invited Christ to come down from the cross that they might believe; had He done so, the object of the proffered belief would have been destroyed, for the sacrifice required His death; He had to die, and He did.


**Biblical assessment.** Accurate and consistent. The mockery is as recorded (Matthew 27:39–43); the necessity of the death is the uniform apostolic teaching (“without shedding of blood is no remission,” Hebrews 9:22; “except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die…,” John 12:24; “for this cause came I unto this hour,” 12:27; “ought not Christ to have suffered these things…?” Luke 24:26). The observation that the demanded sign would have annulled the thing to be believed is sound homiletics resting on those texts. It is noted that this section, affirming that the death itself was “the actual sacrifice to which everything else pointed” and that “He had to die in order for Him to complete… the atonement, the propitiation for sin,” stands in unresolved tension with Lecture 3’s conclusion that the cross “was not where He paid the price for our sins” (assessed at §10 of that essay); the present lecture’s formulation is the one consistent with 1 Peter 2:24, Colossians 1:20, and Hebrews 9–10, and the tension between the two lectures is recorded as an internal matter of the series.


### 17. The resurrection logic: sinlessness and entitlement


**Claims as delivered.** Unlike all who had entered the grave since Adam, Christ had no sin or error to hold Him in the grave; death could have no hold on Him, since the wages of sin is death; anyone who could pass through this experience without succumbing to the world’s sins would be “equally entitled” to come forth from the grave; His death, being unjust and uncompelled, was an infinite and eternal loss that fully compensated the law of justice — He died for all mankind.


**Biblical assessment.** The core is biblical: “it was not possible that he should be holden of it” (Acts 2:24); “the wages of sin is death” (Romans 6:23); the sinlessness texts (Hebrews 4:15; 1 Peter 2:22; 2 Corinthians 5:21); and the infinite-valuation of the offering was assessed and found consistent at Lecture 3 §13 (Hebrews 9:12; 1 Peter 1:18–19). Two qualifications on the generalization. (a) The “equally entitled” counterfactual describes an empty class by Scripture’s own census — “all have sinned” (Romans 3:23); “there is none righteous, no, not one” (3:10) — and, more materially, the New Testament grounds the general resurrection not in any person’s desert but in Christ’s: “as in Adam all die, even so **in Christ** shall all be made alive” (1 Corinthians 15:21–22); “I am the resurrection, and the life” (John 11:25). A sinless-merit theory of resurrection-in-general is attested nowhere and would make resurrection a wage rather than Christ’s gift. (b) Christ’s emergence from death is also predicated on His person and power, not only His record: “declared to be the Son of God with power… by the resurrection” (Romans 1:4); “I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again” (John 10:18). The claim is consistent as applied to Christ; the generalized entitlement principle is an unattested extension.


### 18. The end of the law of Moses in AD 70


**Claims as delivered.** God, using the legs of iron, achieved the end of the law of Moses externally by the siege of Jerusalem and destruction of the temple (“not one stone shall be left upon the other”); the organized practice of the law inside a sanctuary ended in about AD 70 and has not returned.


**Historical and biblical assessment.** The historical data are accurate: Titus’s siege and the temple’s destruction in AD 70 ended the sacrificial cultus, which has not been restored; the dominical prophecy is as quoted (Matthew 24:2; Mark 13:2; Luke 21:6). One theological precision: the New Testament locates the law’s *operative* termination at the cross, not at the siege — “Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth” (Romans 10:4); the handwriting of ordinances “nailing it to his cross” (Colossians 2:14); the veil rent at His death (Matthew 27:51); the first covenant already “old” and “ready to vanish away” when Hebrews was written (Hebrews 8:13). On that accounting AD 70 is the historical cessation of an already-superseded cultus — providential confirmation rather than the effective ending. The lecture’s “externally” arguably concedes the distinction; it is recorded so that the siege is not taken as the theological terminus the epistles place forty years earlier.

## V. Claims Concerning the Eschatological Frame


### 19. Christianity as a step; the future restitution


**Claims as delivered.** The first missionaries mostly taught Christianity as the culmination of all things Jewish; yet Jesus pointed forward to a still greater event — a refreshing or restitution, the kingdom prayed for — when everything from the beginning would be fully restored; the greater part of Matthew 24 concerns the single generation living at His return; prophets of both kingdoms pointed to that event, as does Daniel’s stone; Christ made an initial effort at restoring something far more ancient, not yet fully achieved; God’s religion has never been fully restored.


**Assessment.** The components carry prior findings. The restitution-of-all-things application of Acts 3:19–21 was assessed at Lecture 3 §9, where the passage’s until-clause was found to place the sending of Christ at the restitution as His personal return, not before it through intermediaries; that finding governs the “refreshing” claim here. The Matthew 24 single-generation reading was assessed at Lecture 1 §20 (the referent of 24:34 disputed; the terminal-generation identification an interpretation asserted as fact), with the observation that the present lecture is more careful than Lecture 1 in acknowledging the near-horizon fulfillments (the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple) alongside the far. The prophets-of-both-kingdoms datum is sound (restoration oracles in the northern prophets, Hosea 3:5, Amos 9:11–15, and the southern, Jeremiah 31, Ezekiel 37). The kingdom-still-future frame was assessed at §8 above and found to state the not-yet while contradicting the already. The closing thesis — “God’s religion has never been fully restored” — is the series’ governing claim; on the two axes it is a theological program rather than a checkable assertion, and its biblical supports as offered across the series (Acts 3; Daniel 2; Matthew 24) have each been found, at the sections cited, to be either inverted, disputed, or consistent with readings that do not yield the conclusion. It is recorded as the thesis the evidence assessed does not compel.

## VI. Summary of Findings


Assessed on historical accuracy: accurate or substantially accurate — the divided monarchy and Assyrian deportation frame (§1); the exile’s dispossession and the portability thesis with the synagogue caveat (§3); the Ezra 2:61–63 priesthood datum in substance (§3); the second temple’s inferiority (§4); the Deuteronomist category as genuine scholarship (§5a); the Daniel 2:35/2:38 textual observations (§6); Luke 2:1–2 as quoted and the Roman framing (§7); the ministry-structure data — the three, the twelve, the seventy (§10); Greek predominance and Alexander as Pharaoh (§12); the AD 70 data (§18); and the northern-prophets datum (§19). Inaccurate or materially overstated: the organized northern exodus as a matter of record, its source being 2 Esdras 13 (§1); the Urim and Thummim as an instrument employed at the return rather than a deferred adjudication (§3); the discovered-books narrative — wrong century, wrong agent, wrong occasion, conflating Josiah’s 2 Kings 22 with Ezra and with the 2 Esdras 14 legend (§5b); the Barker-school corruption thesis presented as settled (§5c); “puppet” for Quirinius and the reversed Caesar/Augustus sequence (§7); “hardly any connection to Abraham,” “the blood of Israel is gone,” against John 8:33, Matthew 3:9, Luke 2:36, Acts 26:7, and James 1:1 (§9); the three-as-patriarchs typology as unattested and the seventy antecedent selected without its rivals (§10); and Alexander’s itinerary sequence (§12).


Assessed on biblical consistency: consistent — the regathering prophecies (§1); the second-temple texts (§4); the persistence-of-influence interpretation of Daniel 2:35 as interpretation (§6); the lesser-preparatory character of the law (§13, on Galatians 3 and Hebrews 7–10); the paschal and sacrificial typology and Romans 6:23 (§14); the necessity of the death (§16); Christ’s own sinless liberation from death on Acts 2:24 (§17); and the AD 70 prophecy texts (§18). Inconsistent or unsupported: the denial that the kingdom was established in Christ’s time, against Daniel 2:44’s in-the-days-of-these-kings clause, Matthew 12:28, Luke 17:21, and Colossians 1:13 (§8); restorer-Abraham, against Joshua 24:2 and without biblical attestation, its source being Abraham 1 in the Pearl of Great Price (§9); the restorer-only christology as stating half of the fulfillment picture (§11); the greater-religion-withdrawn mechanism, against Exodus 34:1 and Deuteronomy 10:2, its source being the Joseph Smith revision and D&C 84 (§13); the foot-washing-as-temple-blood claim, unattested culturally, displaced by John 13’s own stated meanings, and traceable to D&C 88 (§15); the generalized sinless-entitlement theory of resurrection, against Romans 3:10, 23 and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 (§17); and the restitution and terminal-generation applications as previously found (§19, carrying Lecture 3 §9 and Lecture 1 §20).


As a matter of source criticism, this lecture rests four load-bearing claims on non-biblical sources presented without identification: the northern tribes’ organized departure (2 Esdras 13); Abraham as restorer of the first fathers’ religion (Abraham 1); the diminished second dispensation at Sinai (JST Exodus 34; D&C 84); and foot-washing as cleansing from the blood of the generation (D&C 88) — alongside a contested scholarly reconstruction (the Deuteronomist-corruption thesis) presented as settled history and a factual misplacement of the book-discovery episode by two centuries. The lecture’s structural argument runs: Israel’s religion was corrupted and truncated by the exile and the Deuteronomists (§§3, 5), Christ came as restorer of the pre-Babylonian original (§§10–11), the kingdom was deliberately deferred (§8), and the full restoration remains future (§19). The assessment finds the corruption premise to mix sound history with a misdated narrative and a contested thesis; the restorer premise to be half-biblical and typologically overbuilt; the deferral premise to contradict the inauguration texts including Daniel’s own; and the conclusion, as in the prior lectures, to inherit the defects of the premises.
