# 260705 - Venzuela Economy and Governance Debate

# Who Do You Trust? Venezuela, Rival Epistemologies, and the Kingdom Test


**Christos Fellowship Essay — Meeting of July 5, 2026**


*Present: Leonard Hofheins, Michael Sherman, Isak Gutierrez (moderating), Charlie Gutierrez (timekeeping). Thomas Abshier was absent for most of the session due to audio difficulties; this essay is reconstructed and analyzed from the meeting transcript.*


*Protocol: Issachar Form — Triage, Deliberation, Action. “Of the sons of Issachar, men who understood the times, with knowledge of what Israel should do” (1 Chronicles 12:32).*


---


## I. Triage: What Happened, and What Is Actually at Stake


The meeting began, as fellowship should, in friendship: Leonard and Isak trading musical histories, Leonard recounting his mission years in Venezuela in the late 1970s — the bands, the traveling production of *El Plan*, the baptismal font he built behind a rented quinta in the east of the country. That biographical detail turned out to be load-bearing. When the conversation drifted from music to Venezuela’s economy, it drifted into the largest geopolitical event of the year, and Leonard was arguing about a country he once loved and served, not an abstraction.


The event underneath the debate is this. On January 3, 2026, the United States executed Operation Absolute Resolve: roughly 150 aircraft from twenty launch sites suppressed Venezuelan air defenses while special forces raided the Fuerte Tiuna compound in Caracas, captured President Nicolás Maduro and his wife Cilia Flores, and flew them to New York, where both pleaded not guilty to narcoterrorism charges. Venezuela’s interior minister later put the death toll at around one hundred, including thirty-two Cubans acknowledged by Havana as military and intelligence personnel; seven U.S. service members were injured. The operation followed months of escalation — strikes on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean beginning September 2025 that killed at least 115 people, and seizures of sanctioned oil tankers in December. Vice President Delcy Rodríguez, Maduro’s own deputy, was installed as acting president; President Trump declared that the United States would run the country until a transition could occur, with an explicit emphasis on the oil sector. Opposition leader María Corina Machado — the 2025 Nobel Peace laureate, who dedicated her prize to Trump — called for the recognition of Edmundo González, the apparent winner of the stolen 2024 election. As of this writing in July 2026, U.S. forces remain postured offshore, and the transition remains a promise rather than a fact.


So the fellowship stumbled onto three genuinely important questions. First, an epistemological one: when two brothers cannot agree on a single source of truth, how do they reason together at all? Second, a historical-economic one: what actually destroyed Venezuela — socialism, kleptocracy, sanctions, or some braid of all three — and what makes an economy “good” in the first place? Third, a moral one: was the American seizure of a sitting head of state an act of liberation or imperial predation, and by what standard would citizens of the Kingdom judge it?


The meeting engaged all three, sometimes brilliantly, sometimes badly. Both the brilliance and the badness are instructive, which is why this essay exists.


## II. Deliberation: The Arguments, Their Strengths, and Their Collisions with Reality


### The Epistemological Contest: Short Notes Versus Body of Work


The most consequential exchange of the evening was not about Venezuela at all. It was the twenty-minute struggle over sources. Michael, attempting to ground the Chávez question in evidence, offered mainstream and academic references — Harvard, Americas Quarterly, university presses. Leonard dismissed each as propaganda and offered a single acceptable source: ZeroHedge, the pseudonymous financial blog whose articles are all published under the name Tyler Durden, a character from *Fight Club*.


Credit where due to both men. Michael’s instinct — “let’s pick a source *you* like” — was a genuine act of intellectual hospitality. He did not demand that Leonard accept his authority; he tried to enter Leonard’s evidentiary world and reason from within it, live-auditing ZeroHedge’s About page, taking its stated principles one at a time and affirming most of them. That is the correct first move of charitable deliberation, and it should be named and honored as such.


Leonard’s position also contains a truth the fellowship should not rush past. Institutional financial journalism *does* have owners, agendas, and a documented record of consensus failures. His instinct that credentialed orthodoxy can be a form of gatekeeping is not paranoia; it is a defensible reading of media history. And his ultimate criterion — he reads ZeroHedge because, over time, its body of work has proven “on point” to him — is at least a track-record argument rather than a credential argument.


But here the weaknesses become severe, and they were exposed in real time. When Michael asked the natural follow-up — what does your trusted source actually *say* about Venezuela before and after Chávez — Leonard searched, found forty-three results, read out one headline about the December tanker blockade (which, as Michael correctly noted, concerned events twelve years after Chávez’s death), and then declined to go further: “I’m not going to read all these.” A source one will cite but not read is not functioning as evidence; it is functioning as a flag. The deeper problem is asymmetry: Leonard applied maximal skepticism to every source Michael offered and near-zero skepticism to the one he preferred, with the stated warrant being agreement — “I like what he writes… I think he’s on point.” That is confirmation, not verification. Judging a source by its body of work is right; but the body of work must actually be weighed against outcomes, the way we weigh a prophet by whether the word comes to pass (Deuteronomy 18:22), not by whether the word pleases us.


The honest low point and the honest high point of the evening belonged to the same man. Pressed on his claim that Californians drive to Arizona for cheap gas, Leonard finally said: “maybe it’s just all BS… it’s just something I heard.” That admission is worth more than it appears. It is the sound of an unverified circulating claim being caught at the moment of transmission — and every one of us transmits such claims daily. The failure was not that Leonard had heard an exaggerated story; it is that the story was deployed as evidence first and examined only under adversarial pressure. A kingdom epistemology examines *before* deploying.


Michael’s epistemology fared better procedurally but not perfectly. His elephant-and-blind-men principle — accumulate multiple partial sources, question each, triangulate — is sound. But he, too, curates a frame, and his frame showed its seams when the conversation reached Iran, where his rhetoric (“we lost the Iran war,” projections of civilizational annihilation, “15 Hitlers”) left the realm of the documented for the realm of the polemical. The lesson cuts both ways: the man with the better method still needs the discipline to apply it to his own strongest feelings.


### The Historical Contest: What Killed Venezuela?


On the substance, both men held pieces of the elephant. Michael is correct that the early Chávez years produced measurable social gains — literacy campaigns, access to clinics, poverty reduction during the mid-2000s oil boom — and that these are recorded in international datasets, not merely in regime propaganda. Leonard is correct that the same project ended in catastrophe: hyperinflation that at its worst ran to six and seven figures annually, an exodus of roughly a quarter of the population, a police state, and rigged elections — all likewise documented, and corroborated by the testimony of every Venezuelan exile Leonard has spoken with.


The precision failure matters, though. The hyperinflation Leonard attributed to Chávez in fact peaked under Maduro, years after Chávez’s 2013 death; under Chávez, inflation was chronically high but not Weimar-like. And Michael’s counterpoint — that the recent collapse cannot be cleanly separated from U.S. sanctions and, most recently, an outright naval blockade — is analytically fair: an economy under embargo is not a controlled experiment in socialism. Leonard’s rejoinder that Maduro was Chávez’s handpicked vice president, and thus continuity not aberration, is also fair. The truthful synthesis, which neither man quite spoke but which their collision produces, is this: Chávez built a petrostate patronage machine that bought real short-term gains for the poor at the cost of destroying the productive and institutional base; Maduro inherited the machine without the oil prices or the charisma and ran it as a kleptocracy; and American pressure, whatever its justification, accelerated the immiseration of ordinary Venezuelans. All three propositions are true simultaneously. Tribal argument demands we pick one; understanding the times requires holding all three.


Leonard’s appeal to exile testimony deserves special comment because it is the strongest kind of evidence he offered, yet it still needs a caveat. The witness of those who fled is real data — the Venezuelan diaspora is one of the largest displacements in hemispheric history, and people do not abandon their homeland over propaganda. But it carries a built-in selection effect: those who leave are, definitionally, those for whom the regime failed. The chavista base that remained is invisible to that sample. Testimony is weighty; a sample is not a census.


### The Economic Contest: Stalin, Monaco, and a Missed Moment of Agreement


The abstract round — can communism produce a viable economy? — produced the evening’s weakest arguments and, hidden inside them, its best moment.


Michael’s example of Stalin was rhetorically self-defeating. It is true that the USSR industrialized from a broken agrarian empire into a superpower between 1924 and 1953. But the argument proves too much: forced industrialization purchased with engineered famine, terror, and the gulag is precisely the case where “the economy grew” and “the society was good” come apart — which was supposedly Michael’s own thesis, as his child-labor example showed. Choosing history’s second-most murderous regime to demonstrate economic viability unnecessarily handed Leonard the moral high ground. Leonard’s rebuttals, however, were also shaky — the “saved by the weather” account of the Eastern Front slights the twenty-five to twenty-seven million Soviet dead who did most of the dying that broke the Wehrmacht, a point Michael rightly made.


Monaco was a better argument disguised as a worse one. Michael offered it as bait — a “great economy” by raw numbers, transparently a tax haven skimming wealth generated elsewhere — precisely to show that “name a good economy” is an underspecified question. Leonard took the bait, refuted Monaco, and in doing so was maneuvered into producing the finest sentence of the night: a good economy is “the abundant flow of capital and the broad dispersion of that within the populace.”


Stop and notice what happened there. Michael immediately and emphatically agreed. Abundance plus broad dispersion is a definition both a Chávez sympathizer and a free-market conservative just endorsed — and it is, not incidentally, a deeply biblical definition. It is the economy of the Jubilee provisions, of gleaning laws, of manna where “he who gathered much had nothing left over, and he who gathered little had no lack” (Exodus 16:18): productivity *and* participation, wealth *and* its wide reach. The two men had, for one sentence, converged on a shared standard by which Chávez, Maduro, Monaco, Stalin, and the United States could all be measured. And then the moment passed unharvested, and the conversation slid into gas prices. The fellowship’s discipline going forward should be to catch such convergences when they occur and build on them, because a shared standard is worth a hundred contested facts.


### The Current-Events Contest: Liberation or Predation?


On the January intervention itself, Michael’s factual skeleton holds up remarkably well against the public record: the capture, the killed bodyguards and Cubans, the boat strikes without trial, the tanker blockade, the Nobel laureate’s dedication of her prize to Trump, the installation of Maduro’s own vice president, and the administration’s open declaration that it will run the country with the oil sector at the center. Where Michael editorializes is the mafia analogy — that this is open extraction, oil-for-obedience, of a piece with the American client-state model in the Gulf. That is an interpretation, and a contestable one, but it is an interpretation *of accurately stated facts*, and Leonard’s blanket “I don’t believe anything he said” was therefore the weakest possible response, because most of what Michael said is simply on the record from sources spanning the U.S. government itself.


Leonard’s counter-interpretation deserves to be stated at its strongest, because he half-argued it: the Maduro regime was a narco-state that stole an election, disarmed and starved its people, and drove millions into exile; removing its head is the necessary condition for any Venezuelan flourishing; and the administration’s stated intent is to sell Venezuelan oil at market rates for Venezuela’s benefit rather than letting it be siphoned to Cuba and China. On this reading, the operation is closer to liberation than larceny, and its critics are mistaking the surgeon’s knife for the assailant’s.


Here is where the fellowship’s own method — judge the word by whether it comes to pass — gives us something better than another round of dueling sources. The two interpretations generate opposite testable predictions. If Leonard is right, over the next one to three years we should see oil revenues visibly reaching Venezuelan reconstruction, a real electoral transition toward González or his equivalent rather than an indefinite American-supervised interregnum, exiles beginning to return, and Venezuelan living standards rising. If Michael is right, we should see revenues routed through American companies with little reaching the populace, a client government, continued emigration, and a widening pattern of similar operations against other resource states (his Colombia point is already a live indicator to watch). The fellowship should register these predictions now, in writing, and revisit them — treating contested political claims the way honest science treats hypotheses: pre-stated, then checked against outcomes. That is the “body of work” standard applied not to pundits but to *ourselves*.


## III. Action: The Kingdom Test


Thomas has asked that these essays evaluate not only the evidence but the fruit — whether the discourse itself moves us toward manifesting the Kingdom on earth. By that standard, the meeting was a mixed offering, and honesty requires saying so.


The failures first. Source nihilism — “everything you cite is propaganda” — is not skepticism; it is the dissolution of the shared reality that any covenant community requires. A fellowship that cannot agree on any procedure for establishing facts cannot deliberate, and a people who cannot deliberate cannot do justice, because justice requires findings. Second, the Sabbath was invoked twice as an exit ramp from losing exchanges rather than honored as a boundary at the outset; using holiness as a debate tactic dishonors both the debate and the holiness. Third, contempt made appearances on both sides — the dismissiveness of “if you want to believe that, go ahead,” the sarcasm of “oh, but I trust you” — and contempt, our Lord teaches, is the seed of murder in the heart (Matthew 5:22), long before it is anything else.


Now the fruit worth keeping, because there was real fruit. The meeting opened in music and closed in care — Isak’s half-joking blood-pressure check was genuine pastoral instinct, and it landed. Charlie’s intervention with the three-minute debate structure was peacemaking as craft: not suppressing the conflict but giving it a form in which both men could be fully heard, which is what “blessed are the peacemakers” looks like in a Zoom room. Isak, the youngest voice present, modeled the posture the whole meeting needed — genuine curiosity toward both elders, an admission that his generation’s news arrives through Instagram and TikTok, and a request to hear each side steelmanned. And Michael’s benediction deserves to be canonized in our practice: “If we just go talk with folks we agree with, we just nod at each other. If we test our ideas, we perhaps grow.” That is Proverbs 27:17 in plain American — iron sharpening iron — and the fact that it was spoken *after* the sharpest exchanges, not instead of them, is exactly the point. The men fought hard and left as brothers. In 2026 America, that is itself a countercultural witness, and it should not be taken for granted.


Concrete protocol for future sessions, offered for the fellowship’s adoption. First, a shared-source covenant: before any factual dispute proceeds, each party names one source *from the other’s world* they will provisionally accept, and the discussion is bounded by the union of the two — hospitality made procedural. Second, the pre-registration discipline described above: contested predictions get written down with dates and revisited, so that our trust migrates toward whoever’s model of the world keeps coming true, including our own. Third, the steelman rule Isak intuitively requested: no one rebuts a position until he has stated it well enough that its holder says “yes, that’s it.” Fourth, the Sabbath boundary is set at the opening prayer, not deployed mid-argument. And fifth, the fruit test as standing agenda item: before closing, the group asks aloud whether the hour’s discourse made us more capable of loving our neighbor — including the Venezuelan neighbor, in whose name both liberation and predation are now being claimed, and whose actual flourishing, not our vindication, is the measure God will use.


The evening’s true finding was spoken by Leonard himself, in the middle of the fray, almost as a lament: “It comes back to the same question. Who do you trust? How do you know it’s true?” That is not a rhetorical dead end; it is the oldest question in our faith’s epistemology, and it has an answer that neither cable news nor ZeroHedge can supply. Trust is earned by a body of work tested against reality — and the One whose words have never once failed to come to pass has thereby earned it absolutely. Every lesser source, left or right, institutional or insurgent, must be weighed on the same scale. The Kingdom is manifested not when our side wins the argument, but when a community of people who disagree this much can still seek the truth together, submit their predictions to reality, and end the evening checking one another’s blood pressure.
