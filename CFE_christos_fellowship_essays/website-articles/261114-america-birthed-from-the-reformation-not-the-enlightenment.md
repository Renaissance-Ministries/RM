# 261114 - America - Birthed from the Reformation not the Enlightenment

# Here We Stand:


### Why American Liberty Came from the Reformation, Not the Enlightenment

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 14 November 2026

## Two Rebirths, Two Revolutions

I hold that American liberty is the child of the Reformation, not of the Renaissance and the Enlightenment that followed it. The common story says the reverse: that Europe woke from a long religious sleep, rediscovered reason, and reason gave us the rights of man. I believe that story has the parentage wrong, and the proof is in the two revolutions that came twelve years apart.

In 1776 the Americans declared that men are endowed by their Creator with unalienable rights. They fought a war, wrote a Constitution, and handed power peacefully from one administration to the next, as they still do. In 1789 the French declared the rights of man and of the citizen. Within five years they had enthroned a goddess of Reason in Notre-Dame, sent about 17,000 people to official execution, and set up the guillotine as the instrument of public virtue. The first revolution produced a republic that has stood for 250 years. The second devoured its own leaders and ended in Napoleon.

Both revolutions spoke of liberty. Both used the vocabulary of natural rights. The difference was not in their words but in what stood beneath them. Beneath the American Revolution stood two and a half centuries of Protestant preaching, Bible reading, congregational self-government, and covenant. Beneath the French Revolution stood a nation that had driven out its Protestants and was left with nothing between the altar of an established church and the salon of the philosopher. Jesus said, *"Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them"* **(Matthew 7:20)**. I propose to judge the two movements by their fruit.

## Two Movements, Two Centers

The Renaissance and the Reformation began close together, and they shared a tool. Both cried *ad fontes*, "back to the sources." The humanist scholars of Italy recovered the Greek and Latin classics. Erasmus applied the same scholarship to the New Testament and published his Greek text in 1516. Luther translated his German New Testament from that Greek. I do not pretend the Reformation owed nothing to the learning of the Renaissance. It owed it a great deal.

But the two movements went back to different sources, and they found different centers. The Renaissance went back to Athens and Rome and found man: man the artist, man the measure, man the shaper of his own destiny. That was a real gift, and it gave us Michelangelo and the modern university. Yet its center was man. When that center was carried forward into the radical Enlightenment of eighteenth-century France, man's reason was set on the throne that God had occupied. Voltaire's cry against the Church, *écrasez l'infâme*, "crush the infamous thing," was the voice of that movement in its maturity.

The Reformation went back to Jerusalem and found God speaking in His Word. Its center was not man's capacity but God's authority, and its claim was that this authority reaches every soul directly, without a priest, a pope, or a king standing in between. That claim, more than any theory of reason, is what made the common man free. A man who answers to God for his own soul will not long consent to have his conscience owned by the state.

## Luther and the Common Man

It began as an academic dispute. In 1517 Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses against the sale of indulgences, in Latin, as an invitation to debate among scholars. Others printed them without his knowledge, translated them into German, and within weeks they were being read across the empire. Gutenberg's press was not yet seventy years old, and it had made something new possible: an idea that could not be confined to the men authorized to discuss it. Through the press, every argument of the Reformation went straight to the common reader.

On April 18, 1521, Luther stood before the Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms and refused to recant. He said his conscience was captive to the Word of God, and that to go against conscience was neither right nor safe. The famous words "Here I stand, I can do no other" may have been added by the printers, but the substance is in the record. One man's conscience, bound to Scripture, had stood against the combined authority of the empire and the papacy. Europe never recovered from the example.

What was new at Worms was not the appeal to conscience. Medieval theologians had discussed conscience for centuries, and a man who humbly retracted an "erroneous conscience" could expect to be pardoned. Johann von Eck, speaking for the empire, offered Luther that customary way out. Luther knew what refusal could cost: a century earlier, the Council of Constance had burned Jan Hus for defying papal authority, despite a promise of safe conduct. Luther refused anyway. The novelty was not that he had a conscience, but that he would not surrender it to any authority except the Word of God.

Three convictions came out of that stand, and each one put power in the hands of ordinary people.


 	- **Scripture alone.** If the Bible is the final authority, then no council, pope, or prince can overrule it. Authority is placed above every ruler, where any man who can read can check it.

 	- **The priesthood of all believers.** Luther taught that the ploughman and the cobbler stand before God with the same standing as the bishop. Peter wrote to ordinary Christians, *"But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people"* **(1 Peter 2:9)**. A people who believe they are priests before God will not long believe they are mere subjects before men.

 	- **The Bible in the common tongue.** Luther's German Bible, and then William Tyndale's English New Testament, put the Word into the language of the kitchen and the field. Tyndale is reported to have told a learned clergyman that if God spared his life, he would cause the boy who drives the plough to know more of Scripture than he did. Tyndale was strangled and burned for that ambition in 1536. His translation lived on in the King James Bible.


A year before Worms, Luther had already stated the principle that would one day overturn kings. In *On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) he wrote: "I cry aloud on behalf of liberty and conscience, and I proclaim with confidence that no kind of law can with any justice be imposed on Christians, whether by men or by angels, except so far as they themselves will; for we are free from all." He was writing about rules the papacy imposed on the church, not about civil law. But the principle, that no law binds the conscience without consent, did not stay inside the church. Two and a half centuries later Americans applied it to a Parliament in which they had no voice, and wrote into their Declaration that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed.

The Bible in the vernacular demanded literacy, and literacy demanded schools. The Protestant lands became reading lands. A man who can read the law of God for himself can read the law of his king, and judge it.

I must be honest about Luther himself. He was no democrat. When the German peasants rose in 1525, partly in his name, he urged the princes to put them down without mercy. In his old age he wrote against the Jews with a cruelty I will not defend. Luther lit the fire, but he did not see where it would spread. The seed he planted grew most fully in other soil: in Calvin's Geneva, in Knox's Scotland, among the English Puritans and Separatists, and finally in the wilderness of North America.

## From Conscience to Covenant

The Reformers soon faced a practical question: what does a Christian do when the magistrate himself persecutes the Gospel? Their answers became the theory of lawful resistance that Americans later used against the Crown.


 	- In 1550 the Lutheran pastors of besieged Magdeburg published a confession arguing that lesser magistrates may resist a superior who attacks true religion.

 	- Calvin, at the close of his *Institutes*, allowed that lower officers appointed to restrain kings betray the people if they fail to do so.

 	- After the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, the French Huguenots produced the *Vindiciae contra tyrannos*, which taught that a king rules under covenant with God and the people, and forfeits his claim when he breaks it.

 	- In 1644 the Scottish Presbyterian Samuel Rutherford published *Lex, Rex*: the law is king, and the king is under the law.


The central idea in all of these is covenant. Government is not a right of conquest or a divine right of blood. It is an agreement made before God, binding on rulers and ruled alike. Peter and the apostles gave the principle its foundation when the council forbade them to preach: *"We ought to obey God rather than men"* **(Acts 5:29)**.

The Pilgrims carried that idea across the Atlantic. Before they left the *Mayflower* in 1620, they signed a compact in which they "covenant and combine" themselves together into a civil body politic, "in the presence of God, and one of another." They had already practiced the same thing in their congregations. A Separatist church was a covenant among believers who chose their own ministers and governed their own affairs. Self-government in the church taught self-government in the town. The town meeting of New England grew from the church meeting.

The pattern repeated. In 1639 the Connecticut towns, under the Puritan minister Thomas Hooker, adopted the Fundamental Orders, often called the first written constitution in America. Presbyterian Scots-Irish settled the frontier with their elders and their distrust of bishops and kings. Huguenots driven out of France by the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 settled in New York, South Carolina, and Massachusetts; John Jay was the grandson of one, and Paul Revere the son of another.

I do not pretend the Puritans were champions of toleration. Massachusetts banished Roger Williams and hanged Quakers on Boston Common. But notice who corrected them. Williams was a Puritan minister turned Baptist, and he argued from Scripture that the civil sword has no authority over the soul. He founded Rhode Island in 1636 on liberty of conscience. William Penn, a Quaker, did the same in Pennsylvania. The American principle of religious freedom was won by Protestants arguing with Protestants out of the Bible. It did not wait for Voltaire.

## The Pulpit and the Revolution

John Adams wrote in 1818 that the real American Revolution happened before the war began. It took place, he said, in the minds and hearts of the people, in a change in their religious sentiments of their duties and obligations. I take him at his word, and I look at where the minds and hearts of colonial Americans were formed. They were formed in church.

The Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s was the first event every colony shared. George Whitefield preached from Georgia to New England to crowds no building could hold. Jonathan Edwards preached in Northampton. Ordinary people learned that they could judge whether their own minister had the Spirit, and leave him if he did not. A generation that learned to question its pastor learned, thirty years later, to question its Parliament.

In 1750 the Boston minister Jonathan Mayhew preached his *Discourse Concerning Unlimited Submission*. He took the text Christians had long used to demand obedience to kings, Romans 13, and showed that Paul commands submission to rulers who are *"the minister of God to thee for good"* **(Romans 13:4)**, not to tyrants who make war on the good. Adams later said the sermon was read by everybody. Mayhew's own theology drifted from orthodoxy, but his argument was an argument from Scripture, preached from a pulpit, to a congregation that knew its Bible.

The British saw clearly where the trouble came from.


 	- Loyalists called the patriot clergy the "Black Regiment" for their black preaching robes.

 	- Edmund Burke, defending the Americans in Parliament in March 1775, said the religion of the northern colonies was *the dissidence of dissent, and the Protestantism of the Protestant religion*.

 	- Horace Walpole quipped that "Cousin America has eloped with a Presbyterian parson." The parson is usually taken to be John Witherspoon, president of the College of New Jersey at Princeton, the only active minister to sign the Declaration, and the teacher of James Madison.


The written record agrees. The political scientist Donald Lutz surveyed American political writing from 1760 to 1805 and found the Bible cited more than any other source, about a third of all citations, and Deuteronomy cited more than any other single book. I want to state this finding exactly, because it is often inflated. Most of those biblical citations came from published sermons, and the Federalist writers of 1787 and 1788 hardly cited the Bible at all. But I think that is the point. The sermon was the press of the common man. The lawyers argued the machinery of government in the language of Montesquieu and Blackstone; the people who would fight the war and ratify the result heard their politics explained from the Bible on Sunday.

Even the Declaration shows the hand of that people. Jefferson's draft was edited by the Continental Congress, and the delegates added two phrases of their own: the appeal "to the Supreme Judge of the world for the rectitude of our intentions," and the closing "firm reliance on the protection of divine Providence." The Liberty Bell, cast in 1752 for the Pennsylvania State House where independence would be declared, carries a verse from the law of Moses: *"Proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof"* **(Leviticus 25:10)**.

## The Constitution and Fallen Man

The Constitution's genius is its distrust of power, and that distrust is a Reformation doctrine. The Reformers taught that sin reaches into every part of man, including the man who holds the scepter. Jeremiah said it plainly: *"The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?"* **(Jeremiah 17:9)**. If the king's heart is as fallen as the ploughman's, then no man can safely be trusted with unchecked power.

Madison, Witherspoon's student, put the same doctrine into political form in *Federalist* No. 51: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." Since men are not angels, the government must first be able to control the governed, and then be obliged to control itself. Hence the separation of powers, the checks of one branch on another, the division between states and nation, and the written limits of a Bill of Rights. Each ambition is set to watch another, because no one's virtue can be assumed.

Compare the philosophy that ruled in Paris. Rousseau taught that man is born good and corrupted by society, and that the "general will" of the people cannot err. He even wrote that a man who refuses to obey the general will may be "forced to be free." A people who believe their own will is infallible have no reason to limit it. That is the road from the *Social Contract* to the Committee of Public Safety.

The Reformation tradition also explains the First Amendment. In 1785 Madison argued, in his *Memorial and Remonstrance*, that every man's duty to his Creator comes before any claim of civil society, and therefore lies outside the reach of the state. The Baptists of Virginia and New England, who had been jailed and taxed to support churches they did not attend, pressed the same case. Freedom of worship was not a concession of indifference to religion. It was the fruit of a people who took religion so seriously that they would not let the magistrate touch it.

Noah Webster, the schoolmaster of the early republic and author of the first American dictionary, stated the founding generation's view in a letter to Madison in 1829: "the christian religion, in its purity, is the basis or rather the source of all genuine freedom in government." In 1836 he explained what he meant and what he did not: "When I speak of the Christian religion as the basis of government, I do not mean an ecclesiastical establishment, a creed, or rites, forms, and ceremonies, or any compulsion of conscience." That is the Reformation position in a sentence. Christianity is the foundation of free government, and precisely for that reason the government may not compel it.

Yet the founders knew a constitution of checks could not run itself. John Adams wrote to the Massachusetts militia in 1798 that the government had no power capable of contending with human passions unbridled by morality and religion. "Our Constitution was made only for a moral and religious People," he wrote. "It is wholly inadequate to the government of any other." The machinery assumed the people. The people had been formed by the church.

## The Other Harvest: Reason Enthroned

France had its own Reformation, and France destroyed it. Thousands of Huguenots were killed in the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572. In 1685 Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes, and some two hundred thousand Protestants fled the country, many of them to England, the Netherlands, and America. What remained was an established church allied with an absolute crown, and opposite it, a class of philosophers who could see no third choice. When they rejected the corruption of the Church, they rejected Christianity with it. France had no dissenting chapels, no covenanted congregations, no tradition of ordinary men reading the Bible and judging their rulers by it.

The contrast shows in the founding documents. The American Declaration says men are endowed with rights by their Creator; the rights come before the government, and the government exists to secure them. The French Declaration of 1789 mentions the Supreme Being, but it places "the principle of all sovereignty" in the nation. Where sovereignty rests in the nation, rights are whatever the nation says they are, and the nation's voice is whoever controls the Assembly.

What followed is well documented.


 	- **1792:** The guillotine was adopted as the official instrument of execution.

 	- **January 1793:** Louis XVI was executed. That October, Marie Antoinette followed him.

 	- **November 1793:** The cathedral of Notre-Dame was renamed a Temple of Reason, and a Festival of Reason was held inside it. A new calendar abolished the seven-day week, and with it the Lord's Day.

 	- **September 1793 to July 1794:** Under the Terror, at least 300,000 suspects were arrested, about 17,000 were officially executed, and perhaps 10,000 more died in prison or without trial, by Britannica's count. Most of the dead were not nobles but commoners: peasants, artisans, and priests who refused the state's oath.

 	- **1793 to 1796:** In the Vendée, where Catholic peasants rose against the revolution, republican armies killed tens of thousands.

 	- **July 1794:** Robespierre himself went to the guillotine. The revolution, as the Girondin Vergniaud is reported to have said, devoured its own children, as Saturn did.


I do not say every man of the Enlightenment wanted this. Many of them would have been horrified. But ideas have consequences, and the consequence of placing man's reason on God's throne is that someone's reason must rule, and it will not tolerate a rival. Paul described the pattern long before Paris: *"Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools"* **(Romans 1:22)**. A liberty with no Author has no limit, and a liberty with no limit becomes license, and license ends in tyranny, because men will not live long without order, and if they will not accept God's order they will submit to a strongman's.

Alexis de Tocqueville, a Frenchman and a Catholic, visited America in 1831 and saw the difference at once. In France, he wrote, he had almost always seen the spirit of religion and the spirit of freedom marching in opposite directions. In America he found them intimately united. He traced that union to the Puritans, who brought with them a Christianity he could only call democratic and republican.

## The Strongest Objection

A serious historian would answer me this way, and I want to give his case its full strength before I reply.

The objection runs like this. Jefferson, who wrote the Declaration, rejected the Trinity and cut the miracles out of his Gospels. Franklin was a deist. Paine, whose *Common Sense* rallied the colonies, later attacked Christianity in *The Age of Reason*. The Declaration's language of life, liberty, and consent of the governed comes from John Locke. Montesquieu was the single most cited writer of the founding era. The Constitution never mentions God, and Article VI forbids any religious test for federal office. In 1797 the Senate unanimously ratified a treaty with Tripoli that says the government of the United States is not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion. Many historians, following Bernard Bailyn, trace the Revolution's ideas to English Whig and Enlightenment writers rather than to the pulpit. And the Renaissance and the French Revolution are three centuries apart; to blame the one for the other skips a great deal of history.

This is a real argument, and parts of it are true. Here is my answer.


 	- **There were two Enlightenments, not one.** The historian Gertrude Himmelfarb distinguished the British and American Enlightenment from the French. The first worked inside a Protestant moral world; the second set itself against the Church. Locke was a Protestant who wrote *The Reasonableness of Christianity* and argued for toleration out of Scripture, and his *Second Treatise* leans on Scripture and on Richard Hooker, an Anglican divine. Montesquieu himself observed that the Catholic religion suits a monarchy best and the Protestant religion a republic. The Enlightenment the founders read was, in large part, the Reformation's own child thinking aloud.

 	- **The deists were few, and they drew on Christian capital.** Jefferson grounded rights in a Creator, not in the state. Franklin rose at the Constitutional Convention in 1787 to propose daily prayer, saying that God governs in the affairs of men. A handful of educated men may have doubted the creeds, but they addressed a nation of churchgoers in a moral vocabulary that came from the Bible. Adams, writing to Jefferson in 1813, said the general principles on which the fathers achieved independence were the general principles of Christianity, in which all the sects were united, together with the general principles of English and American liberty.

 	- **The Constitution's silence is federalism and conscience, not unbelief.** Religion was left to the states and the people. Massachusetts kept a tax-supported church until 1833. The ban on religious tests expresses the Protestant conviction that the magistrate has no business examining a man's soul.

 	- **The Treaty of Tripoli was a diplomatic assurance.** It told a Muslim state that the United States waged no religious war against it. It described the federal government, not the faith of the people who formed it.

 	- **On the Renaissance, I grant the gap in years, but not the gap in lineage.** I do not blame Petrarch for Robespierre. My claim is that the Renaissance placed man at the center, that the French Enlightenment carried that center to its conclusion, and that France had removed the Reformation that might have checked it.


The ideas the Enlightenment contributed to America, the equal worth of every man and rights that no government grants, were borrowed. They are Christian convictions wearing secular clothes. Where the clothes were kept and the body beneath them removed, as in Paris, the ideas could not stand.

## Many Streams, One Riverbed

I do not claim the founding drew on a single source. It drew on several traditions, and the honest account names them all. My claim is about which one held the others together.

At least five streams flowed into the founding generation's thinking:


 	- **Scripture and the Reformation:** covenant theology from the Puritans, liberty of conscience from the Baptists and other dissenters, and the habit of judging rulers by the law of God.

 	- **English common law:** Magna Carta, Sir Edward Coke, and William Blackstone, whose *Commentaries* trained a generation of American lawyers in the rights of Englishmen, trial by jury, and the rule of law over the king.

 	- **The English Whig and Commonwealth writers:** Algernon Sidney, executed in 1683 for writing against absolute monarchy, and Trenchard and Gordon's *Cato's Letters*, which taught colonists to watch every grant of power with suspicion.

 	- **Classical republicanism:** Cicero, Plutarch, and Polybius on mixed government and civic virtue. The authors of *The Federalist* signed themselves "Publius," after a founder of the Roman republic.

 	- **The Enlightenment:** Locke on natural rights and consent, Montesquieu on the separation of powers, and the Scottish philosophers of moral sense and common sense, whose teaching Witherspoon brought to Princeton.


The legal historian John Witte Jr. has traced how four of these camps shaped American religious liberty in particular: Puritans, Evangelicals, Enlightenment Liberals, and Civic Republicans. Each had its own convictions, and Witte argues that the founders' settlement came out of the tension among them. From that tension came six principles that recur in the founding-era debates:


 	- **Liberty of conscience:** the right to believe according to one's own conscience, free of coercion.

 	- **Free exercise of religion:** the right to live and worship according to that belief.

 	- **Religious pluralism:** many faiths living side by side, none suppressed.

 	- **Religious equality:** no citizen favored or penalized in civil life for his faith.

 	- **Separation of church and state:** neither institution ruling the other.

 	- **No establishment of a national religion.**


Each camp contributed something. The Puritans gave covenant and the conviction that a community must have a shared moral foundation. The Evangelicals, especially the Baptists, gave the strongest defense of conscience and the wall that keeps the state out of the church. The Enlightenment Liberals pressed hardest for disestablishment and for keeping the church out of the state; Jefferson's "wall of separation" is their phrase. The Civic Republicans insisted that a free people needs public virtue and that religion is its best support. I credit the Enlightenment with real contributions to that list, especially the fifth and sixth principles. The American settlement is richer for having all four voices in it.

But notice how these streams flowed together. A melting pot needs a pot. In France, Enlightenment ideas met a nation that had expelled its Protestants, and the mixture exploded. In America, the same ideas were poured into a people already formed by two centuries of Bible reading, self-governing congregations, and the conviction that conscience answers to God. Locke and Montesquieu were read by men who had been catechized. The common law was administered by jurors who believed they would answer to God for their verdicts. The classical ideal of civic virtue was taught by ministers who grounded virtue in Scripture. Of Witte's four camps, two were Protestant outright, and the other two worked in a moral world the Reformation had built. The streams were many; the riverbed that held them was the Reformation.

## The Struggle Since

The Reformation did not finish its work when the Constitution was ratified. Each generation since has had to decide whether to keep the inheritance or spend it.

The Second Great Awakening of the early nineteenth century filled the frontier with camp meetings and the cities with reform societies. From those revivals came much of the abolitionist movement: Charles Finney's converts, Theodore Weld, Harriet Beecher Stowe, and the black churches that had read Exodus for themselves. I will not pretend the Bible was only on one side. Slaveholders quoted it too, and Lincoln said in his Second Inaugural that both sides read the same Bible and prayed to the same God. But the conviction that every man is made in God's image, and that no man may own another, was preached from Protestant pulpits until the nation could no longer ignore it. A century later Martin Luther King Jr., a Baptist minister named for the Reformer, wrote from a Birmingham jail that an unjust law is no law at all, and he took the principle from Augustine.

The pattern holds. When Americans have recovered liberty, they have usually done it by returning to the Scripture and the conscience the Reformation gave them. When they have lost liberty, it has usually been because they forgot that rights come from God and began to treat them as gifts of the state.

We live in a time when the secular story of our founding is the one taught in most classrooms. I do not think that story is wholly false; I think it is missing its foundation. A people taught that their rights come from reason alone will eventually be told that reason, in the hands of the state, has revised them. A people taught that their rights come from their Creator will know that no government gave them and no government may take them away. That is why I believe the work of the church, the home, and the Christian school in passing on this heritage is not nostalgia. It is the maintenance of the foundation the house stands on.

I offer this argument as a position I hold, and I hold it open to a better one. But I hold it. American liberty came out of the Reformation: from one monk's conscience bound to the Word of God, from the Bible placed in the hands of the ploughboy, from congregations that governed themselves under covenant, and from pulpits that taught a people the difference between lawful authority and tyranny. Paul wrote, *"Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage"* **(Galatians 5:1)**. And again, *"Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty"* **(2 Corinthians 3:17)**. The founders built on that ground. Our task is to keep standing on it.

This essay leans on the standard historical record: published letters, speeches, and the work of academic historians. That record was assembled largely by scholars who favor the Enlightenment reading of the founding, so evidence for the religious reading may be underweighted in what gets reprinted and taught. In the other direction, popular Christian-heritage writing has sometimes inflated real evidence; the claim that "94 percent" of founding-era citations came from the Bible is a distortion of Lutz, and I have not used it. Several famous lines are reported rather than documented: Luther's "Here I stand," Tyndale's ploughboy remark (from Foxe), and Vergniaud's Saturn image. I have marked those as reported. A popular version of Webster's line, that the Christian religion "must be the basis of any government intended to secure the rights and privileges of a free people," circulates with several different dates; I have used the wording in his 1829 letter to Madison, which the National Archives holds. The overall weight of influence among Scripture, Whig political writing, and Enlightenment philosophy is a matter of judgment, and I have stated mine.
