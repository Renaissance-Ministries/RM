## 2. Parents are the demand driver

Ed Romero — formerly at Regional Center, now working for Charlie's brother Mike — made the point Charlie called probably the biggest find of the day:

> Parents are the biggest driver of getting services and equipment for their children. If a parent sees something, wants something, or knows something exists, they will press the organization relentlessly until they get it.

### Why this reorients the approach

Every channel discussed so far aims at an institutional buyer — DME dealers, clinics, therapists, physicians, facility administrators. This says the demand originates with the parent, and the institution responds to parental pressure rather than generating the decision itself.

For the pediatric and developmental-disability segment, that inverts the sales motion. You don't persuade the caseworker to buy. You make sure the parent knows the machine exists, and the parent moves the caseworker.

It also explains something about the competition. Rifton publishes a funding guide aimed at families and therapists, plus Letter of Medical Necessity templates families can hand to a physician. They're not selling to institutions — they're arming parents to go get the money. That's the model, and now the reason for it is clear.

### The access problem Charlie named

"I have to find out how to get to the parents around here. I don't know how to do that yet."

Three possible routes came up, and they're worth separating because they are not equally available.

**Route 1 — the Regional Center caseworkers.** Available now, and it's the one Charlie has already run.

This is the answer that requires no list at all. Caseworkers exist specifically to tell families what's out there. A demo to Regional Center staff reaches parents indirectly, through the people whose job is to inform them — which is exactly what Charlie's historical demos did. Fastest, legal, and already in his skill set.

**Route 2 — the facility list.** Probably obtainable, and worth pursuing.

Charlie recalls that during the Progress Equipment years he located a state archive or public record listing all the facilities in California — day programs for the developmentally disabled and similar. That kind of list is typically a matter of public record, since licensed facilities are registered with the state. Worth asking Ed, and worth a records request if he doesn't know.

**Route 3 — a state list of individuals with developmental disabilities.** Don't pursue this one.

Charlie is right that the state necessarily has such data, and right to raise privacy and HIPAA as the obstacle. It is the obstacle, and it's a real one: a roster of identified individuals with developmental disabilities is protected health information and won't be released to a manufacturer under any framing.

It's also unnecessary. Route 1 reaches the same parents through the people already authorized to talk to them, and Route 2 reaches the settings where they gather. Nothing is lost by dropping Route 3, and the time is better spent on the other two.

Discounts of 10% prepaid six months, 20% prepaid twelve.

One detail worth filing: their monthly editorial calendar includes a Mobility issue. If an ad ever runs, it runs then.

### The answer to Charlie's question

He asked whether ads could be directed at just Bakersfield. Probably not — and that's the useful finding.

EP sells site banner positions and newsletter eblasts to a national opt-in readership. Those are national placements, not geo-targeted ad inventory. An $800 banner reaches the whole country, which is reasonable value for national reach and entirely wrong for a Bakersfield test bed.

Conclusion: EP is a national channel, worth knowing about for later, and not the tool for this phase. For reaching Bakersfield parents specifically, the platforms that actually geo-target — Meta and local parent and disability groups — do what EP can't, at lower cost. Worth pairing with the short-form video work below, since that's the content those platforms consume.

## 4. Mike's two points

### Short-form video — third time this has come up

Mike sends Charlie mobility-related TikTok and Facebook videos and emphasizes making five, ten, and twenty-second clips of successful Liftwalker usage. His view: he's sorry that people spend their lives on Instagram and TikTok, but it's reality, it's where people look for things, and it's working very well for the people who do it well.

This is now the third independent source in one week. Mika raised it, Charlie and Joelle discussed it on the 14th, and Mike is emphasizing it today. It should move from "good idea" to a named deliverable of the photo and video session — not a byproduct of it.

It also pairs directly with Section 3: short-form video is the content that geo-targeted social advertising runs on, which makes it the actual mechanism for Charlie's "how do I get to the parents" question.

### "Nobody cares what it's made of"

Mike's second point, and it deserves careful handling because it appears to contradict the differentiation work from Sunday:

> Nobody cares about our story or what it's made of — only what problems it solves for them, how it changes their life, how it benefits their kid. Show that, and that's 90% of the journey.

Both Mike and Sunday's material are right, for different audiences. The resolution is that they aren't competing claims, they're two pitches:

| Audience | What lands |
|---|---|
| Parents | Outcome only. Your son gets out of the chair. You never lift him again |
| Facility administrators, DME dealers | Outcome plus the cost and durability case — injury elimination, stainless, twenty-year frames |
| Therapists and clinicians | Outcome plus the mechanism — why the thigh prompts work without lifting |

Worth noticing: Charlie's strongest differentiator already translates into Mike's language. The no-lift mechanism isn't a construction fact — "you will never lift your son again" is precisely the life-change claim Mike is describing. It survives the translation intact.

What doesn't translate to parents is the material story — TIG welding, aircraft-grade hardware, stainless versus painted mild steel. That belongs in the institutional deck and nowhere near a parent conversation.

So the outcome pitch is the one currently underbuilt, and given that parents are now identified as the demand driver, it's the one to write first.
