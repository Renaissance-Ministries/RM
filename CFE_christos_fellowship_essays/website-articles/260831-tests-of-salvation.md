# 260831 - Test of Salvation

# The Seven Tests and the Verse That Was Left Out


## On Self-Examination, the Witness of the Spirit, and Where Assurance Actually Rests

**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 31, 2026**

**Occasion.** A piece came through the MSN feed this week under the headline [*7 Ways the Bible Says to Test Whether You’re Truly Saved*](https://www.msn.com/en-us/lifestyle/other/7-ways-the-bible-says-to-test-whether-you-re-truly-saved/ss-AA27nUbX?ctsrc=dgst&ocid=edgntpruby&pc=EDGEDB&cvid=6a90f6e3d153404dbbe4826c938f6eb1&cvpid=6a9453ebcc8a4aa9b689984ba95a1680&uxmode=ruby&ei=47#image=7). It is a slideshow listicle — seven numbered panels, each with a stock image, a paragraph of warm explanatory prose, and a pull-quoted verse. The originating publisher is Oldest.org, a general-interest site whose ordinary catalog runs to oldest-cities and oldest-artifacts material; the piece is syndicated to MSN under their arrangement, and no author byline was recoverable from either page. Readers who want to judge my characterization can find it at [oldest.org](https://www.oldest.org/religion/test-whether-youre-truly-saved/).

In May, we took up an MSN-syndicated article on why God did not forgive the fallen angels and found it thin, redundant, and unsourced, missing the Christological hinge—Hebrews 2:16—that the classical tradition treats as the actual answer to its own question. This piece is not that. Its seven items are drawn from the Johannine letters and, item by item, continue what the letters say. Its framing paragraph explicitly disclaims the aim of leaving readers in what it calls *agonizing paranoia*, and says the apostles did not intend that either.

But the piece errs by omission. The First Epistle of John contains the seven kinds of evidence the article lists. It also contains two provisions the article does not list — the witness of the Spirit, and the explicit instruction about what to do when your own heart returns a guilty verdict. Take the seven, drop those two, and you have converted a letter written to *settle* anxious Christians into an instrument that produces anxiety in them. Every item stays orthodox. The pastoral vector reverses.

That reversal is what this essay is about. Along the way I want to do four other things: distinguish the three tenses in which the New Testament uses the word *saved*, because the article’s question collapses them; show that the seven tests are not seven; run the seven against a case our Lord Himself adjudicated, and watch them return the wrong verdict; and put the ontological floor under the whole discussion, because the question *how do I know I have been changed?* has an answer at the level of what reality is made of, and that answer is better than any checklist.


---


## I. What the piece gets right

**The genre of self-examination is biblical, not evangelical invention.** John says plainly why he wrote: *These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life* (1 John 5:13). He does supply tests. Paul commands the exercise directly: *Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves* (2 Corinthians 13:5). Peter says *give diligence to make your calling and election sure* (2 Peter 1:10). A Christian who has never asked the question has not been paying attention to his Bible. The article is not importing a foreign practice.

**The particular seven are drawn from the right places.** The light/darkness contrast (1 John 1:5-7), the treatment of confessed sin (1:8-10), obedience as the sign of knowing Him (2:3-4), love of the brethren as the passage from death to life (3:14, with John 13:35), appetite for the word (1 Peter 2:2), the incarnational confession (1 John 4:2), and endurance (Hebrews 3:14 with 1 John 2:19) — six of these seven come from one short letter, and the seventh is its natural companion. The article did not invent its list; it read it off the page.

**Two of the items are better than average.** The second item’s distinction — that the difference between the converted and the unconverted is not the absence of sin but what happens in the hour after the sin — is exactly the distinction Paul draws between godly sorrow and the sorrow of the world: *For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death* (2 Corinthians 7:10). Godly sorrow grieves an offense against a Person; worldly sorrow grieves a consequence to oneself. The article’s language of having wounded a valued relationship rather than broken an arbitrary rule reaches that distinction in ordinary English, and I would be glad to hear it said that well from most pulpits. Its wordplay — that salvation does not make you sinless but does make you sin less — is genuinely good, and true.

The sixth item is likewise right where it matters most: a sincere faith in a Jesus who has been edited cannot save, because the saving is done by the Jesus who is, not by the sincerity of the believer. I will develop that below, because it is the item where the article’s instinct is correct and its reasoning stops one level short.

So: not a heresy, not a hack job. An orthodox summary of real biblical material, written by someone who appears to want to help. The problem is structural, and structural problems in pastoral writing do more damage than doctrinal ones, because nobody notices them.

## II. Definitional precision: *saved* is three tenses, not one

Before the tests, the term. The article’s title asks whether you are *truly saved*, and treats that as a single binary fact about you that the seven items will resolve. The New Testament does not use the word that way. It uses it in three tenses, and confusing them is the source of a great deal of unnecessary torment.

**Past tense — justification.** *Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ* (Romans 5:1). *For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast* (Ephesians 2:8-9). This is a completed transaction, effected outside me, at a point in time not my own. Its ground is Christ’s finished work; its instrument is faith; its evidence is not, in the first instance, anything visible in me at all.

**Present tense — sanctification.** *Unto us which are saved it is the power of God* (1 Corinthians 1:18); the participle is present and continuous — *unto us who are being saved*. And immediately after the Ephesians passage above: *For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them* (Ephesians 2:10). This is a process, underway, uneven, observable.

**Future tense — glorification.** *Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him* (Romans 5:9). *Now is our salvation nearer than when we believed* (Romans 13:11). This has not happened yet.

Now look at what the seven items actually measure. Walking in the light, grieving over sin, obeying, loving the brethren, hungering for the word, holding the confession, enduring — every one of these is an observation about the *present* tense. They are readings taken on sanctification in progress. And the article uses them to adjudicate the *past* tense: whether the transaction happened.

That is not an illegitimate inference. It is precisely the inference John draws, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Fruit does testify to the root. But it is an inference across a category boundary, and inferences across category boundaries have to be made carefully, with attention to what can go wrong. What can go wrong here is obvious once named: the present tense is variable. It has good weeks and bad ones, seasons of appetite and seasons of dryness, illnesses that flatten affect, griefs that flatten everything. If a variable present-tense reading is the sole instrument for adjudicating a fixed past-tense fact, then the fixed fact will appear to flicker on and off, and the Christian holding the instrument will conclude that his salvation is flickering on and off. It is not. The instrument is.

This is why the older divines insisted on distinguishing the *ground* of salvation from the *evidence* of it. The ground is Christ, and it does not move. The evidence is in me, and it does. Confuse the two and every bad month becomes an ontological emergency. The article’s title question — *are you truly saved?* — is asked about the ground; its seven answers are all readings off the evidence; and nothing in the piece tells the reader that these are two different things.

## III. Seven that are three that are one

Now the list itself, and the same structural finding we made in May: the number in the headline is not the number of things being said.

The May piece advertised ten reasons and delivered four. This one advertises seven tests and delivers, on inspection, three — and beneath the three, one.

**The three.** Since Robert Law’s study of the Johannine letters in 1909, students of 1 John have recognized that the letter runs three tests, stated and restated in a spiral rather than a list. Law called them the tests of righteousness, of love, and of belief. Sort the article’s seven into them and they go without a remainder:


 	- **The moral test — righteousness.** Item 1 (walking in the light), item 2 (the reaction to sin), item 3 (obedience). Three panels, one test: *does your will bend toward God’s when the two disagree?*

 	- **The social test — love.** Item 4 (love of the brethren). One panel, one test: *do you actually love these particular people?*

 	- **The doctrinal test — belief.** Item 6 (the incarnational confession). One panel, one test: *whom do you actually worship?*


Item 5 (appetite for the word) is not a fourth test but a symptom of the first and third together — you feed on what you love and trust. Item 7 (endurance) is not a fourth test but the first three extended along the axis of time. Seven panels, three tests.

**The one.** And the three are three angles on a single question, which the Grammar states as the fifth of the seven foundational perspectives: *love is identity formation; we become what we love.* Every one of the three tests is a reading of the direction and object of the heart’s love. The moral test asks what you love enough to obey. The social test asks whom you love enough to serve. The doctrinal test asks whom you love enough to refuse to edit. Augustine put it in four Latin words in the *Confessions*: *pondus meum amor meus* — my love is my weight; by it I am carried wherever I am carried. The tests do not measure seven separate performances. They take seven bearings on one trajectory, and the trajectory is set by what the heart has come to love.

This matters practically, and here is why. A list of seven invites arithmetic — five out of seven, four out of seven, a passing grade. One question does not. If the seven are seven, a man can pass most of them and be at peace while his heart is elsewhere; and a woman can fail two on a bad month and conclude she is lost. If the seven are one, the arithmetic disappears and the question becomes the only one that was ever being asked: *what does your heart love, and what is it becoming?* That question cannot be scored. It can only be answered, in the presence of God, honestly.

## IV. The two provisions the article left out

Here is the omission, and it is the center of this essay.

The First Epistle of John gives the seven kinds of evidence the article lists. It also gives two things the article does not mention at all.

**The first is the witness of the Spirit.** John names it twice, in the two places where he is most explicit about how assurance is actually obtained:
> *And hereby we know that he abideth in us, by the Spirit which he hath given us.* — 1 John 3:24

> *Hereby know we that we dwell in him, and he in us, because he hath given us of his Spirit.* — 1 John 4:13

Paul says the same thing and locates it more precisely: *The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God* (Romans 8:16). Note the grammar. It is not that my spirit examines my behavior and infers a conclusion. It is that another Witness testifies *alongside* my spirit — a second voice in the room, not my own, not produced by my own inference, whose testimony is direct. This is the one item on the whole subject that the believer does not generate, cannot manufacture, and does not score. And it is the only item John explicitly labels as the *hereby* of abiding, twice.

The article contains no Spirit. Seven panels on how to know you belong to God, and the Third Person of the Trinity — whose declared office in this exact matter is to bear witness — does not appear. That is not a small editorial choice. It leaves the reader with a self-administered behavioral audit and no witness but himself.

**The second is the provision for a condemning heart.** In the middle of the love test, John does something no listicle would ever do: he anticipates that the test will hurt, and provides for it.
> *And hereby we know that we are of the truth, and shall assure our hearts before him. For if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things. Beloved, if our heart condemn us not, then have we confidence toward God.* — 1 John 3:19-21

Read that again, because it is the verse the whole genre leaves out. John has just told his readers to love not in word but in deed and in truth. He knows exactly what will happen next in a sincere reader: the reader will look at his own love, find it thin, and be condemned by his own heart. And John’s response is not *then examine yourself further*. His response is that **God is greater than your heart**. Your heart is not the highest court. Its verdict is not final. It knows less than God does, and God — who knows more, including everything your heart is accusing you of and a great deal it has forgotten — is the one whose judgment stands.

That is a provision of extraordinary tenderness, and it exists in the text for one reason: because the tests, honestly applied by a tender conscience, will produce a condemning verdict, and John does not want that verdict to be the last word. He wrote the letter *that ye may know* (5:13), not that ye may wonder.

Strike those two provisions and what remains is not 1 John. It is 1 John’s evidence section with the object removed and the comforter removed — the confirmatory tier presented as though it were the whole structure. Assurance in the New Testament stands on three legs: the **object** (Christ and His finished work, which does not move), the **witness** (the Spirit, given, not achieved), and the **fruit** (the tests, real but variable). The article gives leg three and calls it the stool.

This is the same species of finding as the May essay, and I want the fellowship to see the pattern. There, the listicle asked why the fallen angels are unforgiven and never reached Hebrews 2:16 — the Incarnation, the actual hinge. Here, the listicle asks how you know you are saved and never reaches 1 John 3:20 and the witness of the Spirit — again, the actual hinge. Twice now the form has delivered a correct periphery around a missing center. That is worth noticing as a habit of the genre rather than an accident of two writers.

## V. The practical test: run the seven on Luke 18

Abstractions are cheap. Let us take the article’s seven items, hand them to two men in a case our Lord adjudicated Himself, and see what verdict the instrument returns.

*Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican* (Luke 18:10).

**The Pharisee.** Item 1, walking in the light: he has separated himself scrupulously from every darkness he can name — extortioners, the unjust, adulterers. Passes. Item 2, attitude toward sin: he hates it, publicly and by name. Passes. Item 3, obedience: *I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess* — obedience beyond what the law requires. Passes emphatically. Item 5, hunger for the word: he is a Pharisee; the text is his life’s study. Passes. Item 6, doctrine: his theology is the orthodox theology of his day — one God, the resurrection, angels and spirits, the authority of the Scriptures. Passes. Item 7, endurance: he has been at this for decades and shows no sign of quitting. Passes.

Six of seven, several of them emphatically.

**The publican.** He *would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner.* Ask him about walking in the light and he will tell you he has walked in darkness. Ask about his obedience and he will say he has none worth naming. Ask about his hunger for the word, his record of endurance, his standing among the brethren — he is a tax farmer for the occupying power; the brethren will not stand near him. By self-report he scores approximately zero.

*I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.*

The scorecard returned the wrong answer, and the wrongness is not incidental — the parable was told *unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others* (18:9), which is to say, told against the practice of self-scoring itself. Matthew records the same reversal in still starker terms: *Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name? and in thy name have cast out devils? and in thy name done many wonderful works? And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you* (Matthew 7:22-23). Those men had works to show — better works than most of us can produce. What they did not have was the thing no list can measure: they were not known by Him.

Now, the fair objection, and I want to raise it myself rather than let it sit. **John’s actual tests do catch the Pharisee.** Item 4 — the love test — is the one he fails, and he fails it in the same breath as he passes the others: *God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are.* He despises his neighbor. And 1 John is merciless on precisely that point: *He that saith he is in the light, and hateth his brother, is in darkness even until now* (2:9). So the letter is not defective. Its instrument is self-correcting, because it puts at the center the one test that pride cannot game. A man can counterfeit obedience, appetite, orthodoxy, and endurance. He cannot counterfeit love of the brethren, because contempt leaks.

This is exactly why John’s material must not be flattened into a list of equals. In the letter, love is the center and the others radiate from it. In a seven-panel slideshow, love is item 4 of 7 — one line on a scorecard, worth the same as the others, and easily outvoted by six passes. The flattening is what breaks the instrument. The letter diagnoses the Pharisee; the listicle would have congratulated him.

And the publican? He would have read the article, scored zero, and gone away worse. That is the second half of the damage, and it is the half I care most about pastorally, because in twenty-five years of practice the people I have seen tormented by this question are almost never the ones who should be.

## VI. An etymological pause: two Greek words for *test*

The New Testament has two verbs where our English has one, and the difference is the whole difference between what Paul commands and what the genre performs.

**δοκιμάζω (*dokimazō*)** is the assayer’s word. It is what you do to metal to establish that it is genuine — heat it, test it, and *approve* it. Its result-noun *dokimē* is provenness, tested character; its adjective *dokimos* means approved, having passed. The whole orientation of the word is toward vindication. You assay silver because you expect silver.

**πειράζω (*peirazō*)** is the other word. It also means to test, but its trajectory bends the other way — toward trying, tempting, testing in order to find the breaking point. It is the word used of the devil in the wilderness.

Now read Paul’s command in the Greek: *πειράζετε ἑαυτούς, εἰ ἐστὲ ἐν τῇ πίστει· ἑαυτοὺς δοκιμάζετε* — *examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves* (2 Corinthians 13:5). Both verbs, in that order: probe, then assay. And Paul finishes the sentence by telling the Corinthians what result he expects: *Know ye not your own selves, how that Jesus Christ is in you, except ye be reprobates?* The word rendered *reprobates* is *ἀδόκιμοι* — failed the assay, rejected metal. Paul’s expectation is the negative of the negative. He expects them to find Christ in them. The examination is meant to terminate in a recognition, not in a score.

Set that beside the listicle’s form. A slideshow whose headline asks whether you are *truly* saved, whose panels advance one at a time, and whose revenue depends on your continuing to click, is applying *peirazō* energy to a *dokimazō* task. It probes; it does not assay. It is structurally incapable of the concluding move — *know ye not your own selves, that Jesus Christ is in you* — because that move ends the session, and ending the session is the one thing the form cannot afford.

## VII. The genre finding, advanced

In May I argued that the SEO listicle cannot do theology because it cannot cite, cannot sustain a paragraph, and must pad three or four ideas out to a round headline number. I will not re-argue that; it is in the archive and it stands. But this piece adds a finding the earlier one did not surface, and I think it is the more important of the two.

**The form has a pastoral vector, and the click economy sets it toward anxiety.**

Consider the incentives honestly, without accusing anyone of bad faith. A headline that asks *are you truly saved?* outperforms a headline that says *you may rest in Christ*, because unresolved threat holds attention and resolution releases it. A seven-panel slideshow monetizes the interval between panel one and panel seven; anything that resolves the reader’s disquiet on panel two destroys the remaining six panels’ value. Nothing in this requires the writer to intend harm. It requires only that pieces which relieve anxiety quickly get fewer clicks than pieces which sustain it, and that over thousands of iterations the surviving pieces are the ones that sustain it. That is selection, not malice, and it operates on Christian content exactly as it operates on health scares and financial warnings.

So the genre, applied to the doctrine of assurance, will reliably produce a version of 1 John with the comfort removed — not because the writer removed it, but because the versions with the comfort intact did not propagate. That is the structural reason the two provisions in §IV are missing. They are the two provisions that would have ended the reader’s disquiet, and the form selects against ending the reader’s disquiet.

The fellowship’s discipline follows from this directly, and it is a reading discipline, not a boycott. When a piece of religious content reaches you through an algorithmic feed, ask not only *is this true?* but *what is this shaped to do to me?* Truth-by-item and effect-by-form are two different audits, and an orthodox piece can fail the second while passing the first. This one does.

## VIII. The doctrine test, at the level below it

The sixth item is right that a modified Jesus cannot save, and its instinct — that we do not get to edit Him to fit the moment’s convictions — is sound. But the article gives the reason as sincerity being insufficient, and stops. There is a level underneath that, and it is the level where the claim becomes unanswerable rather than merely asserted.

Every serious religious system faces the same structural problem: there is a moral debt, and something has to be done with it. What distinguishes the systems is not their ethics, which overlap far more than partisans admit, but what each one *does with the debt*.


 	- **Islam** hopes that mercy will outweigh it, with the scales unrevealed until the day.

 	- **Buddhism** exits the system in which debts are contracted at all.

 	- **Hinduism** amortizes it across lifetimes, with no principle by which the ledger ever closes.

 	- **Secular humanism** denies that the debt was ever incurred.

 	- **Christianity** alone says that the debt is real, is paid in full, and is paid by the Creditor at His own cost.


Now put the edited Jesuses into that frame. The Jesus who is a good moral teacher is not a diminished Christianity; he is the *humanist* answer wearing Christian vocabulary — a Jesus who instructs about the debt rather than discharging it. The Jesus who is a life coach is the therapeutic answer in the same costume. These are not smaller versions of the Christian solution. They are structurally other solutions, and they solve nothing, because instruction and encouragement do not retire a debt.

This is why John’s test is worded the way it is. Not *does this spirit speak well of Jesus* but: *Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God* (1 John 4:2). The load is on *come in the flesh*. The confession that saves is a confession about the Incarnation, because the Incarnation is the mechanism — God taking on the nature of the debtor in order to pay in that nature. Which is exactly where the May essay landed from the opposite direction: *he took not on him the nature of angels; but he took on him the seed of Abraham* (Hebrews 2:16). Two listicles, two missing centers, and the missing center is the same one both times. It is always the Incarnation. That is not a coincidence about listicles; it is a fact about the gospel, which has one hinge, and about every substitute for it, which has none.

The pastoral form of the sixth test is therefore not *have you screened your Christology for errors?* It is: *when you pray, who is at the other end?* If it is the Christ who became flesh, died as a substitute, and rose bodily, you are praying to the One who is there. The test is about the object, not about the quality of your grip on it — which is the mercy in it, because grips vary and objects do not.

## IX. The endurance test: where the tradition is firm, and where it is not

The seventh item asserts that real faith lasts, citing *For we are made partakers of Christ, if we hold the beginning of our confidence stedfast unto the end* (Hebrews 3:14) and *They went out from us, but they were not of us* (1 John 2:19). The fellowship should know which part of this is settled across the Christian traditions and which part has been contested for five centuries, because the article presents the whole of it as flat fact.

**Firm, and held by every branch:** final salvation belongs to those who endure. No tradition teaches that a man may abandon Christ finally and be saved anyway. *He that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved* (Matthew 24:13) is not a Protestant verse or a Catholic verse; it is in everyone’s Bible, and everyone means it.

**Contested, and honestly so:** what the failure to endure *proves*. The Reformed reading takes 1 John 2:19 as the interpretive key — those who leave were never regenerate, and the elect are preserved. The Arminian and Wesleyan reading takes the Hebrews warning passages as the key — *it is impossible… if they shall fall away, to renew them again unto repentance* (Hebrews 6:4-6) presupposes that the fall is a real possibility for real partakers. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold something closer to the second, with mortal sin severing the life of grace and the sacraments restoring it. These are not careless readings; each is doing justice to texts the others find difficult, and each has to work to accommodate the other’s proof texts.

I hold the Johannine reading, and I will say why in a sentence: 2:19 is John’s own explanation of the phenomenon under discussion, offered in the letter that also supplies the tests, and I take an author’s explanation of his own case as weightier than an inference from a warning addressed to a different situation. But I state it as a position among positions, and I note the thing that matters more than the adjudication: **the pastoral upshot is identical in every tradition.** Continue. Hold fast. No branch of the church has ever told a worried Christian that the answer to his worry is to stop. The warning passages and the preservation passages both terminate in the same imperative, which is why Paul can put both halves in consecutive sentences without embarrassment: *work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure* (Philippians 2:12-13). Work, because it is real work; and the working is His.

And on the other side of the same tension: *Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ* (Philippians 1:6). The beginning was not yours; neither is the finishing. That is the sentence to set beside the seventh item, and the article does not have it.

## X. The floor: what a changed heart is, ontologically

Now the level the article cannot reach, and where the fellowship’s own framework does its work.

The whole discussion of assurance presupposes an answer to a prior question: *what is it that changed, when a person is converted?* If salvation is only a change of legal status recorded elsewhere, then self-examination is the only access I have to it, and the article’s method is the only method available — inspect the behavior, infer the status. But that is not what the framework holds, and it is not what Scripture describes.

Reality, on the Conscious Point account, is constituted by conscious points sustained moment by moment within the divine consciousness. *In him we live, and move, and have our being* (Acts 17:28) is not a devotional flourish; it is an ontological description. And the corollary — developed in the June seed entry on borrowed power — is that **every creature acts on power that is lent to it, continuously, by the One it is acting toward or against.** *Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above* (John 19:11), said to Pilate at the moment of his maximum apparent authority.

Two consequences follow, and both bear directly on assurance.

**First, walking in the light is a description, not a metaphor.** Light, in John’s usage, is not a figure for good conduct. God *is* light, and there is no darkness in Him at all. To walk in the light is to move with the grain of what actually exists; to walk in darkness is to move against the grain of what actually exists, while being sustained in existence, instant by instant, by the very One whose nature is being contradicted. That is the real weight of sin, and it is heavier than rule-breaking: every sin is committed on borrowed power, using the Provider’s continuous provision to do the thing the Provider is not. The first item on the article’s list, properly grounded, is not a lifestyle question. It is a question about which way you are facing in a universe that has a direction.

**Second — and this is the pastoral payoff — a new love is not the kind of thing a creature can manufacture.** The creature has no aseity. Nothing in it is self-originating; that is the whole content of derivative existence. So when a man who did not love God finds that he now does — not perfectly, not steadily, but really — he is looking at something that did not come from him, because nothing comes from him. He did not decide to find Christ beautiful and then execute the decision. The affection arrived. *We love him, because he first loved us* (1 John 4:19).

This reorients the whole examination. The question the article puts to the reader is *have you performed sufficiently?* — a question about output, asked of a creature with no independent power to produce output, which is why it terminates in either despair or self-deception. The question the framework puts is different and answerable: *is there something here that I did not put here?* Do I grieve at offending God — an odd grief for an animal to have, and one I did not train into myself? Do I find, in the middle of a bad month with nothing on the scorecard, that I still want Him? Did I want that ten years ago?

And note what this does with the reader who is most tormented. The anxiety itself is data, and it points the opposite direction from the one the anxious reader assumes. A man does not lie awake grieving that he may not love God unless he already, at some level, does. The indifferent do not agonize about indifference. That is not a proof — I will not hand anyone a formula that makes anxiety itself a sacrament — but it is evidence, and it is evidence of exactly the kind the article’s method cannot see, because its method only reads outputs and this is a reading of direction.


---


## XI. To the two readers

Every piece of writing on this subject is read by two people, and what helps one damages the other. So let me address each.

**To the one whose heart condemns him.** You have read the seven, you have scored yourself, and you came up short. Hear the verse the article did not print: *if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things.* Your heart is a witness, not a judge, and it is a witness with a known bias — it knows your failures in detail and your Savior only by report. God knows both, exhaustively, including the things you have forgotten and the affections you have not noticed you have. His court outranks yours. Take your case there and stop retrying it in the lower one at three in the morning. And notice, please, what your distress is made of: it is grief at the possibility of being separated from Him. That grief is not the disease. Turn from what you know to be wrong, put your eyes back on the object — the Christ who came in the flesh and finished the work — and let the fruit be what it will be. It is His to grow.

**To the one who read the seven and was comfortable.** The Pharisee scored six. Six is available to anyone with discipline and a good memory for verses, and it is the score most compatible with never having been changed at all, because the six can all be performed and the seventh cannot. So take the one test that pride cannot counterfeit: not *do I love humanity*, but *do I love these people* — the ones in the fellowship who irritate me, the ones whose politics are wrong, the ones who need something from me this week. *By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another* (John 13:35). If that one is thin, no arithmetic on the other six will help you, and the honest thing to do is to say so to God tonight rather than to reason about it.

For myself, I will say plainly where I stand, because I do not think a man should write about assurance from behind a screen. My assurance does not rest on my performance on any list, and it had better not, because I have had months that would fail the audit. It rests on three things, in this order: on Christ, who came in the flesh and finished the work, and who has not moved; on the Spirit He gave, whose testimony alongside my spirit is not something I manufactured and not something I can argue myself out of; and, third and last, on the fruit — the fact that over decades the direction has held, that I still want Him on the days I have nothing to show, and that the wanting was never mine to begin with. That is the order. Reverse it and you get a religion of anxiety with Christian vocabulary. Keep it and the tests do exactly what John meant them to do: not to put you on trial, but to confirm what the Spirit is already telling you.

Seven tests, three tests, one question. And underneath the question, a Person who is greater than your heart and knows all things.
