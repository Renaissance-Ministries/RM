# 260810 - Denver Snuffer - Lecture 7

# An Academic Assessment of Denver Snuffer’s 500th-Anniversary Lecture 7


## Historical Accuracy and Biblical Consistency


**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 10, 2026**


[**Source:** ](https://restorationarchives.com/blog/index.php/2020/06/16/7th-address-to-christians/)Denver Snuffer Jr., seventh lecture of the eight-lecture series delivered on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of the Protestant Reformation, delivered under the assigned title “What is God up to Today?” Internal references (other speakers on Buddhism, the Native American tradition, and Messianic Judaism; a preceding Book of Mormon presentation by Gary Gibson; the Doctrine of Christ “read to us here today”) indicate delivery at a multi-speaker, interfaith-format event, a setting differing from the single-speaker venues of Lectures 1–3. The series and its supporting materials are published at https://www.christianreformation500years.info and at https://www.learnofchrist.org. Readers are encouraged to hear or read the lecture in full. **Method:** As in the assessments of Lectures 1–6, this essay is an academic evaluation only. Each substantive claim is extracted and assessed on two axes: (1) **historical accuracy** against the documented record and (2) **biblical consistency** against the text of Scripture (King James Version), with the text-reportage check retained. Personal anecdotes (the motorcycle ride, the schoolyard marbles, the porch, the songbird) are experiential material outside both axes and are not assessed, except where a checkable claim is embedded. Claims already assessed in earlier essays are cross-referenced rather than re-argued.


---


## I. Perfection and the Two Forces


### 1. The little-child saying


**Claim as delivered.** Christ said that except one becomes as a little child, one shall not enter the kingdom.


**Biblical assessment.** Substantially accurate as paraphrase: “Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 18:3; likewise Mark 10:15; Luke 18:17). The omission of “be converted, and” is noted only for completeness; the invitation to ponder the saying is unobjectionable.


### 2. Perfection as the experienced moment


**Claims as delivered.** Everyone has known moments of absolute perfection — moments wholly inhabited, in which nothing would be changed; perfection occurs absolutely in the moment; if we can conceive of or experience perfection even momentarily, then perfection exists, is real, and is attainable, even here, even by us. (Illustrated by the motorcycle at design speed, the dying Katsumoto’s “they are all perfect” in *The Last Samurai*, the marbles, the dogs, the porch, and the songbird.)


**Assessment.** The illustrations are anecdotal and outside the axes (the film scene is accurately recalled from the 2003 picture; the traffic-engineering distinction between design speed and posted limit is a genuine engineering concept; the incidental road identification — “Idaho five… from the Montana border to the Washington border” — does not match Idaho State Highway 5, a short route in the Panhandle’s interior, but nothing turns on it). Two assessable elements remain. (a) *The inference:* “if we can conceive of it… it exists… it is attainable” is, as logic, invalid in the general case — conceivability does not entail attainability (one can conceive the unattainable) — though as homiletics it functions as encouragement rather than demonstration. (b) *The definition:* Scripture does command and predicate attainable perfection — “Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect” (Matthew 5:48); “walk before me, and be thou perfect” (Genesis 17:1); Job “perfect and upright” (Job 1:1) — but the biblical term (Hebrew *tamim*, Greek *teleios*) denotes completeness, integrity, maturity — and in Matthew 5:48 specifically the completeness of indiscriminate love, the verse concluding the love-of-enemies paragraph (5:43–47; compare Colossians 3:14, “charity, which is the bond of perfectness,” and James 1:4). The lecture’s definition — perfection as the flawless, fully inhabited moment — is a contemplative-experiential sense adjacent to, but not identical with, the biblical usage; the moment-perfection may illustrate *teleios* but does not translate it. Recorded as a homiletic redefinition, consistent in spirit with the attainability texts, distinct in content from them.


### 3. The two opposing forces: love and fear


**Claims as delivered.** Two opposing forces cause everything in this creation to be and to exist; they are not good and evil but love and fear; everything generative comes from love; every vice and all negativity — anger, pride, envy, impatience, greed — has its root in fear.


**Biblical assessment.** The formulation has one genuine biblical anchor and several points of collision. The anchor: “There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment” (1 John 4:18), with the companion “God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind” (2 Timothy 1:7) — the opposition of love to servile fear is authentically Johannine and Pauline. The collisions: (a) as an account of vice’s root, Scripture is more various — sin springs from *desire* (“every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed… lust… bringeth forth sin,” James 1:14–15), from *pride* (Proverbs 16:18; 1 John 2:16, “the pride of life”), and from *the love of money* (1 Timothy 6:10, “the root of all evil” in the KJV’s own superlative) — the canon’s named roots are not reducible to fear without remainder; (b) the same canon commends a fear it calls the *beginning of wisdom* — “the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom” (Proverbs 9:10; Ecclesiastes 12:13; “fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell,” Matthew 10:28) — so “everything negative comes from fear” holds only by equivocating between servile dread and reverent fear, a distinction the lecture does not draw; and (c) the cosmological claim — that these two forces “cause everything there is to be and to exist” — is a dualism attested nowhere in Scripture, where creation has one cause (“all things were made by him,” John 1:3; Colossians 1:16). As history of ideas, the love/fear binary in this totalizing form is a characteristic formulation of modern therapeutic and New Thought literature (its best-known modern vehicle being *A Course in Miracles*‘ dictum that there are only two emotions, love and fear), not of the biblical or patristic corpus. **Finding:** the love-against-servile-fear opposition is biblical (1 John 4:18); the totalizing ontology and the fear-monism of vice are modern formulations without biblical attestation and in tension with the fear-of-the-LORD theme and James 1:14–15.

## II. Ephesians 1:10 and the Gathering of All Things


### 4. The exegesis of the gathering


**Claims as delivered.** Ephesians 1:10 — that in the dispensation of the fullness of times God might gather together in one all things in Christ — requires, before the Lord’s return, the gathering of every fragment of truth wherever preserved: truths lost to Christianity but preserved in Hinduism or Buddhism must be gathered home into Christ; like a fractured sculpture, the original’s unity is restored only when every piece is found and placed; the search must cross cultures, traditions, and religions.


**Biblical assessment.** The verse is quoted accurately, twice. The reading, however, substitutes an object the verse does not name. The text’s “all things” is immediately glossed by the verse itself: “**both which are in heaven, and which are on earth; even in him**” — the totality of created reality summed up under Christ’s headship (the verb *anakephalaiōsasthai*, to head up or sum up), the Ephesians parallel to “by him to reconcile **all things** unto himself… whether they be things in earth, or things in heaven” (Colossians 1:20) and to the exaltation “far above all principality, and power… and hath put all things under his feet” (Ephesians 1:21–22). The object of the gathering is the cosmos under its Head, and the agent is God in Christ; the verse does not speak of doctrinal fragments, and it assigns the summing-up to God’s dispensation rather than to a comparative-religion recovery project. The narrower principle the lecture needs — that truth exists outside the Christian tradition and is to be owned wherever found — has genuine biblical footing on *other* texts: Paul quoting the pagan poets with approval (“as certain also of your own poets have said,” Acts 17:28; Titus 1:12), the law written on Gentile hearts (Romans 2:14–15), and God’s witness among all nations (Acts 14:17); and it has a patristic pedigree (Justin Martyr’s doctrine of the *logos spermatikos*, the seed of the Word scattered among the Greeks, *Second Apology* 8, 13) and a Restoration pedigree (Joseph Smith’s documented maxim that Mormonism’s principle is to receive truth from whatever source). The principle is sound on its own supports; Ephesians 1:10 is not among them. **Finding:** verse accurately quoted, object and agent of the verse displaced; the truth-wherever-found principle biblically and historically supportable on other grounds.


### 5. The fragments dispersed into the East


**Claims as delivered.** Truths broken away from Christian awareness departed into the East and were retained by the Hindus and Buddhists; the highest ideals of Buddhism and Hinduism are present in the Gospel of Christ; Christianity lacks the meditative power found in Buddhism and Hinduism; one will not understand the sayings of Jesus as well without putting on Hindu or Buddhist eyes; reunifying all things in Christ will reunify the fractured Hindu and Buddhist worlds as well.


**Historical assessment.** Two claims must be separated. (a) *The dispersal history* — that these traditions retain pieces “that left Christian awareness” — is untenable as chronology in the form stated: Hinduism’s Vedic stratum (second millennium BC) and Buddhism (fifth century BC) are documented as prior to and independent of Christianity, so nothing can have departed *from* Christian awareness *into* them. If the intended claim is the series’ deeper frame — fragments of the primeval religion of the first fathers dispersed among the nations (the *prisca theologia* thesis, with its Restoration form assessed at Lecture 4 §9 and Lecture 5 §10) — it is a speculative genealogy without documentary attestation: the comparative-religion record shows independent development with occasional contact, not fragmentation from a recoverable original. (b) *The deficiency claim* — Christianity “lacks the meditative power” of the Eastern traditions — is contradicted by the documented Christian contemplative corpus: the desert fathers and *Apophthegmata*, the hesychast tradition and the *Philokalia*, Benedictine *lectio divina*, the Victorines, *The Cloud of Unknowing*, the Carmelite doctors (Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross), and the continuous Eastern Orthodox practice of the Jesus Prayer. A defensible statement is that much of Western Protestant practice has neglected its own contemplative inheritance — a common observation — but “Christianity lacks” it is falsified by the tradition’s own libraries. The milder claims (high ideals shared; reading with other eyes can illuminate) are unobjectionable as hermeneutical counsel and consistent with the Acts 17 pattern of building from the hearer’s frame. The Christ-centered terminus of the gathering (all traditions gathered *into Christ*) keeps the frame consistent with John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 rather than contrary to them; that consistency is recorded.

## III. Claims Concerning Church History


### 6. Twelve apostles, twelve versions, no unified message


**Claims as delivered.** Christ sent twelve messengers — apostles meaning simply someone with a message — and they brought twelve different versions of what they learned from Jesus, with no attempt at a unified message; the earliest studies of the faith focus on the varieties keyed to particular apostles; Paul then teaches yet another view.


**Assessment.** The lexical gloss is serviceable (*apostolos*, one sent with commission; “someone with a message” is close enough). The historical claim reprises the apostolic-communities heuristic assessed at Lecture 2 §13 (a contested scholarly construct presented as settled), and its strong form — “no attempt at having a unified message” — is contradicted by the New Testament’s own record of exactly such attempts, successful ones: the church continued “in **the apostles’ doctrine**” in the singular (Acts 2:42); Paul went up to Jerusalem “and communicated unto them that gospel which I preach… **lest by any means I should run, or had run, in vain**” (Galatians 2:2), receiving “the right hands of fellowship” on a common gospel (2:7–9); the Jerusalem council issued one binding decree across provinces (Acts 15:22–29); Paul’s own formula of unity — “whether it were I or they, **so we preach**, and so ye believed” (1 Corinthians 15:11) — asserts the identity of the apostolic message; and where party-versions by apostolic names *did* appear, the apostle on the spot condemned the phenomenon rather than sponsoring it — “every one of you saith, I am of Paul; and I of Apollos; and I of Cephas… Is Christ divided?” (1 Corinthians 1:12–13). Real diversity of emphasis among the apostolic writings is a datum; “twelve different versions… no attempt at unity” is contradicted by Acts 15, Galatians 2, and 1 Corinthians 1 and 15.


### 7. Third-century Christians killing Christians; Constantine’s motive; the “house arrest” at Nicaea


**Claims as delivered.** By the third century Christians are killing Christians over Christianity; Constantine, seeking imperial unity, chose Christianity as the new state religion, then discovered Christians killing Christians within his empire; he convened the bishops at Nicaea “under house arrest” to force a unified statement — an ugly coercive moment later spun as the first great ecumenical council; the emperor did not care what they agreed on, only that they agree; the result bears the hallmarks of compromise.


**Historical assessment.** The paragraph mixes one documented kernel with several claims contrary to the record. (a) *Third-century intra-Christian killing:* this repeats, and antedates further, the claim assessed at Lecture 2 §16: in the third century Christians possessed no coercive power and were themselves under intermittent imperial persecution (Decius, Valerian, Diocletian’s era opening in 303); the first execution of a Christian for heresy at Christian instigation is Priscillian (385), and intra-Christian mob violence (Donatist, Arian–Nicene) belongs to the *post*-Constantinian fourth century. As pre-Nicene description the claim is anachronistic. (b) *State religion:* the Theodosius correction of Lecture 3 §6 applies — Constantine legalized and favored; establishment came in 380. (c) *“Under house arrest”:* unattested and contrary to the sources. Constantine summoned the bishops with the imperial post placed at their disposal and hosted them at public expense; the attendees included confessors bearing the scars of the recent persecution; the council deliberated for weeks with open dispute; and the coercion that did occur came *after* the decision — the exile of Arius and the two non-subscribing bishops. No ancient source describes the bishops as detained to force agreement; the phrase is rhetorical invention. (d) *The documented kernel:* Constantine’s motive was indeed imperial concord, and his theological indifference at the outset is documented in his own letter to Alexander and Arius calling the dispute a trifling question unworthy of division (preserved in Eusebius, *Life of Constantine* 2.64–72); “he just wanted an agreement” has that much genuine support. (e) *Compromise verdict:* the council’s actual output ran opposite to compromise on the contested point — *homoousios* was the maximal anti-Arian term, adopted over the accommodating alternatives; whatever else Nicaea was, its creed was not a split-the-difference formula (per the Lecture 2 §17 findings). **Finding:** motive kernel documented; the killing chronology, the state-religion attribution, the house-arrest mechanism, and the compromise characterization are each contrary to, or unsupported by, the record.


### 8. From Roman Empire to “the Holy Roman Empire, which is Catholicism”


**Claim as delivered.** The state religion of Rome evolved over time from the Roman Empire, Catholic meaning universal, to the Holy Roman Empire, which is Catholicism; the Holy Roman Empire, in the form of the Catholic church, exercised the abuses expected of dictatorial power.


**Historical assessment.** The etymology is right (*catholicus*, universal) and the abuse characterization of the medieval church has been granted at Lecture 2 §6–7. The institutional identification is wrong: the **Holy Roman Empire** — the polity inaugurated with Charlemagne’s coronation (800) and constituted under Otto I (962) — was a Germanic-centered *political* empire, juridically and institutionally distinct from the Catholic Church, and for long stretches the church’s principal *rival* (the Investiture Controversy, Canossa 1077, the wars of empire and papacy through the Hohenstaufen era). “The Holy Roman Empire, which is Catholicism” conflates two frequently opposed institutions into one; the accurate statement is that Latin Christendom comprised both a universal church centered at Rome and a Holy Roman Empire with which it contended.


### 9. Luther’s protection and the reformers who “met and spoke with one another”


**Claims as delivered.** Luther, conscience-pricked and believing the New Testament, saw the gap between it and the church; he was spared burning because a political leader saw advantage in protecting him, and so was allowed to post his disagreement and found a new Christianity; immediately upon Lutheranism’s emergence came — in the same generation, “these people met and spoke with one another” — John Knox, John Calvin, Zwingli; Protestantism fractured immediately and repeatedly.


**Historical assessment.** The protection claim is accurate: Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony, shielded Luther (the safe-conduct to Worms, the staged “kidnapping” to the Wartburg, 1521), and political advantage was part of the calculus; the contrast with Hus’s fate is the standard one (the sequence is compressed — the posting of 1517 preceded the protection crisis of 1520–21 — but not falsified). The fragmentation claim is accurate (the Zurich radicals from 1525; the Lutheran–Zwinglian breach). The met-and-spoke claim is only partially true: **Luther and Zwingli** met once, at the Marburg Colloquy (1529), and famously failed to agree on the Supper; **Calvin never met Luther** (their contact was indirect, through Melanchthon); **Knox never met Luther or Zwingli** (Knox, born c. 1513, came to the Continent after Luther’s death in 1546 and worked with **Calvin** in Geneva in the 1550s). As a same-conversation cohort the four never existed; the pairings that met are Luther–Zwingli and Calvin–Knox. The generational span (Luther b. 1483; Knox b. c. 1513) also stretches “the same generation.” The substantive point — Protestantism’s immediate internal division — survives on the corrected data.

## IV. The Two Paths to Unity


### 10. Coercive unity versus persuaded unity


**Claims as delivered.** If fighting is the evil to be avoided, there are two approaches: coercion, exclusion, and punishment (Constantine, the popes, and some centrally controlled organizations today), or a benign minimalism — the Doctrine of Christ, belief in Christ, His Father, the Holy Ghost, baptism in faith, and liberty of thought under the Spirit’s animation — in which unity comes by persuasion, welcome, and patience with those who decline for a season.


**Assessment.** The persuaded-unity principle is biblically consistent and has been so recorded across the series: “knowing therefore the terror of the Lord, we **persuade** men” (2 Corinthians 5:11); “in meekness instructing those that oppose themselves” (2 Timothy 2:24–25); “not… as being lords over God’s heritage” (1 Peter 5:3); no coercion text exists in the apostolic corpus, and the lecture’s contrast between imperial compulsion and gospel persuasion is historically fair as a contrast of methods. The minimal-creed content is the 3 Nephi 11 “Doctrine of Christ” carried through the series; the Lecture 1 §18 findings on its exclusivity clause govern without repetition, with the standing observation that this lecture’s own additions (§§11–12 below) again exceed the minimum it commends. The dichotomy itself (only coercion or minimalism) is rhetorically neat but historically incomplete — confessional churches practicing voluntary, non-coercive adherence to *maximal* creeds constitute a third, well-populated category — noted as a false dilemma in form, though the preference expressed is not thereby inaccurate.

## V. The Testimony of Jesus and the Visitation Claims


### 11. The D&C 76 description and quotation


**Claims as delivered.** Joseph Smith had a revelation dictated from beyond the veil, recorded by a scribe, read back, and confirmed by Joseph and Sidney Rigdon, who shared the vision; it speaks of those disappointed at the end of this life because “they received not the gospel, neither the testimony of Jesus, neither the prophets, neither the everlasting covenants.”


**Assessment.** Accurate as reportage and identified as to source: the Vision (Doctrine and Covenants 76, February 16, 1832, Hiram, Ohio) was received jointly by Smith and Rigdon, and the alternating-dictation procedure described matches the surviving participant account (Philo Dibble’s recollection). The quotation is D&C 76:101 as it stands. The attribution pattern is recorded to the lecture’s credit, consistent with the naming trend documented in Lectures 5 and 6.


### 12. The redefinition of “the testimony of Jesus”; salvation based upon it


**Claims as delivered.** The testimony of Jesus is not one’s testimony about Him but His testimony *to* you — His confirmation that you have part in His kingdom, His promise of eternal life; the Book of Mormon is filled with accounts of people who had that experience; it was once an expected and normal part of the Christian experience, now denounced by many denominations; “Christian salvation is based upon the testimony of Jesus to you of your salvation”; whenever one lives, if true and faithful, one will have that experience.


**Biblical assessment.** The lexical claim has a genuine foothold: in the Revelation, “the testimony of Jesus” (Revelation 1:2, 9; 12:17; 19:10, “the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy”) is a genitive that scholarship reads both ways — the testimony *borne by* Jesus (subjective) or *about* Him (objective) — and the subjective reading, on which believers “have” and “hold” Jesus’ own testimony, is a live exegetical option; the lecture’s reading is therefore not linguistically illegitimate. The doctrinal superstructure, however, exceeds and at one point inverts the New Testament’s stated economy. That Christ manifests Himself to the faithful is biblically affirmed as promise — “he that loveth me shall be loved of my Father, and I will love him, and **will manifest myself to him**… we will come unto him, and make our abode with him” (John 14:21, 23) — and instanced (Stephen, §13 below; Paul; John on Patmos). But the proposition that Christian *salvation is based upon* a personal manifestation-testimony from Jesus to the individual stands against three explicit texts: the beatitude reserved precisely for faith without sight — “blessed are they that **have not seen, and yet have believed**” (John 20:29), pronounced at the moment sight-based faith was granted to Thomas; Peter’s description of the normal Christian condition — “whom **having not seen**, ye love; in whom, though **now ye see him not**, yet believing, ye rejoice with joy unspeakable” (1 Peter 1:8), with salvation received in that unseeing believing (1:9); and the stated organ of assurance — “**the Spirit itself** beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God” (Romans 8:16; likewise 1 John 5:10–13, where the assurance of eternal life is written “unto you that believe”). The New Testament’s stated basis of salvation is grace through faith confessing the risen Lord (Ephesians 2:8; Romans 10:9); its stated instrument of assurance is the Spirit’s witness; a christophany is a promise and a possibility within that economy, not its basis. The doctrine as delivered — the lecturer’s signature Second Comforter teaching — makes the exceptional instance the criterial norm. **Finding:** manifestation-as-promise, biblical (John 14:21–23); salvation-based-upon-personal-visitation, contrary to John 20:29, 1 Peter 1:8–9, and Romans 8:16 as the stated economy. The Book of Mormon reportage (theophany narratives throughout) is accurate as reportage.


### 13. Stephen


**Claims as delivered.** Stephen, while being stoned, had the heavens opened, saw Christ, forgave his killers, and parted this life rejoicing.


**Biblical assessment.** Accurate to Acts 7:55–60 in every checkable element — “I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing on the right hand of God” (7:56); “Lord, lay not this sin to their charge” (7:60) — with “rejoicing” an inference from the scene (the text says “he fell asleep”) and a fair one. It is noted that Stephen’s vision came unsought at martyrdom as the Spirit’s gift (7:55, “being full of the Holy Ghost”), which fits manifestation-as-promise (§12) rather than manifestation-as-salvation’s-basis; Stephen’s standing with Christ is presented as the cause of the vision, not its effect.


### 14. Alvin Smith and the vision of the celestial kingdom


**Claims as delivered.** Joseph Smith’s older brother Alvin, dying in Joseph’s youth, said in his last moments that angels were in the room, talking with him, and he was conversing with them; years later Joseph saw Alvin in the celestial kingdom, wondered how, since Alvin died before the gospel was fully restored, and was told that anyone who *would have* accepted the truth, the gospel, the testimony of Jesus, the prophets, and the everlasting covenants will be saved though they died when it was unavailable.


**Historical assessment against the sources.** Checked against the family record. (a) *The deathbed detail* has a documented kernel and an amplified retelling: Joseph Smith’s own later reminiscence states of Alvin that “when he died **the angel of the Lord visited him** in his last moments” (History of the Church 5:126–27, 1842); Lucy Mack Smith’s detailed deathbed narrative records Alvin calling each family member for counsel (finish the house; obtain the record; care for the parents) without mentioning angels. The lecture’s version — Alvin *saying* there were angels (plural) in the room and conversing with them — attributes to Alvin’s own dying words, in the plural and in dialogue, what the source states as Joseph’s later report of a singular visitation. Kernel documented; details amplified beyond the sources. (b) *The vision* is accurately retold: the January 1836 vision (Doctrine and Covenants 137) records Joseph marveling at seeing Alvin in the celestial kingdom, “seeing that he had departed this life before the Lord had set his hand to gather Israel” (137:6), answered by the principle that “all who have died without a knowledge of this gospel, **who would have received it** if they had been permitted to tarry, shall be heirs of the celestial kingdom” (137:7). The lecture’s list (“the truth, the gospel, the testimony of Jesus, the prophets and the everlasting covenants”) imports the D&C 76:101 catalogue (§11) into the D&C 137 answer — a conflation of two revelations’ wording, immaterial to the substance. Biblically, the would-have-received principle is an inference beyond the text of Scripture but consonant with the judgment-according-to-light texts (Romans 2:12; Luke 12:47–48; John 15:22); it is recorded as Restoration doctrine with those biblical adjacencies, attributed by the lecture to its Restoration source.


### 15. St. Francis


**Claims as delivered.** Francis believed and practiced the Sermon on the Mount at a time when Catholicism was the only Christianity; he asked the pope to found an order under a vow of poverty; the pope initially rebuffed him — no one can do that; find people who will, and return; Francis was known for giving away every coat he was given; he found twelve men, and the pope granted the Franciscan order; in his last days Francis said angels were coming and ministering to him.


**Historical assessment.** Substantially accurate to the sources as hagiographic tradition. Francis sought approval from Innocent III (1209/10); the sources record initial papal hesitation before oral approval of the primitive rule (the tradition adding the pope’s dream of the collapsing Lateran upheld by the poor man); the founding company is remembered as Francis and eleven companions — twelve in all, and the lecture’s “found 12 men” is within the tradition’s own round figure; the serial coat-giving is a fixture of the early lives (Thomas of Celano; Bonaventure); and the deathbed accounts (1226) belong to a tradition rich in angelic ministration around Francis (the earlier seraph vision at La Verna, 1224, being the most famous). The assessment notes the genre: these are hagiographic sources, reported here as what the tradition records, which is how the lecture uses them. The theological use — a faithful man within a corrupted-era church receiving heavenly ministration — is consistent with the remnant pattern this series of assessments has repeatedly set against the total-apostasy thesis (Lecture 1 §15): Francis inside Catholicism functioning as the lecture’s own counterexample to “the only brand of Christianity was corrupt,” a tension recorded without adjudication.


### 16. “Shall not taste death, for it shall be sweet unto them”


**Claim as delivered.** There is a revelation saying some will not taste death because it shall be sweet unto them; the authentic Christian’s final moments, caught up in the testimony of Jesus, are joyful.


**Assessment.** The source is Doctrine and Covenants 42:46 (“those that die in me shall not taste of death, for it shall be sweet unto them”), identified generically as “a revelation” — semi-attributed. The wording has a Johannine antecedent (“If a man keep my saying, he shall never taste of death,” John 8:51–52, there of eternal life rather than of dying’s sweetness), and the comfort claim is consistent with Psalm 116:15 and Philippians 1:21–23. No inaccuracy; the criterial caveat of §12 applies to the framing.

## VI. Summary of Findings


Assessed on historical accuracy: accurate or substantially accurate — the *Last Samurai* recollection and the design-speed concept (§2, incidental); the Constantine-motive kernel from the documented letter (§7d); Frederick the Wise’s protection of Luther and the immediacy of Protestant fragmentation (§9); the Marburg and Geneva pairings as far as they go (§9); the D&C 76 provenance and quotation (§11); the Acts 7 reportage (§13); the D&C 137 reportage and the HC 5:126–27 kernel (§14); the Franciscan tradition as tradition (§15); and the D&C 42:46 content (§16). Inaccurate or materially overstated: the third-century Christians-killing-Christians chronology (§7a, carrying Lecture 2 §16); Constantine as maker of the state religion (§7b, carrying Lecture 3 §6); the “house arrest” of the bishops at Nicaea, unattested and contrary to the sources (§7c); the compromise characterization of the Nicene formula (§7e, per Lecture 2 §17); “the Holy Roman Empire, which is Catholicism,” conflating two frequently opposed institutions (§8); the four reformers as a mutually acquainted cohort — Luther never met Calvin or Knox (§9); “no attempt at a unified message” among the apostles, against Acts 2:42, Acts 15, Galatians 2:2–9, and 1 Corinthians 1:12–13 and 15:11 (§6); the dispersal-into-the-East history as chronology (§5a); the “Christianity lacks the meditative power” claim against the documented contemplative corpus (§5b); and the amplification of the Alvin deathbed detail beyond its sources (§14a).
