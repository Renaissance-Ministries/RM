## 1. CAD file recovery - solved

### The problem

Charlie couldn't open his own drawings: the Xenon license had lapsed and
renewal costs $500. He sent Leonard a .xe file, which Leonard read as a
compressed .exe and flagged as suspect.

### What the format actually is

Ashlar-Vellum Xenon. The native format is .xe - a genuinely rare format
outside a niche community of industrial designers and jewelers. Xenon users
almost always convert to STEP, IGES, or DWG before sending anything to a
supplier, which is why Leonard had never seen one.

### The solution - and the $500 stays in the bank

Cobalt Share, a free viewer utility from Ashlar-Vellum, opens .xe files and
allows viewing, measuring, printing, and export to standard formats
including DWG. Isak downloaded it and has the drawings open.

No license renewal required. Charlie should not spend the $500.

Two quirks worth passing along to anyone else who uses it: the zoom-in
button works but zoom-out is buried in the menu, and PDF export saves either
blank or wildly zoomed on a single component. Isak verified the DWG export
works by uploading it to a browser-based viewer.

## 3. Drop shipping - the finding that changes the DME channel

### What Thomas proposed

Rather than shipping to the DME who then ships to the patient, Ideomotion
ships directly on the dealer's behalf - effectively becoming the dealer's
fulfillment center.

### It works, and it's standard

This is drop shipping, it's common in the DME industry, and Rifton already
does it. The DME dealer handles the paperwork and billing; the manufacturer
ships directly to the patient or facility.

The rules:

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Supplier of record | The DME remains the supplier of record - they own the claim, the prior authorization, and the patient relationship, regardless of who physically ships |
| Records | The DME must maintain records showing they arranged and authorized the delivery |
| Labeling | The shipping label and packing slip must carry the DME supplier's name and contact information, not just the manufacturer's - CMS wants the patient to know who to call for service |
| Proof of delivery | Goes back to the DME. They need it to bill |

### Why this matters more than it first appears

On 10 September, Isak identified the strongest reason a DME dealer would
refuse to carry the Traveler: they won't stock an unproven product with no
code and no track record, because that's inventory risk stacked on top of
claim risk.

Drop shipping removes the inventory risk entirely. The dealer carries no
stock, ties up no capital, and holds nothing they might not sell. That isn't
an answer to the objection - it's the objection disappearing.

Isak's framing is the one to use in the sales approach: "We'll be your
warehouse for this product. You don't have to carry it."

### Three secondary benefits

1. Eliminates double shipping. Ideomotion -> DME -> patient becomes
Ideomotion -> patient. Given that Medi-Cal does not cover shipping (Isak
knows this for certain), removing a leg of freight is real money.
2. Negotiating room on the dealer percentage. Providing fulfillment is
worth something. It may not move a dealer off 30%, but it should at minimum
cover the shipping cost.
3. It fits the operation Thomas wants to build anyway. A dedicated
storage and shipping room - possibly an extension of Charlie's existing
setup - with manufacturing at Victory Circle or overseas, inventory flowing
back to the warehouse, and shipping going out on the DME's instruction.

### The operating model this implies

Ideomotion creates demand - occupational therapists, physical therapists,
physician assistants, and orthopedists learn the product and want to
prescribe it. The patient calls the DME dealer. The dealer calls Ideomotion.
Ideomotion ships, labeled as the dealer.

The dealer's role narrows to billing and the claim. Ideomotion holds the
product, the clinical relationship, and the demand generation - which is the
right division, because those are the parts a dealer can't do and Ideomotion
can.

Logged for the pricing conversation: fulfillment is a concession worth
trading for. Don't give it away free when the dealer percentage comes up.

## 4. Reimbursement research - the plan, with one correction

### What Isak found

PDAC - Pricing, Data Analysis and Coding. This is the right body and it's a
good find. PDAC is managed by Palmetto GBA under contract to CMS, and its
job is guiding manufacturers and suppliers on proper HCPCS use through
product reviews and decisions. Website: dmepdac.com.

Noridian Healthcare Solutions - the DME MAC that processes California DME
claims. They have a Provider Outreach and Education department specifically
for answering new-supplier questions, and they run webinars and training
sessions. They're in North Dakota, so phone beats a visit.

### The correction worth making

The call treated "what will they pay us" as one question. It's three,
answered by three different bodies, and mixing them up wastes calls:

| Question | Who answers it |
|---|---|
| What code does our product fall under? | PDAC - via informal guidance or a formal Coding Verification Review |
| What does that code pay? | The DMEPOS fee schedule - available free online through DMECS |
| Will it be covered for this patient? | Noridian (the DME MAC), via the LCD and coverage policy |

The fastest step is one nobody has taken: DMECS. PDAC maintains the Durable
Medical Equipment Coding System, an online application with HCPCS coding
assistance and national pricing information available 24 hours a day,
including a fee schedule lookup you can print or download. That will answer
"what does this pay" for any established code today, without waiting for
anyone to pick up a phone.

### An important trap to avoid

PDAC's own guidance is explicit: price and fees are not part of correct
coding. HCPCS codes describe the product, not the price, and selecting a
code based on the fee schedule may result in an incorrect coding
determination.

Given how the question has been framed in these calls - what will they pay
us - this is worth naming. Look up the fee schedule to understand the
economics, but the code has to be chosen on what the device actually is.
Manufacturers have no authority to assign their own codes, and unofficial
coding recommendations carry no official status; in a claim review they can
produce a denial.

The official route is a Coding Verification Review, which produces a code
verification review letter stating the correct code for Medicare billing.
Informal PDAC Contact Center guidance is useful for orientation but is
explicitly not a formal determination.

### The E1399 fallback, restated

If no established code fits, the product bills under E1399, miscellaneous
DME - which means each claim is individually reviewed by the DME MAC, with
no fee schedule amount and unpredictable timing. This is exactly the route
Rifton runs, and it's why they publish sample Letters of Medical Necessity
for every product.

### Thomas's parallel route - and it may be the better one

Rather than working through the bureaucracy, call the DME dealers and ask
them directly.

The framing Thomas proposed is the right one, because a dealer is an ally
rather than a gatekeeper - they make money when they have products to sell:

"I want to sell through you. What can I get when I sell my product through
you with a physician's recommendation? What do companies sell to you for?
What's MSRP versus the retail price? Is there a competitive bidding
requirement? Do they average the industry and pay the lowest? Is it 75%?"

Start with local dealers and widen out. Los Angeles is the density play - if
the answer isn't findable there, it isn't findable.
