# PART 2 - POSITIONS AND ARGUMENTS

## Thomas's argument for the lift/assist framing

The product's real value is standing, not training. From observing a facility: residents sit all day, get strapped into a lift for toileting, and return to the chair. No training happens.

His view of the sales problem: nobody buys a device to learn to walk, because nobody in a wheelchair believes they can walk. A doctor proposing it to a patient gets laughed at. What makes Charlie's demonstrations work is faith and physical proof, and that does not scale into a catalog.

His preferred framing: an assist device that helps a person stand, get to the toilet, get to the living room, and stand rather than sit all day - with training as a secondary benefit.

## Joelle's argument for the walker/training framing

She sees the lifting function as secondary to training. Her point about distinctiveness: selling on caregiver injury and workers' comp is a market where competitors already operate and Ideomotion has nothing that sets it apart. The neuroplasticity and "what is possible" story is the revolutionary part and the long game.

She also raised the practical constraint: going the lift route means facility inspections and more regulatory process, and it would tie Charlie's hands on the thing that is his whole mission.

Her analogy: this is the pharmaceutical-vs-supplement problem. You know what it does, but you are not allowed to advertise it.

## Isak's position

ITJ, on four grounds: buyers will see a walker; it supports prescription as a walker rather than as a transfer lift; the company's history and Charlie's practice point that way; and if Ideomotion were actually building a transfer device it would look completely different - bigger, a crane, a Hoyer.

He also named the constraint on the other side: going lift-only means biting your tongue on gait training in every demo and video.

## Thomas's exoskeleton concern

Raised via Isak: an exoskeleton company spent roughly ten years getting a category created because the technology was genuinely new. Both agreed Ideomotion cannot absorb that timeline and should fit an existing category rather than create one.

# PART 3 - CORRECTIONS AND FLAGS

**These need attention before the FDA call.**

## 3.1 - The video plan is the highest-risk item in the meeting

**What was proposed:** Joelle suggested showing transfer capability in website videos without putting it in the written copy. Isak agreed and extended it - a video shows only a slice, so it could show getting up or sitting down without stating a transfer claim.

**Why this does not work:** under 21 CFR 801.4, intended use is established by objective intent including labeling, advertising, and oral statements by sales representatives. **Video is advertising.** A video demonstrating a bed-to-chair transfer is evidence of intended use as surely as printed text - arguably stronger, because it is demonstrative rather than descriptive.

Thomas named the same risk from the sales side: saying "it lifts them off the chair" in a sales conversation is a statement of intended use. He is right, and it applies to filmed demonstrations equally.

**This is the practice that most threatens the ITJ position**, and the meeting adopted it as a plan rather than rejecting it. It needs to be raised on the FDA call and settled before any video is produced.

## 3.2 - A consultant cannot authorize claims

Thomas proposed listing everything the company wants to say, having a consultant sign off, and using that sign-off as a defense - "be our advocate."

A consultant memo is useful evidence of diligence. It is **not** authorization. No consultant can permit claims that exceed the generic type of the declared product code, and a signed memo does not bind FDA. The instrument that produces an official answer is a **513(g) request**, not a consultant opinion.

The consultant is still worth hiring, for labeling, Rx-vs-OTC, quality system scope, and complaint handling. Just not as a shield.

## 3.3 - "Combination product" means something specific

Thomas used the Certs "two mints in one" framing and the term "combination product." In FDA usage, a combination product is a drug-device, biologic-device, or drug-biologic combination. It does not mean a device with two functions.

The concept Thomas is reaching for is real, but the term will cause confusion on the FDA call. Better phrasing: a device that provides powered support to achieve standing and then supports ambulation.

## 3.4 - Class I exempt does not mean no inspection

Isak said that going the ITJ route means "nobody's going to be reviewing it or inspecting it."

FDA can inspect any registered establishment regardless of device class. Class I exempt removes premarket review, not FDA's inspection authority. Complaint handling and MDR reporting under 21 CFR 803 also still apply.

The inspection burden is genuinely lower on the ITJ path, but it is not zero, and planning on "nobody will look" is not a position that survives an incident.

## 3.5 - Prescribing for the caregiver does not work

Thomas relayed Omar Stephen's concept: prescribe the device for the caregiver rather than the patient, on workers' comp injury-avoidance grounds ($60,000 to $100,000 per injury).

The institutional sale to facilities and staffing agencies on injury-avoidance grounds is legitimate and is the Biodex model. **The prescription part is not.** DME benefits attach to a patient with a documented medical need. A device prescribed for a caregiver's benefit and billed against a patient's coverage is a false claim.

Keep the two separate: sell to facilities on cost avoidance; prescribe to patients on medical necessity. Never merge them in one argument.

## 3.7 - Joelle's unanswered question

She asked whether the constraint runs both ways: if classified as a lift, are you barred from calling it a trainer, the way ITJ bars transfer claims?

**Yes, and worse.** ITJ costs you transfer language the company already decided it does not want. FSA would cost you walking, ambulation, gait, and mobility language - which is the entire product story, the LiftWalker trademark, Charlie's mission, and the clinic channel.

Both codes constrain. Only one constrains in a direction the company was already going. **This asymmetry is the strongest argument for ITJ and should be stated on the FDA call.**
