# 261106 - Democrats Governing Texas

# Will They Govern for the Texans Who Voted Against Them?


### Weighing the likelihood that the open letter to Gina Hinojosa and James Talarico will be honored


*Dr. Thomas | 06 November 2026*


---


## The letter and the question under it


A friend in Texas posted an open letter after Tuesday’s election to Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa and Senator-elect James Talarico. The letter is gracious and firm. It reminds Hinojosa that she is inheriting a state with an extraordinary economic foundation: no individual income tax, national leadership in energy, and a long run of business relocations and population growth. It asks Talarico to reach across party lines, to judge legislation on its merits rather than by which party wrote it, and to demand evidence before joining partisan investigations and impeachment fights. It closes with a challenge: *we are watching, save this post, and let’s revisit it in a year and again in four.*


On its face, the letter asks a narrow question: will these two officials keep their promises? Under it sits a larger one. Can a citizen reasonably expect a party that has just won power to govern with real regard for the people who voted against it? Or is that expectation naive, the polite request of the losing side to a winner who owes it nothing?


My thesis is this. **The letter will be honored only in part, and selectively.** Where the Democrats’ convictions and the conservative voter’s interests overlap, as on household costs, data-center abuses, and property-tax relief, cooperation is likely. Part of the reason is that the arithmetic of Texas government now forces it. Where the Democrats’ convictions are the point of their victory, as on school vouchers, Medicaid expansion, the reversal of Republican legislation, and the investigative pursuit of the Trump administration, the letter’s request will not be honored, and the winners will not think they owe it. Expecting otherwise is naive. But the letter is not naive in its *method*. A public, dated, specific benchmark that the writer promises to revisit is exactly the right tool for a citizen in the minority.


## What changed on Tuesday


The scale of the change matters to the analysis, so it is worth stating plainly.


- Hinojosa defeated Greg Abbott by roughly seven points and becomes the first Democratic governor of Texas since Ann Richards left office in 1995.

- Talarico defeated Ken Paxton with about 55 percent of the vote. He is the first Democrat Texas has sent to the U.S. Senate since 1988.

- Democrats won every statewide executive office on the ballot, including lieutenant governor, where Vikki Goodwin unseated Dan Patrick.

- Democrats flipped the Texas House. **Republicans kept the Texas Senate, 19 to 12.**

- Nationally, Democrats won both chambers of Congress, with a House majority projected around 255 seats and a Senate majority of roughly 54 to 56.


That last pair of facts is the key to the whole question. In Austin, Democrats hold the governor’s office, the lieutenant governor’s gavel, and the House, but not the Senate. In Washington, Democrats hold both chambers, and the President is a Republican. These are two different settings, and the letter’s request will fare differently in each.


## Austin: divided government forces some of what the letter asks


A Texas governor cannot pass a law. Repealing the voucher program, redirecting business subsidies to property-tax relief, and expanding Medicaid all require a bill through both chambers. A Republican Senate majority of 19 can block all of it, and Senate Republicans are already reported to be considering rules that would limit Lieutenant Governor Goodwin’s power over which bills advance.


So Hinojosa’s choice is not between imposing her agenda and sharing power. It is between dealing and getting nothing. That pressure points toward the letter’s request, at least on economic questions:


- **Data centers.** Lawmakers across a broad swath of both parties have already backed stripping data-center tax breaks and setting rules on where they can be built. This is the easiest early win, and it will be bipartisan because it already is.

- **Property taxes.** Homestead tax relief has drawn bipartisan support in past sessions and has passed with voters. Hinojosa has promised to cut residential property taxes by ending what she calls corporate welfare. A Republican Senate will want relief too, though by different means. A negotiated property-tax bill is plausible.

- **The governing posture she has announced.** At her first news conference she called for a “bipartisan” administration. She put on her transition team Ernest Bailes, a Republican who lost his House seat in 2024 after Abbott backed his primary opponent over vouchers. She has called her own program “postpartisan” and has said she will spend her energy on an agenda most Texans share rather than on fights she knows she cannot win.


On the “protect what works” half of the letter, one guardrail is built into the foundation. Since 2019 the Texas Constitution has barred a personal income tax, and removing that bar takes a two-thirds vote of the Legislature and a statewide vote of the people. Abbott accused Hinojosa of wanting an income tax. Whatever her private views, she cannot deliver one, and nothing she has proposed requires one.


**Where the letter will not be honored in Austin.** On education, the winners mean to undo, not to conserve. Hinojosa has promised to fire Education Commissioner Mike Morath on her first day, end state takeovers of local districts, end the STAAR test, and repeal what she calls the “voucher scam.” She cannot repeal the voucher law alone. She can veto future voucher legislation and can use the line-item veto to strip education-savings-account funding from the budget. She has also promised a one-time $1,500 payment to every household from the state’s Rainy Day Fund, and supporters expect pressure from her party’s progressive wing to revisit cultural issues she set aside during the campaign.


This is where the letter’s request and the winner’s mandate collide. For a parent who uses a voucher, or a taxpayer who thinks the Rainy Day Fund should stay a rainy-day fund, “protect what works” means leave this alone. For Hinojosa, the voucher program is one of the things that most needs fixing, and she won on that promise. No amount of goodwill dissolves that conflict. It will be settled by votes, vetoes, and the Senate’s leverage.


## Washington: unified control removes the pressure


Talarico’s setting is different. His party will hold both chambers. That removes the structural pressure that Austin puts on Hinojosa, and leaves only his personal disposition and his votes.


His disposition, as he presents it, is close to what the letter asks. He ran on what he calls a “politics of love.” The day after the election he said his win was “not about the Democratic Party or the Republican Party.” He framed it as a demand from voters, including some who had voted for Trump three times, for something other than political warfare. He has said he will meet every senator seeking a leadership post before he decides whom to back, and has declined to commit to his party’s leader in advance. These are real signs.


But his victory speech also showed the limit of that disposition. He described his opponents’ campaign as “the final howls of a dying establishment.” He said the real divide in American life is not left against right but “top versus bottom,” with billionaires and their allies keeping ordinary people angry at each other. That is a unity of a particular kind. It welcomes the working conservative as a fellow victim of the powerful, but it does not easily include the conservative business owner who sees the “top versus bottom” frame as an accusation against him. The letter writer, who speaks for people who “own businesses, employ people, pay taxes,” may find that the welcome is real while the agenda is not built for her.


**On investigations and impeachment**, the letter’s specific concern, the record is already clear about intentions. Senate Leader Schumer has promised “legislative razor wire” against the President. Representative Jamie Raskin, the likely House Judiciary chairman, called impeachment “not taboo” in September. Party leaders are divided on whether a third impeachment of Trump is wise. Reporting describes a compromise forming around impeaching Defense Secretary Hegseth instead. Some Republicans also want Hegseth gone, and Representative Thomas Massie has said he will push for a vote on impeachment articles during the lame-duck session. Democrats will take committee chairs with subpoena power and have announced oversight agendas on immigration enforcement, the President’s family business dealings, and more.


Impeachment begins in the House. Talarico’s part would be as one juror among a hundred in a Senate trial, where conviction needs two-thirds. The letter’s question to him, *will you demand evidence?*, is therefore the right one, because that is the only role he will have. Whether he demands it will be visible in his votes, and the letter’s “save this post” method can measure it.


## Is this a Democratic vice, or a feature of power?


The deeper premise behind skepticism of the letter is that Democrats in particular take whatever they can get and call it compromise: they ask for half, take it, and come back next session for the rest. There is real force in this, and I’ll return to it. But honesty requires looking at the Texas record from both sides before assigning the vice to one party.


The Republican trifecta that just lost power did not govern with deference to the Democratic third of the state either. Governor Abbott made vouchers his first priority for years. When House Republicans from rural districts blocked the program, he backed primary challengers against them until he had the votes. Bailes, now on Hinojosa’s transition team, was one of those Republicans. In 2025, at the President’s urging, the Legislature redrew congressional districts in the middle of the decade to gain seats. House Democrats broke quorum to stop it, and the maps passed once they returned. Each side reads these episodes as the other side’s lawlessness and its own self-defense.


Survey data show how symmetrical the suspicion has become. In Pew’s 2022 study, 72 percent of Republicans said Democrats are more immoral than other Americans, and 63 percent of Democrats said the same of Republicans. Both numbers had risen sharply since 2016. When majorities on each side believe the other is morally worse, every concession feels like collaboration with wrongdoing. Neither party will spend its mandate pleasing the side it believes is corrupt.


This does not mean the two parties’ agendas are morally equal. A citizen is right to judge an agenda by whether it is righteous, and the losing side does not owe the winners its agreement. It means the *behavior* the letter fears, a winning party governing by its convictions and spending its majority on them, is how power works under polarization, not a mark peculiar to one party. A conservative who expects Democrats to rule by Republican preferences is asking for something Republicans did not give Democrats either. That is the sense in which the letter is naive.


## Why the conservative concern remains plausible


Having said that, the worry behind your skepticism is not unfounded, and three features of this moment sharpen it.


**First, the ratchet is real.** Some policies are much easier to enact than to repeal, because they create beneficiaries who defend them. Benefit programs, coverage expansions, and payments are the clearest cases. The first-week agenda House Democrats have described includes restoring expired Affordable Care Act subsidies and reversing roughly $1 trillion in Medicaid cuts. Hinojosa wants Medicaid expansion in Texas. Once these are in place, a future Republican majority will find them politically costly to undo, much as it found the ACA itself. The conservative who says “they take half and come back for the rest” is describing a real asymmetry. It is not that one party is more ambitious. It is that expanding government creates constituencies, and contracting it has to fight them.


**Second, the winners have told us their mandate is change.** Hinojosa called the result a mandate for change. Talarico’s speech promised to get big money out of politics and to pursue universal health care. These are honest statements of intent. They are not promises to conserve. A citizen should take them at their word.


**Third, large majorities change behavior.** A House majority of 255 is the largest since 2008. That was the year whose Congress passed the Affordable Care Act without a single Republican vote. Large majorities feel less need to bargain. Party leaders’ talk of “razor wire” and investigative pushes suggests the national party reads this election as a license to confront, not to reconcile.

The letter’s own method is its best feature. “Save this post. Let’s revisit it in a year” turns a vague hope into a test. To make that test fair and sharp, measure:


- **Votes, not speeches.** Count how many of Talarico’s significant votes break from his party’s leadership, and on what.

- **Bills signed and how they passed.** For Hinojosa, did the property-tax and data-center bills pass with Republican Senate votes, and were Republican priorities treated as negotiable or as targets?

- **Use of the veto.** Line-item vetoes of voucher funding will show whether she governs through the Legislature or around it.

- **The Rainy Day Fund.** Track its balance before and after the $1,500 payments.

- **Oversight.** Did Talarico demand evidence in public before taking positions on impeachment or major investigations?


## Conclusion


A party that wins power will spend it on its convictions. That is true of Democrats now and was true of Republicans in Austin for thirty years. Expecting the winners to satisfy the losers’ preferences is naive. Expecting them to keep their own promises, to represent every citizen, and to treat the minority as fellow citizens rather than enemies is not naive. It is the minimum a free people may demand. Hinojosa will be pushed toward some of what the letter asks by a Republican Senate. Talarico will be pushed toward it only by his own word and by voters who keep the receipts.


*Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people.* **(Proverbs 14:34)** The letter’s best instinct is that righteousness in office is measured by what a ruler does after the vote, not by who won. Its weakness is hoping the winners will measure themselves by their opponents’ standard. They will not. So the minority must hold them to their own: the “politics of love” and the “bipartisan” administration they promised. And it must do so in public, with dates and numbers.
