# 261121 - Biblical Scripture as the Basis of Science

# The Renaissance: Two Fountains


### How the return to the ancient sources revived both Hermes and Moses, and why only one of them could build science

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 21 November 2026

I recently read an essay by Scott Lively titled "The Death of Magic." His thesis is that Christian Hebraism, the careful study of the Hebrew Bible by Christian scholars, broke the spell of alchemy and cleared the ground for experimental science. He names Robert Boyle, Isaac Newton, and John Locke as the men who made the turn. I think he is substantially right, and I want to place his argument in a larger frame: the Renaissance itself.

The Renaissance is usually told as the rebirth of classical learning. That is true, but it leaves out the drama. When Europe went back to its ancient sources, it reopened two fountains at once. One was the fountain of Hermes, the supposed Egyptian sage whose writings promised hidden power over nature. The other was the fountain of Moses, the Hebrew Scriptures read in their own language. Both flowed into the same centuries, often into the same minds. My argument is that the scientific revolution was, at its root, the moment Europe learned to tell the two waters apart, and that the Hebrew fountain made science possible.

## What the Renaissance Was: Back to the Sources

The watchword of the Renaissance humanists was *ad fontes*, Latin for "to the sources." Medieval scholars had learned the ancients mostly through commentaries, translations of translations, and digests. The humanists of fourteenth- and fifteenth-century Italy, and then of the North, wanted the original texts in the original languages. They hunted manuscripts in monastery libraries, learned Greek from Byzantine scholars, and used the new printing press to spread what they found.

That instinct was a good one. It assumes that truth has a source, that the source can be recovered, and that later layers of interpretation should be tested against it. But the instinct is only as good as the source it reaches. A man who returns to a poisoned well with great diligence is still drinking poison. The Renaissance returned to two wells, and they did not hold the same water.

## The First Fountain: Hermes and the Revival of Magic

In about 1460, a monk brought a Greek manuscript to Florence. It contained treatises attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, "Thrice-Great Hermes," believed to be an Egyptian priest-king who lived near the time of Moses. Cosimo de' Medici, then dying, told his scholar Marsilio Ficino to set aside his translation of Plato and translate Hermes first. Ficino's Latin version, the *Corpus Hermeticum*, went through many printings in the following century and a half.

The texts seemed to confirm everything a Christian Platonist hoped for. They spoke of a creative Word, a Son of God, and a creation story that echoed Genesis. Ficino and his circle concluded that God had given an "ancient theology" (*prisca theologia*) to the Gentiles as well as to Israel, passed from Hermes to Orpheus to Pythagoras to Plato. If that were true, the Hermetic writings stood beside Scripture as a second revelation.

They presented a picture of the cosmos as a living hierarchy, bound together by hidden sympathies. The phrase "as above, so below" summed it up. The stars influenced the metals; the metals answered to the organs; the wise man who knew the correspondences could draw down celestial powers through images, music, and substances. Ficino wrote a book on drawing life from the heavens. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola went further, declaring that no science gives greater assurance of Christ's divinity than magic and the Kabbalah. Henry Cornelius Agrippa compiled the whole system in his *Three Books of Occult Philosophy* (1533). Paracelsus carried it into medicine and chemistry with his three principles of salt, sulfur, and mercury.

This was the setting in which alchemy flourished. Lively is right that alchemy had a long prior history in Alexandria, the Islamic world, and medieval Latin scholarship. But the Renaissance gave it a new prestige, because it now seemed to rest on wisdom older than Plato and nearly as old as Moses. The alchemist's Great Work was the laboratory form of the magus's ambition: to perfect nature through secret knowledge and gain power over it.

Scripture had already named that ambition. *"There shall not be found among you any one that maketh his son or his daughter to pass through the fire, or that useth divination, or an observer of times, or an enchanter, or a witch, or a charmer, or a consulter with familiar spirits, or a wizard, or a necromancer. For all that do these things are an abomination unto the LORD"* **(Deuteronomy 18:10–12)**. The Renaissance magi did not think of themselves as doing any of that. They believed they were practicing a "natural" magic, free of demons. But the root was the same: the desire for hidden power over a world imagined as full of half-divine forces.

## The Second Fountain: Hebrew and the Recovery of Scripture

The same *ad fontes* instinct sent Christian scholars back to the Hebrew Bible. In 1506 the German humanist Johann Reuchlin published *De rudimentis hebraicis*, a Hebrew grammar and lexicon written so that Christians could read the Old Testament in its own language. It was the first real work of its kind by a Christian, and Reuchlin is often called the father of Christian Hebraism in Central Europe. Erasmus did the same service for the Greek New Testament in 1516. Luther, Tyndale, and the translators of the King James Bible built on both.

One complication was that Hebrew study did not automatically break the spell. Reuchlin learned his love of Kabbalah from Pico, and he wrote books trying to build a Christian Kabbalah out of the mystical letter-speculation of the rabbis. The Hebrew language, approached as a code of hidden powers, could feed magic as easily as Greek Hermeticism did.

What broke the spell was not Hebrew as such but a particular way of reading it: plainly, grammatically, historically, asking what the words meant to the people who first received them. The Reformers pressed this hard. They rejected the medieval habit of piling allegorical, moral, and mystical meanings onto every verse and insisted on the literal sense as the foundation. The historian Peter Harrison has argued that this change in how Protestants read the Bible also changed how they read nature. When a text stops being a forest of symbols, the world it describes stops being one too. A plant is no longer chiefly a sign of a virtue or a planet; it is a creature with a structure that can be examined.

The Hebrew text itself supports that reading of the world. Genesis opens with a God who speaks creation into being from outside it. The sun and moon, which every surrounding nation worshiped, are demoted to "lights" set in the sky to mark seasons. Matter is neither divine nor evil. *"And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good"* **(Genesis 1:31)**. Good, but made; real, but not a god. That is exactly the kind of world one can study by experiment.

## Breaking the Spell

The spell broke in three steps, and each step used a tool the Renaissance itself had sharpened.

The first was philology. In 1614, the Protestant classical scholar Isaac Casaubon examined the Greek of the Hermetic writings and showed that they could not be the work of an Egyptian sage contemporary with Moses. Their vocabulary and ideas belonged to the early Christian centuries; they borrowed from Plato and from Christian teaching rather than foreshadowing them. A Catholic scholar, Dionysius Petavius, reached similar conclusions. With one careful reading of the text, the "ancient theology" lost its antiquity. Hermes was no longer a prophet beside Moses. He was a late author in Egyptian dress. Many people kept believing anyway, but no educated person could do so honestly after Casaubon.

The second was method. Francis Bacon, in his *New Organon* (1620), demanded that natural philosophy proceed by collecting observations and testing them, not by deducing from ancient authorities. He criticized the alchemists not for their curiosity but for their secrecy, their boasting, and their habit of explaining away every failure. Bacon also gave the new science its theological charter. He liked to quote Solomon: *"It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter"* **(Proverbs 25:2)**. God hides things to be found, not to be seized by incantation.

The third was the laboratory itself, and here Robert Boyle stands at the center. In 1661 he published two books. One was *The Sceptical Chymist*, which attacked both the four elements of Aristotle and the three principles of Paracelsus as unproven, and which insisted that claims about the makeup of bodies be tested openly and reported in plain language. The other, in the same year, was *Some Considerations Touching the Style of the Holy Scriptures*, a defense of the Bible's language and a guide to reading it carefully. The two books are a matched pair. The same man, in the same year, was teaching Europe to read God's two books, Scripture and nature, with the same honesty.

## Boyle, Newton, and Locke

Lively is right that these three men stand at the turning point, and right that their biblical seriousness was part of what they were doing, not a private hobby beside it. Boyle studied the biblical languages and paid to have the Bible translated into Irish, Turkish, and other languages. He regarded his theology and his natural philosophy as one seamless work. Late in life he wrote *A Free Enquiry into the Vulgarly Received Notion of Nature* (1686), arguing that "Nature" should not be spoken of as a wise, purposeful agent standing between God and the world. That is the heart of Lively's point, and Boyle made it himself in so many words. Locke learned experimental practice in Boyle's Oxford circle and later edited Boyle's *General History of the Air*. His *First Treatise of Government* is largely a close reading of Genesis, and his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* holds that revelation may go above reason but never against it.

The record is more complicated in one respect, and I think the complication helps the thesis. The turn did not happen *between* the alchemists and these men. It happened *inside* them.

Boyle believed he had seen a transmutation of metal in his own laboratory, and he lobbied Parliament in 1689 to repeal an old law against multiplying gold. Newton left behind about a million words on alchemy, written in secret over some thirty years, with pages stained by the acids of his experiments. When the economist John Maynard Keynes bought the papers in 1936, he concluded that Newton was "the last of the magicians." Newton's theology was also unorthodox in ways Lively does not mention; privately he rejected the doctrine of the Trinity.

So these men did not simply drop the philosopher's stone. They dropped the *method* that had surrounded it: the secrecy, the appeal to ancient authority, the symbolic language that could never be proven wrong. Recent historians such as William Newman read Newton's alchemy as serious experimental chemistry carried out under an older vocabulary. Boyle and Newton kept hoping for transmutation, but they tested the hope in the open, published their methods, and let the results decide. The theory that required the stone did not survive that kind of testing. In the end, the method that grew out of their faith was stronger than the hopes they inherited from Hermes. That, to me, is a more convincing story than a clean break, because it shows that the biblical picture of creation did its work even on men who were still half-enchanted.

## Why the Thesis Holds

I stand with Lively's central claim: the biblical doctrine of creation, recovered through careful reading of the Hebrew Scriptures, supplied the picture of the world that experimental science requires. Historians argue about how much weight to give it against other causes, and they are right to argue. Copernicus and Galileo were Catholics; medieval scholastics and Islamic scholars built much of the apparatus; printing, trade, and new instruments all mattered. But those qualifications describe the materials of science. They do not explain why Europe in the seventeenth century finally committed to open experiment and abandoned secret correspondence. For that, the picture of the world matters most.

Experimental science needs a world with four properties at once:


 	- **Orderly**, so that there are regularities to find. A Lawgiver makes that expected.

 	- **Contingent**, so that one must look rather than deduce. A Creator who was free to make things otherwise means we cannot know the world from our armchairs; we have to go and see.

 	- **Not divine**, so that it is permitted to cut, weigh, and dissect it. A creature can be probed; a god must be appeased or manipulated.

 	- **Good**, so that it is worth the trouble to study. *"The works of the LORD are great, sought out of all them that have pleasure therein"* **(Psalm 111:2)**. James Clerk Maxwell had that verse carved over the door of the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge.


The Hermetic cosmos fails the second and third tests. Its forms and sympathies are known by insight, not measurement, and its powers are half-divine. Aristotle's cosmos fails the second, because its essences are deduced rather than discovered. Only the Genesis picture passes all four.

Science also needs a moral posture, and here the contrast is just as sharp. Magic is secret because it seeks power, and power is not shared. Experimental science is public because it seeks truth, and truth is tested by witnesses. *"At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established"* **(Deuteronomy 19:15)**. The Royal Society's practice of performing experiments before witnesses and publishing every step is that principle applied to nature. The sociologist Robert Merton observed in 1938 that the early Royal Society was dominated by devout Protestants. Its members did not see a conflict between their faith and their work, because their faith was what told them the work was honest, permitted, and worth doing.

## The Renaissance We Still Need

This ministry carries the name Renaissance, and the first Renaissance teaches a lesson worth keeping: the return to the source is not enough by itself. It matters which fountain one drinks from.

Our age has two errors that mirror the old ones. The first is the Hermetic error in modern dress: the drive for power over nature, and over human nature, by any technique that works, with no question asked about whether it is Godly. The second is the opposite error: a materialism that says matter is all there is, that nothing is made and nothing is meant. The Renaissance magus made the world too divine. The modern materialist makes it empty. Neither can say why truth should be told when a lie would pay.

The biblical picture stands between them, and I would add one thing central to everything I teach. God did not make the world and step away. He inhabits it, and He experiences every person's life from the inside. In a real sense, we are God's experience of His own creation. That does not make the creature a god to be worshiped or coerced, as the magi imagined. It means the opposite: because God lives our experience, He feels every choice we make. I go into this more fully in my [Premise Change handout](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/10/31/260725-premise-change-handout/).

That changes what scientific honesty is. When Boyle reported a failed experiment plainly, or when a researcher today refuses to massage his data, he is not only following a procedure. He is refusing to make God live through a lie. The alchemist hid his work because he wanted power. The Godly experimenter shows his work because he loves the One who sees and feels it with him, and he loves the neighbor who will build on it.

The philosopher's stone was never found because it was never there. What Europe found instead, by reading Moses carefully and testing nature openly, was a way of knowing that has fed, healed, and connected billions of people. That is the fruit of the right fountain. The renaissance we need now is a return to the same source, read with the same care, and lived with the same love.

These sources lean on the mainstream academic record. Three limits are worth naming. First, I could not confirm Lively's more specific claims about Boyle's Hebrew (a personal Hebrew grammar, consultation with Menasseh ben Israel) in the sources I checked, so I left them out; they may appear in older Boyle biographies. Second, for a century, the history of science has been written largely by scholars who treat religious motives as secondary, and Harrison's work is part of a correction that is still underway. Third, scholarship on Newton's private papers is ongoing, so the picture of his alchemy and theology is still being refined.
