# 260927 - Recap - Islam, Coexistence, and the Constitutional Order

# Islam, Coexistence, and the Constitutional Order


# PART ONE — The Conversation, Point by Point


## 1. Michael’s Opening: The Mirror Between Extremes


Michael opened by saying he had a list of points written down in response to Thomas’s essay of the previous week, and that he saw a parallel in it that was stark and significant.


He began with Thomas’s references to Islamic writings that call for violence and for the imposition of Islamic belief. When 9/11 happened, Michael said, he had several friends in New York City who worked in ecumenical cooperation among mosques, churches, and temples. They pursued, ardently, the work of letting everyone know that the attack was not about the religion of Islam but about a group of extremists. President George W. Bush, for several days afterward, repeatedly sent the same message: this is not about Islam; it is about these extremists. Michael said that message mattered because violence was being done to Muslims simply for being Muslim, in something like a racist reaction but directed at religion. The government and the churches — Lutheran, Methodist, Episcopalian — made a concerted effort, gathering at large public events with imams and rabbis, and he found that heartening. Yet, he said, there was still harsh condemnation of the religion by people who never received the message.


The parallel, Michael said, is this. There are Muslims who look at Christians who want to do what Thomas proposes — restrain, watch, and monitor Muslims — and conclude that Christians are against them. They point to groups such as the Proud Boys and the Oath Keepers, whom Michael described as Christian groups that want to murder Muslims outright and at times do, and they conclude that Christianity is evil. Michael said he would answer such a Muslim: remember 9/11; remember how the peacemaking Christians supported you; do not give in to the Christians who say these terrible things about restricting you, because Christians believe in cooperation and in sharing this planet. But those Muslims point to people who speak of a “Christian nation” and say, “See, Christians want to restrict us.”


Here, Michael said, is the contradiction: the very thing Thomas alleges about Muslims — that they are unfair and want to restrict others — is exactly what those Muslims allege about Christians.


Michael said it is an inherent property of his mind to place whatever he hears on a spectrum. The spectrum is really n-dimensional, but for this purpose he kept it to one dimension. At each end are the extremes. Muslim extremists point at Christian extremists and say, “They hate us; they want to do horrible things to us,” and Christian extremists say the same of Muslims. The two ends are mirrors of each other. In between are the people who say, “We differ in our views, but we want to live together.”


That same spectrum, he said, is not only Christian–Muslim. It describes Protestant and Catholic; seven hundred years of Eastern Orthodox and Catholic division; the long Protestant–Catholic history in Ireland; the Mormons, who endured their own horrifying history. Whenever he hears this kind of alienation, he places it on the spectrum: at the far ends, both sides want to kill each other; a little further in, they do not want to kill but, as Thomas proposes, to monitor the other and place government restrictions on them. Muslims face this too: in France there are rules against wearing the hijab, so a person may not wear his own religious garb.


As an empathetic person, Michael said, his answer is coexistence. When he heard Thomas say “Christian nation,” he thought, “You are at the far end of the spectrum, saying about the other exactly what they say about Christians. You cite what they say about Christians; they cite what you say about Muslims.”

>
**Note (Bush’s statements).** On 17 September 2001, speaking at the Islamic Center of Washington, President Bush said that the face of terror is not the true faith of Islam and that Islam is peace. In his address to Congress on 20 September 2001 he said the terrorists were traitors to their own faith and that the enemy was not Muslims or Arabs but a radical network. Michael’s summary of the message is accurate. Thomas’s later description of it as “a religion of peace” (see the paired file) refers to the same statements.


>
**Note (Proud Boys and Oath Keepers).** Neither organization defines itself as a Christian body. The Proud Boys describe themselves as a “Western chauvinist” men’s club; the Oath Keepers were an anti-government militia organization. Leaders of both were convicted of seditious conspiracy in connection with 6 January 2021. Neither is known primarily for organized violence against Muslims. Anti-Muslim violence in the West has been committed more often by individuals and small networks; the Christchurch mosque shooter (New Zealand, 2019) wrote an ethno-nationalist manifesto, not a Christian one. Michael’s broader point — that some Muslims perceive Western hostility and attribute it to Christianity — stands independently of these two examples.


>
**Note (France).** France prohibited conspicuous religious symbols, including the headscarf, in public primary and secondary schools in 2004; prohibited face-covering garments in public places in 2010; and extended the school rule to the abaya in 2023. There is no general French ban on adult women wearing the hijab in public. The rules rest on the French doctrine of *laïcité* (state secularism) and apply in principle to conspicuous symbols of every religion.


## 2. Thomas: Resolving Along the Axis of Truth


Thomas answered that he thought the question resolves along the axis of truth. The question is whether Islam contains within it an element that is simply incompatible with Western values. Can Muslims and non-Muslims actually get along, coexist, each doing his own thing, under a single Constitution — rather than having Muslims living as a completely separate body inside the nation?


## 3. Michael: Where the Origin Sits; Western Values as a Moving Target


Michael replied that the axis of truth depends on where one places the origin, as one says in mathematics. If the axis begins from Christianity, or from Islam, the reading of truth comes out differently — and that is the core of the matter. His own work, and what he enjoys, is finding a way to see a truth that incorporates both, as in the story of the blind men and the elephant. Where you stand determines what you see. He said he did not want to say truth is relative, but that it is relative to the person determining it.


On “compatible with Western values,” Michael said Western values evolve. In the 1500s, a Western value was that the Spanish invaded, enslaved the natives, and killed some ninety percent of the Arawaks and many others; Western values meant going to a country, enslaving its people, taking their gold, killing most of them, and leaving missionaries behind to preach Christianity to people who did not understand Spanish — or, later, English, French, or Portuguese — and so were never going to become Christians. If that is too long ago, take the 1800s: slavery was a Western value; women did not vote; black people did not have citizenship. In the 1920s the Ku Klux Klan was at its height, promoting Christian nationalism and opposing the mixing of the races. Those, he said, were Western values in their time.


Michael then said he had been talking too long and would stop, but first addressed something from the prior week: the claim that the First Amendment is not part of the Constitution. The amendments are part of the Constitution, he said. Without them women would not vote and black people would still be counted as three-fifths of a person. We amended the Constitution because we came to believe women should vote and black people should be counted fully. If one wants to hold to the original Constitution alone, those things are not in it.

>
**Note (Arawak/Taíno population).** The Taíno (Arawak-speaking) population of Hispaniola collapsed from an estimated several hundred thousand (estimates range widely, from about 100,000 to over a million) to a few thousand within roughly thirty years of 1492. Historians attribute the collapse to epidemic disease, the forced labor of the *encomienda* system, violence, famine, and social disruption together; disease is generally regarded as the largest single factor, though forced labor and violence greatly compounded it. Contemporary Spanish critics, notably Bartolomé de las Casas — himself a Dominican friar — condemned the conduct on Christian grounds.


>
**Note (terminology).** The transcript’s “misogynation” is *miscegenation*, the term then used for interracial marriage and sexual relations. The second Ku Klux Klan peaked in the mid-1920s with estimates of three to six million members and used explicitly Protestant rhetoric.


>
**Note (amendments).** Article V states that ratified amendments “shall be valid to all Intents and Purposes, as Part of this Constitution.” The three-fifths clause (Art. I, §2) was superseded by §2 of the Fourteenth Amendment (1868); women’s suffrage was secured by the Nineteenth Amendment (1920). Michael is correct that the amendments are part of the Constitution.


## 4. Thomas Summarizes for Charlie


Charlie said he had joined late and did not know how the discussion had begun. Thomas summarized: Michael was responding to Thomas’s essay of the previous week, in which Thomas argued that Islam is a threat and that the nation needs to monitor people who threaten the constitutional order. Michael’s point was that after 9/11 people came together, and President Bush urged everyone to get along, yet the country still made Islam “other” and an enemy, so that people hate Islam because of a few extremists. The problem, in Michael’s view, is not Islam but a few extremists — which, Thomas said, is exactly the opposite of his essay.


## 5. Charlie: The Historical Record and Sharia


Charlie said the historical record, as far as he could tell, shows that it is “only a few radicals” until Muslims form a majority in a country, and then there is hell to pay. Islam carries an ideology that is incompatible with the West. Michael may think it compatible, Charlie said, and he wished Michael would spend some time living in an Islamic country to see what it is really like. Charlie said he needs to visit someone in Egypt soon, but he would not want to live there; he does not like how it looks. He has lovely Muslim friends, but he does not know whether they would stay so lovely if they were the majority, given what history shows.


Sharia law, Charlie said, is not compatible with the West, and it is not only a few radicals who wish to live under it. It is always described as “a few radicals,” but among the great majority of Christians and Jews, no one is cutting off anyone’s head, and no one is stoned for being gay. That, he said, is a very large difference.

>
**Note (survey evidence on sharia).** The Pew Research Center’s 2013 survey *The World’s Muslims: Religion, Politics and Society* (39 countries) found support for making sharia the official law of the land ranging from 99% in Afghanistan and 91% in Iraq, through 72% in Indonesia and 74% in Egypt, to 12% in Turkey, 10% in Kazakhstan, and 8% in Azerbaijan. Among those who favored sharia, support for specific penalties varied sharply by country: for example, majorities in Egypt, Jordan, Afghanistan, and Pakistan favored the death penalty for leaving Islam, while in Turkey, Bosnia, and Central Asia only small minorities did. The data therefore support Charlie’s claim that support for sharia is not limited to “a few radicals” in many Muslim-majority countries, and support Michael’s claim that the picture varies enormously by country. Pew’s 2017 survey of U.S. Muslims found that about 92% said they were proud to be American and a majority said there is more than one true way to interpret Islam’s teachings.


## 6. Michael: American Mosques, Sectarian Division, and Coexistence


Michael said he fully agreed with what Charlie described, but that he has visited many mosques and discussed religion with the people there, and in American mosques he has heard none of it. What happens elsewhere is horrifying; he thought the Afghans have it worst, and the level of horror a woman growing up there endures deflates his heart. But he has found none of that in any American mosque he has visited.


He added that Shiite and Sunni Muslims will kill each other before they go after Christians, because they hate each other so much. So Islam should not be categorized as one thing. That would be like treating Christianity as one thing when it includes Unitarians, Mormons, Methodists, Quakers, and Catholics. They share principles, as Muslims do, but putting them all in one basket misses a great deal.


In Iran, Michael said, one of the large coming transformations is that the young do not accept the sharia regime, because through their phones and connections they are aware of the West. There was significant rebellion against that regime, and it was making good progress until “we put up this war.” Now, he said, Iranians reason: we may hate sharia law, but being bombed and killed is worse, so the sharia people are right that we must defend our families, and we side with them even though we despise their views. If we ever stop bombing, the natural course, he believes, is that Muslims in Iran and elsewhere will evolve to be like Muslims in the United States. If Muslims ever became a majority here, American Muslims would be much like American Christians. The underlying difference would be the name they give to God. If that difference matters greatly to Thomas, Michael said, then it matters; but for a person focused on cohabitation, the important thing is acceptance.


He compared this to Protestants and Catholics, saying that one side holds the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost to be one and the other holds them to be three different things, and therefore they must kill each other because God is on their side. Differences of that kind make it laughable that both call themselves Christian while hating each other. What he seeks, deep down, is coexistence — between Christians, and between Christianity and Islam. We give God different names; we can kill each other over it or accept it. There are extremists on both sides who find one another at the ends of the spectrum, but that is not what is really going on. Visit a mosque with an open heart, he said, and you will find no one saying these things; he has looked and has not found it.

>
**Note (Shia–Sunni conflict).** Sectarian violence between Sunni and Shia Muslims has been severe in modern Iraq (especially 2006–2008), Syria, Yemen, Pakistan, and elsewhere, and the Iran–Saudi rivalry has a strong sectarian dimension. The Iran–Iraq war (1980–1988) was fought chiefly along national lines. Michael’s claim that the sects “will kill each other before they go after the Christians” is a generalization: in many conflicts Christians have been targeted by the same groups (e.g., ISIS attacks on Iraqi and Syrian Christians and Egyptian Copts) that also targeted Shia.


>
**Note (Iran).** The largest recent protest wave followed the death of Mahsa Amini in police custody in September 2022. “This war” most likely refers to the June 2025 Israel–Iran conflict, during which the United States struck Iranian nuclear facilities, or to hostilities since. Observers disagree about whether external attack strengthened the regime by rallying the population or weakened it; both effects have been reported.


>
**Note (the Trinity).** Roman Catholics, Eastern Orthodox, and historic Protestant churches all affirm the doctrine of the Trinity — one God in three Persons. They do not divide over whether God is one or three. The historic Catholic–Orthodox division concerned, among other things, the *filioque* clause (whether the Spirit proceeds from the Father “and the Son”) and papal authority; the Catholic–Protestant division concerned justification, authority, and the sacraments. Groups that deny the Trinity (Unitarians, Latter-day Saints, Oneness Pentecostals, Jehovah’s Witnesses) are generally classed as nontrinitarian.


## 7. “Do You Think They Would Tell You?”


Charlie asked whether Michael thought Muslims would tell him if they held such views.


Michael answered with a question: would Christians tell you? The ones who believe it will, he said, because they think it is righteous — the Christian who wants to kill Muslims, or who wants Christian nationalism and a Christian country, will say so, just as the Muslim extremist will say he wants the country to be Muslim. But Christian nationalists and Muslim nationalists are statistically rare, and they incite each other. Most people have jobs, go to work, raise children, and send them to school.


Michael closed this point by naming the one paradigm he said every religion throughout history has accepted: mathematics, which he teaches. Everyone agrees that two plus three is five. There are other points of agreement — the temperature measured today, for instance. Start with the points of agreement, he said, and build from there, because life is too full, rich, and delightful to spend hating each other.


## 8. Leonard: Rule of Law and the Constitutional Republic


Thomas asked Leonard for his comment. (Leonard joked that he was looking for a book; Thomas said he had set him up for it.)


Leonard said the genius of the founders was the rule of law — setting men against men in government, controlling them by their own ambitions. Whoever comes to this country, whether naturalized, born here, or converted to any religion, must still conform to the basic understanding of the rule of law. We do not live in a democracy, Leonard said. The United States is a constitutional republic with representative government chosen by a democratic process. He asked Charlie what a democracy is; Charlie answered, “It’s a mob,” and Leonard added: “Mobocracy — two wolves and a sheep deciding what to have for dinner.”


People who come to this country need to understand that the state of being here is the rule of constitutional law, and anything against it is unlawful and should not be permitted — or the whole structure comes down, which, Leonard said, is what some want: to tear it all down and rebuild it as their own. He agreed that the majority in every group — Christian, Muslim, Jewish, Hindu — are peaceful people. But every group has its radicals, and in his opinion, if one compares the radicals of all these groups, those who hold a radical understanding of Islam are the least compatible with the rule of law in this country. That rule of law has held for more than 250 years, he said, and if it is not maintained, the country is finished.


## 9. Michael: Locke, the Tyranny of the Majority, and the Filibuster


Michael said he agreed completely. The structure of the country and the Constitution, drafted largely by Jefferson’s and Madison’s generation, did not begin with them; Michael recounted that someone asked Madison where he got the idea of the Constitution, and the answer was “Pure Locke.” A century earlier, Locke had been arguing that the rule of kings was intolerable before any alternative was possible. Jefferson, Michael said, wrote about the potential tyranny of the majority — exactly the wolves-and-sheep problem Leonard described. That is where the filibuster comes in: one side gains power and might run roughshod over everything, and the filibuster means that if a third of the Senate says no, the majority faces serious hurdles. He called it an incredible piece of genius. When his side is in the majority he wishes the vote would pass; when the other side is in power, he thanks heaven for the filibuster. That, he said, is how the system was designed — with restrictions. He agreed that a democracy can become a mob without constraints, but said that democracy means that we vote, and that he does not dismiss democracy as harshly as Leonard seemed to.

>
**Note (Madison and Locke).** No source is known for Madison saying the Constitution was “pure Locke.” Jefferson, writing to Henry Lee in 1825 about the Declaration of Independence, named Aristotle, Cicero, Locke, and Algernon Sidney among its sources. Locke’s *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) strongly influenced the Declaration; the Constitution drew also on Montesquieu (separation of powers), English constitutional practice, colonial charters, and classical republican thought. Leonard’s interjection of “Native Americans” is addressed in the note to Section 11.


>
**Note (tyranny of the majority).** The fullest founding-era treatment is Madison’s, in *Federalist* No. 10 (faction) and No. 51 (ambition counteracting ambition), which is also the source of Leonard’s “pitting men against men” description. Jefferson, in *Notes on the State of Virginia* (Query XIII), warned that “173 despots would surely be as oppressive as one,” criticizing elective despotism. He did not write poems on the subject. The phrase “tyranny of the majority” was popularized by Alexis de Tocqueville (*Democracy in America*, 1835) and John Stuart Mill (*On Liberty*, 1859).


>
**Note (filibuster).** The filibuster is not part of the Constitution or the founders’ design; it arose after the Senate dropped its “previous question” motion in 1806, which left no rule for ending debate. The cloture rule (1917) required a two-thirds vote to end debate; in 1975 the Senate reduced this to three-fifths of senators (60). A “third” (34 senators) could block cloture under the old rule; today 41 can. The founders’ structural checks against majority tyranny are bicameralism, the presidential veto, the Senate’s staggered and (originally) state-legislature-selected membership, federalism, judicial review, and the supermajorities required for treaties, veto overrides, and amendments.


## 10. Pure Democracy, the Article V Convention, and the Difference in Islam


Leonard explained that pure democracy means voting on everything, with the majority ruling: if 51% vote to abolish the police, the police are abolished, which is absurd. That, he said, is why the founders despised pure democracy. They used the democratic process to choose representatives in a constitutional republic, so that everyone is represented — “no taxation without representation” was a central cause then and remains one. He said that with modern technology a pure democracy might be technically possible, but it would need further rules, such as a supermajority for any change, since otherwise 51% could change everything.


He added that the Constitution itself contains a vehicle for wholesale change — a constitutional convention — which he described as carte blanche: gather a majority of the states in convention and they could wipe everything out. He called that a frightening possibility, and said it is truly a miracle the country has lasted as long as it has; after the Civil War, people must have thought it was finished. He was amazed the nation is still viable, despite continuing craziness in government.


Leonard then returned to Islam: one has to acknowledge that there is a difference between the Islamic view of government and the Judeo-Christian view — a difference in the value of the soul. And he said to Michael: you brought up “values” and named a list of things; none of those are values. Look up the word. Those were events and things people did, not values.

>
**Note (Article V).** An Article V convention, called on the application of two-thirds of the state legislatures (34), may only *propose* amendments. Any proposal takes effect only when ratified by three-fourths of the states (38), through their legislatures or ratifying conventions. A convention therefore could not by itself “wipe it all out.” Whether a convention could be confined to a single subject, or could “run away” as some argue the 1787 Convention did, is a genuine and unresolved legal debate; but the ratification threshold is a separate and very high barrier.


## 11. Democracy, Republic, and *Res Publica*


Michael began to reply, starting with “Democrats and Republicans.” Leonard interjected that the parties have changed over time — the Democrats of today were the Republicans of yesterday, or the reverse, and the Democrats of today were the communists of yesterday. Michael agreed the parties have gone through full cycles but said he meant the definitions, not the parties.


Michael defined the difference this way: democracy has everyone voting on everything; a republic elects officials who then do the voting. The problem with pure democracy is that no one wants to vote on every street sign. So we elect people and tell them to decide where the street signs go. That is where the republican model supersedes the democratic. But the democratic model applies in that those officials are democratically elected — not appointed by a priest, imam, or rabbi. Leonard supplied the phrase “the consent of the governed.” Michael said that is why it is called a democratic republic: democratically, we vote for those who will make the decisions.


Leonard said nothing in the Constitution says anything about being a democracy; Michael agreed that the word does not appear but said the concept — voting — appears throughout. Leonard said the vote is the one right the Constitution protects and it is under attack. He asked whether anyone knew where the word “republic” comes from. Michael said Plato (*The Republic*). Leonard, Charlie, and Thomas worked out the Latin: *res publica* — *res*, the thing, the matter, the affair; *publica*, of the people. The republic is “the thing of the people.”


Michael asked who decides the thing; we vote for who decides it — that is democracy. Leonard said no, Michael was conflating two things: it is not a democracy but a democratic process. By that process the governed voice their consent or disagreement. Where does government’s power come from? Thomas said, “The people.” Leonard said, “The consent of the governed” — those who are governed consenting to it, and voicing that consent through a piece of paper placed in a box and counted. It is only a process. Democracy, he said, has been conflated with the idea that everything must be voted on, which some would love, but a society cannot run that way. We find people who will represent our ideas and place them in positions to decide for us while we live our lives and work. Leonard said he thought the founders borrowed much of this from Native Americans, who had a similar concept within their tribes even though they often warred with each other.


Michael said he has studied many Native American tribes and they varied as widely as European nations; the Navajo, Arapaho, and Sioux were very different, and he has trouble making any single statement about all of them. Leonard asked whether he had opened another can of worms; Michael said he had opened one of those Fourth of July cans of spring snakes, and would not address it.


On values, Michael said the values the nation undertakes are determined by the people we elect to choose them; those values have evolved considerably over time, but they are values we vote for.

>
**Note (terminology).** In founding-era usage, “democracy” usually meant direct rule by assembled citizens and “republic” meant representative government under law (Madison, *Federalist* No. 10 and No. 14). Modern political science usually classes the United States as a constitutional (or liberal) democracy and a federal republic; the terms are not exclusive in current usage. Leonard and Michael largely agreed on the substance — elected representatives, limited by a constitution, chosen by vote — and differed on the label. The Constitution itself guarantees to every state “a Republican Form of Government” (Art. IV, §4).


>
**Note (Native American influence).** The “Iroquois influence thesis” holds that the Haudenosaunee Confederacy influenced American federalism; Benjamin Franklin referred to the Iroquois union in 1751, and Congress passed a resolution acknowledging the influence in 1988 (H. Con. Res. 331). Many historians regard the direct influence on the Constitution as modest compared with European and colonial sources. Michael’s point about tribal diversity is correct.


## 12. Armond: Chasing Rabbits


Thomas had muted Armond for background noise and invited him to comment. Armond first joked about taking the Fifth. He then said the discussion was over his head at the moment and that the group was chasing rabbits — trying to herd cats, or catch something elusive. This kind of discussion, he said, is exactly what keeps people separated on principle, because there is nothing finite on which anyone can reach a conclusion — even among respected peers and loved ones — unless everyone agreed that one person was simply right, which no one can claim given the nature of the discussion.


## 13. Leonard: A Line in the Sand


Leonard said a line could be drawn using the Federalist Papers and the Constitution with its amendments. What the Constitution is about, he said, is preserving the rights of the people so that they can exercise them freely and responsibly, with consequences if they do not. But, he observed, the Constitution does not apply directly to the people: the word “people” appears nine times, but the document does not act upon them, create anything for them, or give them anything. So what did the Constitution constitute? Thomas answered: it is a restriction on the federal government. Leonard agreed: it limited the federal government to specific enumerated powers.


Instead, Leonard said, we have built a vast, out-of-control government with layer upon layer. He said he did not know whether it was true, but it seemed that a majority of employed people now work for government in some way, and that the Biden administration hired large numbers. The line in the sand, he said, would be a shared understanding of the government’s limited powers and of the rights that government protects, enumerated in the Bill of Rights. Once everyone agreed that this is the foundation, it would be easier to understand the other issues that come into conflict with it.

>
**Note (“people” in the Constitution).** Leonard’s count is correct if the amendments are included: the Preamble; Art. I, §2; Amendments I, II, IV, IX, and X; and the Seventeenth Amendment (twice). The Constitution does, however, act directly on individuals in several places: Art. I, §9 (habeas corpus, bills of attainder), Art. III, §3 (treason), the Thirteenth Amendment (which binds private persons, forbidding slavery by anyone), and the taxing and commerce powers, which reach individuals directly — the key change from the Articles of Confederation, which acted only on the states.


>
**Note (government employment).** Federal, state, and local governments together employ roughly 23 million people, about 14–15% of total U.S. nonfarm employment (Bureau of Labor Statistics). Federal civilian employment is about 3 million. Including government contractors and publicly funded sectors (e.g., parts of health care and education) raises the share considerably, but not to a majority.


## 14. Susan: *Because They Hate*


Susan said she basically agreed with Leonard, and also took issue with Michael’s view that the country is a democracy, but wanted to address the question of Muslims living peaceably with Americans before the group turned to its planned topic.


She held up Brigitte Gabriel’s book *Because They Hate*, which she had recommended before. Gabriel grew up in Lebanon, which Susan described as basically a Christian nation. Muslims moved in gradually and were very peaceable with the Christians, and they gradually rose into positions of authority. They remained peaceable until they were numerous enough to overpower the Christians. Then, Susan said, even those who had not seemed radical united with the radicals in a killing spree to take power. Gabriel, she said, identifies this as a pattern: live peaceably with others until the numbers and positions of power are sufficient to overcome them. Susan asked Michael to read the book and consider whether it changed his outlook.

>
**Note (Lebanon).** Brigitte Gabriel’s *Because They Hate: A Survivor of Islamic Terror Warns America* (2006) recounts her childhood in southern Lebanon during the civil war (1975–1990). The 1932 census showed a slight Christian majority, which underlay the 1943 National Pact allocating offices by sect; by the 1970s Muslims were widely believed to be the majority, and no census has been taken since. The civil war involved many factions: Christian (especially Maronite) militias, Palestinian organizations (the PLO had relocated to Lebanon after 1970), Sunni, Shia, and Druze militias, and Syrian and Israeli intervention. Massacres were committed by several sides — e.g., against Christians at Damour (1976) and against Palestinians and Muslims at Karantina (1976) and Sabra and Shatila (1982, by Christian Phalangist militia). Gabriel’s account is valued by supporters as eyewitness testimony and criticized by others as one-sided. The demographic-shift explanation is part of the scholarly account; historians also stress the Palestinian armed presence, Cold War and regional interventions, and the rigidity of the sectarian power-sharing system.


## 15. The Inquisition and “Mainstream Christianity”


Michael said the pattern Susan described is historically well documented — and began to cite the Spanish Inquisition, which put thousands to death for not professing to be Catholic.


Thomas interrupted to ask: is that the mindset of America now? Does America have any policy that if you are not Catholic and do not pray the proper devotions, you will be put on the rack and in the iron maiden and tortured until you adopt proper doctrine? Is that anywhere in Christendom now? Was it an aberration, or is it what mainstream Christianity is?


Michael began to cite New York in the 1880s, when Catholics and Protestants were at each other’s throats. Thomas asked the same question of that example: did it survive as a general cultural mandate? **Note (the Spanish Inquisition).** The Spanish Inquisition operated from 1478 to 1834. **Note (Surah 9:5).** Surah 9 (*at-Tawbah*, “Repentance”) opens with a declaration of release from treaties with polytheists of Arabia who had broken their agreements. # PART TWO — Meta-Essay: Is Islam Compatible with the Constitutional Order?


## 1. What Was Actually in Dispute


The conversation looked like a single disagreement — Michael’s “it’s a few extremists” against Thomas’s, Charlie’s, and Susan’s “it’s the religion” — but it contained at least five distinct questions that were repeatedly traded for one another:


- **Doctrine:** What do Islam’s authoritative sources (the Quran, the hadith, the classical law schools) teach about non-Muslims, apostates, and the relation of religion to the state?

- **Practice:** What do Muslims actually believe and do — in Muslim-majority countries, in the West, and in the United States specifically?

- **Trajectory:** What happens when Muslim populations grow from minority to majority? (Charlie’s and Susan’s central claim.)

- **Symmetry:** Is Christianity’s record comparable, so that singling out Islam is a double standard? (Michael’s central claim.)

- **Policy:** What, if anything, should the United States do — monitor, restrict, require renunciation, or simply enforce general law equally?


Much of the talking-past came from answering one question with evidence relevant to another. Michael answered the doctrine question with evidence about practice (“no one in any mosque I visited says this”). Thomas answered the symmetry question by asking about present practice (“is that what Christianity is now?”). Susan answered the practice question with evidence about trajectory (Lebanon). Each piece of evidence was real; each was aimed at a different target. The most useful thing this essay can do is separate the questions and weigh the evidence for each.


## 2. Michael’s Mirror Argument: What It Gets Right and Where It Breaks


Michael’s opening was a genuine argument, not merely a plea for tolerance. It had three parts: (a) extremists on both sides describe each other in mirror-image terms; (b) people who call for restricting Muslims supply Muslim extremists with the evidence they need that Christianity is hostile; (c) therefore the call for restriction is itself a form of the extremism it condemns.


**What it gets right.** Part (b) is supported by evidence. Jihadist recruitment literature has for decades used Western anti-Muslim rhetoric and policy as proof that the West is at war with Islam; the post-9/11 effort by President Bush to separate al-Qaeda from Islam was deliberately designed to deny that recruiting narrative, and U.S. counterterrorism officials across administrations have argued for it on those grounds. Part (a) is also partly true: extremist movements do tend to validate one another, and the dynamics of reciprocal radicalization are documented in the research literature on political violence.


**Where it breaks.** Part (c) depends on the claim that the two “ends of the spectrum” are equivalent — that a Christian who wants Muslims monitored is the mirror of a Muslim who wants Christians subjugated. That equivalence holds only if one ignores content. A mirror argument treats positions as symmetrical because each side accuses the other; but accusations are not self-validating in either direction. Whether a given accusation is accurate is the question, and the spectrum model cannot answer it, because it measures distance from the middle, not distance from the truth. This was precisely Thomas’s reply — “it resolves along the axis of truth” — and it is the decisive logical point of the first half of the conversation.


Michael’s rejoinder — that the axis of truth depends on where you place the origin — is the crux. If truth is simply “relative to the person determining it,” then no claim about Islam or Christianity can be adjudicated and coexistence is the only remaining value. But Michael did not actually hold that position consistently. He believed it was *true* that American mosques do not teach violence (an empirical claim he had tested by visiting), *true* that the Spanish killed most of the Arawaks, *true* that Afghan women suffer horribly. He relied on a shared axis of fact throughout. His own closing proposal — “start with the points of agreement, like two plus three is five, or today’s temperature” — is itself an appeal to a common, non-relative standard. The disagreement is therefore not whether there is an axis of truth but which facts are relevant and how they are weighted. That is a resolvable kind of disagreement.


A fair statement of the asymmetry test, which Thomas was reaching for, is this: *Does the tradition’s mainstream — its recognized scholars, its institutions, its states where it governs — currently teach and enforce coercion of outsiders or dissenters as a religious duty?* This test does not ask whether a tradition has ever done evil (all have), nor whether its extremists do evil (all traditions have extremists). It asks what the tradition’s living center authorizes now. Applied honestly, it neither exonerates Islam wholesale nor condemns it wholesale; it yields a differentiated answer, which Sections 4–6 develop.


## 3. “Western Values” and the Difference Between Values and Events


Michael’s catalog — conquest of the Americas, slavery, disenfranchisement of women, the Klan — was offered to show that “Western values” is a moving target, so that asking whether Islam is compatible with them is incoherent. Leonard’s reply — “none of those are values; those are things people did” — was brief but important, and Armond later developed the same distinction in the paired session (principles versus the behavior of those who claim them).


The distinction matters because the Western moral tradition’s own principles were the instruments of every one of those reforms. Las Casas denounced the conquest from the pulpit and before the Spanish crown on Christian grounds in the 1500s. The abolition movements in Britain and America were led overwhelmingly by evangelical Christians (Wilberforce, the Clapham Sect, the Quakers, Garrison’s circle) who argued from the *imago Dei* and the equality of souls before God; the Declaration’s “all men are created equal” was quoted against slavery by Lincoln and Douglass. The civil-rights movement was organized in black churches and argued from Scripture and natural law. In each case, the reform was a recovery of a principle against a practice that contradicted it.


That is the real meaning of “compatible with Western values”: not compatible with whatever Western people happened to do in 1520 or 1850, but compatible with the principles — the equal dignity of persons, the rule of law binding rulers, government by consent, freedom of conscience — by which the West reformed itself. Michael’s list, read this way, actually supports the relevance of the question rather than dissolving it: the West’s record shows that a civilization can carry a gap between principle and practice and close it, *provided the principle is present and authoritative*. The question for Islam then becomes: does its authoritative tradition contain principles by which its own coercive practices can be reformed from within, and are those principles gaining or losing authority? That is a much better question than “is Islam good or bad,” and it is the one the rest of this essay pursues.


Michael was right on the narrower point that the amendments are part of the Constitution. Article V says so explicitly. His use of this point also cut against his own relativism in an instructive way: the amendments abolishing slavery and enfranchising women were not arbitrary shifts in taste but corrections toward principles already stated in the founding documents. They are an example of the self-correcting mechanism described above.


## 4. Is Islam One Thing?


Michael insisted that “Islam” is not one thing — Sunni and Shia, Sufi, the schools of law, national cultures — just as “Christianity” spans Unitarians, Mormons, Quakers, and Catholics. This is correct and it matters, but it does not settle the question, because diversity at the level of sect does not by itself tell us what the shared authoritative sources teach.


The main dimensions of variation are:


- **Sect.** Roughly 85–90% of Muslims are Sunni and 10–13% Shia (most of these Twelvers). Smaller groups include Ibadis (Oman) and the Ahmadiyya (who are persecuted as non-Muslims in Pakistan).

- **Law school.** Sunni jurisprudence has four surviving schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi’i, Hanbali); Shia jurisprudence is mainly Ja’fari. On apostasy, blasphemy, and the status of non-Muslims, the *classical* positions of all these schools were broadly similar: apostasy was a capital offense for adult males, and non-Muslims under Muslim rule held a protected but subordinate status (*dhimma*) involving the *jizya* tax and various legal disabilities.

- **Modern movements.** Modernist and reformist scholars (Muhammad Abduh in the 19th century; Fazlur Rahman, Abdullahi an-Na’im, Khaled Abou El Fadl in the 20th–21st) argue for reinterpretation; Salafi and Wahhabi currents argue for return to early practice; Islamist movements (the Muslim Brotherhood, Jamaat-e-Islami, the Iranian revolution) seek to make sharia the law of the state; jihadist movements add revolutionary violence.

- **State practice.** According to Pew’s 2019 survey of laws, apostasy was a criminal offense in about 22 countries and blasphemy in about 79 worldwide (the latter including some non-Muslim countries), with the harshest penalties concentrated in Muslim-majority states. Many Muslim-majority states — Indonesia, Senegal, Tunisia, Albania, Kazakhstan, Bosnia — govern largely under secular or mixed civil law.


So the honest answer to “is Islam one thing?” is that its sources and classical law are substantially shared, its interpretations and state practices are highly diverse, and the global distribution of opinion (see the Pew data in the Part One notes) includes both very large majorities for sharia in some countries and small minorities in others. Charlie’s claim that support for sharia is not confined to “a few radicals” is correct as a global statement. Michael’s claim that American Muslims do not hold those views is largely supported by U.S. survey data. Both men were describing real populations; they were describing different ones.


## 5. The Textual Question: Surah 9:5 and the Problem of Normative Violence


Susan made the strongest single argument of the session in one sentence: in the New Testament one will not find a command to kill people over belief; in the Quran one will. Michael agreed with the first half. The second half needs precision, because it is where the asymmetry between the traditions actually lies — and it is not quite where the conversation placed it.


**The Christian case.** The Old Testament contains commands of warfare and destruction (e.g., Deuteronomy 7 and 20, concerning the nations of Canaan), which Thomas himself later cited in the paired session. Christians did at times use these texts to justify violence — the Crusades, the Inquisition, the wars of religion, colonial conquest. What made the renunciation of those uses possible and, eventually, nearly universal within Christianity is structural: the New Testament presents the Old Testament conquest commands as bound to a specific time and people, and it gives the church no civil law code and no sword. “My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight” (John 18:36). The apostolic instruction is submission to civil authority, love of enemies, and persuasion (Romans 12–13; 2 Corinthians 10:4, “the weapons of our warfare are not carnal”). When Christians coerced belief, they had to argue *against* the plain teaching of the founder; the reformers who opposed coercion had the founding texts on their side.


**The Islamic case.** Islam’s founding period includes Muhammad as both prophet and statesman — lawgiver, judge, and military commander in Medina. The Quran contains both conciliatory verses (“there is no compulsion in religion,” 2:256; “to you your religion and to me mine,” 109:6) and verses commanding fighting (9:5, 9:29, 2:191, 8:39). The classical jurists, working from these texts and the prophetic example, built a comprehensive law (*sharia* as elaborated in *fiqh*) that governs the state as well as the individual, including rules for warfare, for the status of non-Muslims, and for apostasy. Many classical jurists held that the later Medinan verses abrogated the earlier conciliatory ones. This means that a Muslim reformer who argues against coercion must argue against a large part of the classical legal tradition — though he can appeal to other texts within the Quran itself and to the historical-context reading of the war verses.


This is the precise asymmetry. It is not that Islam’s texts contain violence and Christianity’s do not; the Bible contains a great deal of violence. It is that **Christianity’s founding texts subordinate the sword to the gospel and locate coercion outside the church’s mission, while Islam’s founding texts and classical law integrate religion, law, and state power, so that the question of coercion is internal to the religion’s law.** Susan’s intuition was right; the reason it is right is this structural difference, which is more defensible than a verse-count comparison.


**What this does not prove.** It does not prove that most Muslims, or most Muslim scholars today, hold the classical expansionist positions. Many do not. It proves that the reformist position must be argued against a heavier weight of inherited authority, and that it is therefore more fragile — which is exactly why its advance or retreat is worth tracking (see Section 8 and, in the paired file, the discussion of Muslim renunciations).


Michael’s plan to take Surah 9:5 to his imam is a good one. He is likely to hear the contextual reading (treaty-breaking tribes; see 9:4 and 9:6). That reading is held in good faith by many scholars. The fair follow-up question is not whether the contextual reading exists — it does — but whether the classical abrogation reading has been explicitly repudiated by the authorities his imam recognizes, and what those authorities teach about apostasy and about the civil status of non-Muslims in a Muslim-majority state.


## 6. The Demographic Thesis: Charlie and Susan


Charlie’s and Susan’s claim is that Muslim communities remain peaceful as minorities and become coercive as majorities, so that present peaceful behavior is not evidence of future peaceful behavior. This is a serious claim and deserves serious evidence on both sides.


**Evidence for.** (1) Historically, when Muslim polities conquered or came to dominate Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and Hindu populations, the dhimma system followed, and over centuries those populations declined, through conversion under legal and fiscal pressure and at times through violence (e.g., the gradual Islamization of Egypt, Anatolia, and Persia; periodic massacres; the Armenian, Assyrian, and Greek genocides at the end of the Ottoman Empire, which had religious as well as nationalist dimensions). (2) In modern cases where Christians became minorities in states with rising Islamist politics — Iraq, Syria, parts of Nigeria, Pakistan, Egypt — Christians have faced violence and emigration; Iraq’s Christian population fell from roughly 1.5 million in 2003 to perhaps a quarter of that or less. (3) Lebanon, Susan’s example, did shift from a Christian plurality to a Muslim majority, and the power-sharing arrangement collapsed into civil war. (4) Survey data (Pew 2013) show that in many Muslim-majority countries, majorities support sharia as state law, and among those, many support severe penalties for apostasy.


**Evidence against or complicating.** (1) Some Muslim-majority states are stable constitutional or semi-constitutional orders with substantial religious freedom — Senegal (about 95% Muslim, whose first president, Léopold Senghor, was a Catholic), Albania, Kosovo, Bosnia’s Muslim community, Indonesia’s Pancasila constitution (though Indonesia has blasphemy prosecutions and regional sharia in Aceh). (2) Lebanon’s war was not a simple demographic takeover; it involved an armed foreign (Palestinian) presence, Syrian and Israeli intervention, and atrocities by Christian militias as well as against them. (3) Where Christian populations declined sharply in recent decades, war and state collapse (Iraq after 2003, Syria after 2011) were the proximate causes, and jihadist groups were the principal perpetrators — groups that also massacred Muslims. (4) In the West, second- and third-generation Muslims show measurable movement toward host-country norms on many questions, though the pace varies and some European surveys show persistent gaps on questions such as homosexuality and the relation of religious to civil law.


**Assessment.** The historical record supports the claim that classical Islamic governance, when implemented, placed non-Muslims under subordinate legal status and that this status often eroded those communities over time. It supports the claim that Islamist movements, where they gain power, restrict or persecute religious minorities. It does not establish a law that *any* Muslim population, on becoming a majority, will turn coercive, because there are counterexamples, and because the outcome appears to depend heavily on which interpretation of Islam has institutional authority, on the strength of the civil state, and on regional conflict. The demographic thesis is best stated not as a law but as a risk that rises with the authority of Islamist interpretations and falls with the authority of reformist ones. That formulation is both more defensible and more useful, because it points to something that can be measured and influenced.


For the United States specifically, the numbers are relevant. Pew estimated about 3.45 million Muslims in the U.S. in 2017 (roughly 1.1% of the population), projected to reach roughly 2% by 2040–2050. A national Muslim majority is not a realistic scenario in any foreseeable period. Local concentrations (Dearborn and Hamtramck, Michigan; parts of Minneapolis; the Texas case Leonard raised) are the practical sites where the questions arise. Hamtramck’s all-Muslim city council’s 2023 vote to bar Pride flags on city property is often cited by both sides: by some as evidence that majority Muslim communities impose their norms, and by others as ordinary democratic politics within constitutional limits (the ban applied to flags on government property only, and was framed as a neutral rule allowing only national, state, and certain other official flags).


## 7. Rule of Law as the Common Ground: Leonard’s Line in the Sand


Leonard offered the most promising point of agreement, and Michael accepted it immediately: whatever anyone believes, everyone in the United States must conform to the rule of constitutional law, and whatever violates that law is not permitted. Michael and Leonard agreed on the danger of majority tyranny, on representative government, and on constitutional limits; their disagreement over “democracy” versus “republic” was largely terminological (see the note in Part One).


This common ground has concrete content in existing law:


- **Free exercise is not a license for conduct that violates general law.** In *Reynolds v. United States* (1879), the Supreme Court upheld the prohibition of polygamy against a Latter-day Saint’s free-exercise claim, distinguishing belief (protected absolutely) from conduct (subject to neutral laws). *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990) confirmed that neutral, generally applicable laws apply regardless of religious motive, while the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (1993) and later cases restored heightened protection for religious exercise against federal burdens. No religious law — Islamic, Christian, or Jewish — can override criminal law, constitutional rights, or family-law protections.

- **Religious arbitration** is permitted only within the limits of ordinary arbitration law: parties must consent, and courts will not enforce outcomes that violate public policy or constitutional rights. Several states passed “anti-sharia” or “foreign law” statutes around 2010–2017. Oklahoma’s amendment, which named sharia explicitly, was struck down in *Awad v. Ziriax* (10th Cir. 2012) for discriminating against a particular religion; later statutes framed neutrally (“foreign law that violates constitutional rights”) have generally survived.

- **Advocacy of overthrow** of the government is already a bar to naturalization (8 U.S.C. §1424), and incitement to imminent lawless action is unprotected speech (*Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 1969).


Leonard’s line in the sand therefore already exists in law. The real disagreement is not over whether the constitutional order binds everyone — everyone in the conversation agreed it does — but over whether (a) that general law is sufficient protection, and (b) whether particular belief systems should be singled out for additional scrutiny. Thomas’s essay of the prior week answered (b) yes; Michael answered no. That is where the next round of the conversation (in the paired session file) takes up the question, with Thomas’s proposal of a renunciation requirement for naturalization.


Leonard’s distinction between the Constitution as a restriction on the federal government and the Constitution as a grant to the people is broadly correct and is the classical reading (*Federalist* No. 84, where Hamilton argued a bill of rights was unnecessary because the government had only enumerated powers). His claim that the document never acts on individuals needs the qualification given in the Part One note: acting directly on individuals rather than only on states was one of the Constitution’s defining innovations over the Articles of Confederation.


## 8. “Would They Tell You?” — The Problem of Evidence and Sincerity


Charlie’s question to Michael — “Do you think they would tell you?” — is the epistemological center of the entire dispute, and it recurs in the paired session as Thomas’s discussion of *taqiyya*. It deserves careful treatment because it can function either as a reasonable caution or as an unfalsifiable claim.


As a caution it is reasonable. Visitors to a congregation hear what is said to visitors. Surveys depend on candor. Any community under suspicion has an incentive to present its most moderate face. Michael’s evidence (personal visits) is real but limited in the way all anecdotal evidence is limited.


As an unfalsifiable claim it becomes a problem: if peaceful words are evidence of peacefulness, and peaceful words are also evidence of concealment, then no possible evidence could count against the hypothesis. A claim that no evidence can refute cannot be established by evidence either; it can only be assumed. Michael’s reply — “would Christians tell you?” — was aimed at exactly this point: the extremists on every side tend to announce themselves, and most people are not concealing a program but simply living their lives.


The resolution is to look for evidence that is costly to fake: (1) what institutions teach their own members in their own languages (curricula, Friday sermons, fatwas, textbooks, which are increasingly documented by researchers on all sides); (2) behavior over time, especially behavior when concealment would be advantageous; (3) public repudiations that carry a real cost to the one who makes them; and (4) how communities treat their own dissenters and apostates. These are observable. They produce a differentiated picture — some institutions and communities pass, others do not — rather than a verdict on 1.9 billion people.


## 9. Mainstream Christianity and the Question Thomas Kept Asking


Thomas’s repeated question — “Is that what Christianity is now? Did it endure as a cultural mandate?” — was the right question, and Michael did not answer it directly. Michael’s answer (“the same way Islam does”) asserted symmetry without addressing the test. The test has a clear answer for Christianity: no Christian denomination of any size today teaches the coercion of belief, the execution of heretics, or the conquest of non-Christian peoples as a religious duty; the major bodies have explicitly repudiated those practices (the Catholic Church’s *Dignitatis Humanae* at Vatican II in 1965 affirmed religious freedom as a right grounded in human dignity; John Paul II’s apology of 2000; the Southern Baptist Convention’s 1995 resolution on slavery and racism; the formal renunciations of the Klan by denominations).


Michael’s strongest point in this exchange was about the *past*: Christian Europe’s history is full of Christians killing Christians, each side claiming God. That is true, and it is the historical origin of the Western doctrine of religious liberty — which was forged in large part by Christians (Roger Williams, the Baptists, John Locke’s *Letter Concerning Toleration*, William Penn) out of the experience of the wars of religion, and argued from Christian premises: that faith coerced is not faith, and that the magistrate has no commission from Christ over conscience. Michael’s question — “is the underlying principle that because my belief is right, I kill you?” — was answered, within Christianity, by Christians, over the course of the 17th and 18th centuries: no, because the gospel itself forbids it. That answer is the reason Thomas can truthfully say the Inquisition is not what Christianity is now.


The question the Christian tradition’s history poses to Islam is whether an equivalent internal answer is available and is gaining authority. That is an open and live question; the paired session file surveys the evidence both ways.


## 10. The Coda: Local Concentrations, Public Symbols, and Equal Treatment


Leonard’s closing observations raised the equal-treatment question in a concrete form: Muslims obtain permits for amplified calls to prayer, while a Christian group that gathers is labeled a “compound.” Two points follow.


First, on public symbols, the constitutional rule is neutrality: if church bells are permitted under a noise ordinance, the call to prayer must be treated the same way, and vice versa. A community that wants neither may regulate both by volume and hour. The real grievance many people have is not unequal law but unequal public sympathy — a sense that expressions of Christian identity are treated as threatening while those of minority religions are treated as diversity. That perception is widespread, and it is a legitimate subject of cultural argument; but the legal remedy is consistent neutrality, not a reciprocal restriction.


Second, the Waco comparison cuts in more than one direction. The government’s conduct there is widely criticized, and the use of loaded words such as “compound” to frame a religious community does shape public judgment — a point that applies equally to how Muslim communities are described. The Branch Davidian case also involved illegal weapons and credible abuse allegations, which is the kind of conduct-based evidence that justifies state action against any religious group. The principle that emerges is the same as in Section 7: act on conduct and on credible evidence of planned lawbreaking, and describe all communities in the same terms.


## 11. Resolutions Reached and Open Questions


**Points on which the participants agreed, explicitly or implicitly:**


- The constitutional order and the rule of law bind every resident regardless of religion (Leonard, Michael, Thomas, Susan).

- Majority tyranny is a real danger that the constitutional structure was designed to check (Leonard and Michael).

- The New Testament does not command killing over belief (Susan and Michael).

- The conditions of women in Afghanistan and under harsh sharia regimes are horrifying (Charlie and Michael).

- Past Christian atrocities — the conquest of the Americas, the Inquisition, the Klan — were wrong and are not endorsed by mainstream Christianity today (Thomas and Michael).

- Islam is internally diverse, and American Muslims as a population differ substantially from populations in several Muslim-majority countries (acknowledged by Charlie’s “lovely Muslim friends,” and by the survey evidence).


**Points still in dispute:**


- Whether Islam’s authoritative sources, rightly read, require the subordination of non-Muslims and the fusion of religion and state (Thomas, Susan, Charlie: yes; Michael: no, or not as practiced in the U.S.).
