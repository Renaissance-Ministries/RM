# The Revolutionary and the Devotee


### Meeting the Threat of Islam to Liberal Democracy

*Fellowship Discussion Essay | 12 September 2026*

*For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father.* — **Romans 8:15**

**Occasion.** On July 7, 2026, PragerU published [Episode 39 of *The Jeremy Boreing Show*](https://www.prageru.com/videos/a-former-muslim-exposes-lies-about-islam-or-ep-39), a long conversation between Boreing and Danny Burmawi titled “A Former Muslim Exposes Lies About Islam.” Burmawi was born in Jordan, lived his first nineteen years as a Muslim, spent a year as a zealous defender of the faith, left it after reading its own sources, became a Christian, lived fifteen years in Lebanon, and now leads the Ideological Defense Institute (IDI) and has written *Islam, Israel and the West: A Former Muslim’s Analysis*. The 2-hour interview offers an informative first-person account of living as a Muslim. Burmawi argues that Islam is not a religion among religions, but a civilizational order that contains a religion. He makes that case from the inside, as a man who paid for his knowledge by living the life and learning from it. You can watch it at [https://www.prageru.com/videos/a-former-muslim-exposes-lies-about-islam-or-ep-39](https://www.prageru.com/videos/a-former-muslim-exposes-lies-about-islam-or-ep-39).

This essay is exceptionally long because I address each major idea in the recording and analyze the concepts in depth in Section V (I recommend reading Section V if you don’t want to spend time reading a 23,000-word essay). The interview features Burmawi and PragerU’s Jeremy Boreing. I paraphrased the interview; please listen for the full detail and texture of who Danny Burmawi is and his experience of living in Islam. I agree with most of the ideas and programs Burmawi proposes, but my proposal is for a more aggressive transformation of American culture and the Western church (as a result, it is less likely to be implemented, but I believe it is where the cure lies). I think the foundation is transforming the accepted assumptions about what Islam is: realizing that it is a religion intertwined with a political system that is incompatible with our Constitutional Republic. The examination touches current events and public controversy — Tucker Carlson, Zohran Mamdani, Canadian Islamophobia legislation, and Israel. We examine doctrine — grace, sanctification, the sanctification of Muhammad, the nature of the Islamic Jesus, dispensationalism and its critics. We confront practical, theological, ontological, and relational implications.

The order of the ideas confronted below is mine, not the interview’s.

## I. Triage: What the Interview Asserts and What We May Assert After It

Burmawi spoke for almost 2 hours without notes, and he made a few factual errors. I shall note those first. **Proverbs 18:17** tells us what to do about it: *He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him.* The following are points that may be contested in Burmawi’s story.

**Tier one — public and verifiable.** The Islamic conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries took the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, and Spain from a Christian world; Aramaic, the language of Jesus, gave way to Arabic across the region over the following centuries; Jerash, Burmawi’s home city, was a city of the Decapolis and is today a ruin visited by tourists. The Ottoman *devşirme* — the levy of Christian boys for conversion and service — is documented history. Hagia Sophia in Istanbul was a church for nine centuries, a mosque for five, a museum for eighty-six years, and has been a mosque again since 2020, with its Christian mosaics curtained during prayers. Jordanian shari’a courts have, in documented cases, treated conversion from Islam as apostasy with civil consequences: marriage annulled, custody lost, inheritance forfeited. The statue of Christ in Fuheis, Jordan’s Christian-majority town, was erected by the local youth club in January 2023 and removed by the municipality the next day after a social-media outcry, and relocated to a Christian cemetery; the Jordanian King visited the town weeks later to affirm coexistence. Zohran Mamdani is mayor of New York; his father Mahmood Mamdani wrote *Good Muslim, Bad Muslim* (2004). John Calvin approved Servetus’s execution in 1553. The Aisha tradition Burmawi cites is in Sahih al-Bukhari, and it says what he says it says: she remarked that his Lord seemed to hasten to fulfill his wishes. The hadith about expelling Jews and Christians from the Arabian Peninsula is in Sahih Muslim. The account of Jesus asking a companion to be substituted for him at the crucifixion appears in classical commentaries on Qur’an 4:157—al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir—not in the Qur’an itself, which says only that “it was made to appear so to them.” Increase Mather’s *The Mystery of Israel’s Salvation* (1669) argued for a future national conversion of the Jews, two centuries before Darby.

**Tier two — informed interpretation.** The reading of Qur’an 9:29 as an unbounded command, the three-stage pattern of Islamic expansion, the claim that Islamic soteriology is structurally fear-based, the argument that Islam is a total order rather than a religion, the analysis of the red-green alliance, the characterization of Mamdani’s commitments, the assessment that the West’s post-9/11 accommodation accelerated Islamic growth are all assertions that need to be examined more deeply to ensure these statements of fact are well grounded in evidence.

**Tier three — held loosely.** Four items need correction or caution.

First, Burmawi paraphrases Qur’an 9:29 as a command to *kill* unbelievers until they pay the *jizya*. The verb in 9:29 is *qātilū* — fight — not *uqtulū* — kill; the verse reads, in the standard translations, fight those who do not believe in Allah and the Last Day, and do not hold as forbidden what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, and do not acknowledge the religion of truth, from among the People of the Book, until they pay the *jizya* with willing submission and feel themselves subdued. The verse that says *kill* is Qur’an 9:5, and it is addressed to the polytheists, not the People of the Book. A command to *make war on Christians and Jews until they submit to a humiliating tax* is the more accurate reading, and it is the reading that has governed Islamic jurisprudence on the *dhimma* for thirteen centuries.

Second, the traditions in Bukhari say that a woman was punished for confining a cat until it starved, and that a prostitute was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog; a separate tradition says that keeping a dog other than for hunting or guarding diminishes one’s reward daily. Burmawi reverses the animals and inflates the penalties. More important, the actual traditions do not support his point that Islamic merit is random and chaotic — they read as ordinary moral teaching about cruelty and mercy toward animals. His broader argument that the only clear trajectory to assurance is martyrdom rests on other ground.

Third, the casualty figures — eighty-six million dead in India, a possible total of three hundred eighty million across the history of Islamic conquest — are from a contested literature (K. S. Lal’s demographic estimates for India; Bill Warner’s aggregate). They are not fabrications; they are upper-bound reconstructions. The conquest was vast and murderous by any accounting.

Fourth, I could not confirm reports that Tucker Carlson bought a home in Qatar. In December 2025, he said he was going to buy one, but I could not verify whether he completed the purchase. Nothing in this essay depends on it.

Two additional items to qualify: Burmawi says there have been sixty-five thousand Islamic terror attacks in recent decades; the database (through Dr. Gad Saad) counted about forty-nine thousand since September 2001. The exact number isn’t significant, since they’re the same order of magnitude. And the anecdote in which Pascal, asked by the king for evidence of God, answers that the Jews are the evidence: the anecdote is apocryphal in that form (it circulates attached to several names), but the substance is authentically Pascal — the *Pensées* devote extended sections to the survival of the Jewish people as a standing proof of Providence. The IDI essay Burmawi commends should be read as a meditation on Pascal’s actual argument, not as a transcript of a conversation.

Having given my opinion of what I believe were errors, let us examine Burmawi’s ideas.

## II. Fourteen Centuries and the Curtained Mosaics

Burmawi opens with the story that Tucker Carlson and his guests have treated the phrase *Islamic jihad* as a manufactured narrative — something invented by people with an agenda. Burmawi answers with fourteen centuries of history. Two-thirds of the ancient Christian world occupied. Languages replaced. Civilizations reduced to ruins that only tourists visit. The *dhimma*. The *devşirme*. Churches turned into mosques, of which Hagia Sophia is the last great one, where he stood under the ceiling and saw the Virgin and the apostles curtained off because one cannot pray in a mosque with Christian icons visible, yet they cannot be destroyed because the world’s heritage authorities forbid it.

The mosaics are under curtains, not chiseled off. That is the West’s exact condition today. Our Christian past is not destroyed; it is covered. The law, the universities, the hospitals, the *imago Dei* that Burmawi correctly names as the foundation of the human-rights declarations that even non-Christian nations sign — all of it is still on the ceiling. We simply do not look up. And a different worship has moved into the room beneath it.

A scripture belongs here. The seven churches of Revelation 2 and 3 were in what is now Turkey. Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamos, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea — every one of them is today in a Muslim-majority nation, and the Lord’s word to the first of them was: *Remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works; or else I will come unto thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his place, except thou repent* **(Revelation 2:5)**. The candlesticks of Asia Minor were removed. Not by Islam’s strength alone; Islam was the instrument. They were removed because the churches did not do the first works. Burmawi’s history lesson is true: conquest does not happen to a church that is on fire. It happens to a church that has become a museum of its own past — mosaics on the ceiling, curtains beneath.

## III. The Revelation of Convenience

Burmawi’s testimony centers on text. He became devout in 2006 to defend Muhammad against the Danish cartoons. He describes the propaganda he was raised on — that an astronaut heard the call to prayer on the moon, that a Palestinian invented the computer and the Americans killed him for it — and the sermon by a Kuwaiti imam that moved him to tears, recounting how the Companions tore a poetess in half between two camels for mocking the Prophet. He read the Qur’an, the *Sira*, and the Hadith not to test Islam but to master it. And he found that whenever Muhammad needed something, Gabriel arrived with a revelation to provide it—settling a quarrel with a wife, granting permission to marry his adopted son’s former wife by abolishing adoption. And he found that Aisha had noticed it first, and said so, and that her remark is preserved in the most authoritative collection Islam possesses.

The Torah gives a test for prophets: *When a prophet speaketh in the name of the LORD, if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken, but the prophet hath spoken it presumptuously: thou shalt not be afraid of him* **(Deuteronomy 18:22)**. That is the falsification test; it is Popper in the Pentateuch. But the biblical record supplies a second, subtler test, never stated as a rule and everywhere present as a pattern: **genuine revelation of God rebukes the man who receives it.** Nathan walks into David’s court and says *Thou art the man* **(2 Samuel 12:7)**, and the king, who could have had him killed, writes Psalm 51 instead. Peter, the rock, denies the Lord three times, and the Gospels, his own church’s preserved record, record every denial. Paul writes that *when Peter was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed* **(Galatians 2:11)**, and the church canonized the letter. Moses is barred from the land. Jonah sulks. The prophets are, almost without exception, men whom the Word crushes before it uses. A revelation that arrives only to gratify its recipient, that never once tells him he is wrong, that adjusts the moral law of the universe to resolve his domestic difficulties — that is not the signature of the God of Israel. It is the signature of a man.

Burmawi’s friend saw it, and Burmawi saw it, and Aisha saw it fourteen centuries ago. The test does not require hostility to Islam. It requires only reading Islam’s own books with the question the Torah taught the world to ask.

## IV. The Judge, the Shahada, and What a Religious Test Is

Burmawi tells of his Jordanian friend who converted to Christianity, was sued by his father for apostasy, was jailed, lost his wife and children and civil standing, and was offered a way out by a humane judge: recite the *shahada*, say the words, and everything is restored; the state does not care about your heart. The friend, broken, said the words. Years later his whole household became Christian. Burmawi’s verdict on the judge is that he was a decent man *despite* Islam, not because of it.

That courtroom was a religious test—the thing Article VI, Clause 3 of our Constitution forbids—enacted as the ordinary operation of law. In my April 18 essay on the Kingdom of God and the kingdoms of men ([https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/04/18/the-kingdom-of-god-and-kingdoms-of-men/](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/04/18/the-kingdom-of-god-and-kingdoms-of-men/)), I distinguished the *religious test* the Founders prohibited — a legal requirement that you profess something to hold office or civil standing — from *testimony*, the voluntary declaration of allegiance that the free-exercise clause protects and that Christ commands. The Jordanian court is the religious test in its purest form: say the creed or lose your wife. And the whole Western tradition of liberty of conscience, which the secular world imagines it invented, is in fact the Christian recognition that a creed recited to keep one’s children is not faith and cannot be, that *choose you this day whom ye will serve* **(Joshua 24:15)** presupposes the liberty to choose otherwise.

I add a pastoral word. Did the friend deny Christ? Christ said, *whosoever shall deny me before men, him will I also deny before my Father which is in heaven* **(Matthew 10:33)**. But the first to hear those words was Peter, and Peter denied Him, and was restored on a beach at breakfast. Matthew 10 is a summons to courage, not a sentence on the coerced. The Jordanian convert did not deny Christ; he was robbed, and the robber’s demand was a sentence in Arabic. The friend’s household is Christian today. I am not the one who will tell him he failed.

## V. Islam is not Just a Religion


The points in this section are central to Burmawi’s thesis. Burmawi’s solution to the problem of Islamic immigration is for the State Department to examine Islam’s theology on the grounds that Islam is a subversive ideology which is antithetical to the existence of the Constitutional Republic. Boreing raises the obvious objection to Burmawi’s demand for a State Department program to examine Islamic theology because America has freedom of religion and the State does not critique theologies. Burmawi answers this objection with a reframing of what Islam is: “Islam is not a religion; Islam has a religion.” His point is that Islam should not be considered to be only a religion because its beliefs, practices, and doctrines extend beyond a man’s personal relationship with God and into the domain of the State.


He likens Islam to a house with a sofa in one room. The room with the sofa is the religion that Islam has; the political, state-forming doctrine of Islam is a completely different room in the house. Just as liberal democracy and communism are political ideologies separate from Christianity and atheism, Islam has a political ideology that is separate from religious Islam. He claims the two have been combined: the sacred/theological/religious program has been joined to the political ideology, and by that joining the whole has evaded scrutiny by the State Department as a subversive group that should be monitored. Burmawi makes the distinction that Islam contains aspects of practice and belief that are clearly religious in the conventional sense of what a religion is, including prescriptions for worship, prayer, fasting, alms, pilgrimage, creed, etc. But he notes that another aspect of Islam contains prescriptions for establishing a political, legal, military, and civilizational program whose stated end is the rule of Allah’s law over all mankind. All of Islam is called a religion, but inside that definition has been smuggled a program of societal-governmental overthrow to replace our Constitutional Republic with its own form of governmental order.


Such a move requires an etymological pause to examine the validity of this distinction: Religio—Cicero’s derivation is *relegere*, to read again, to be scrupulous; Lactantius’s is *religare*, to bind fast. Either way, the word “religion” names a relation between the soul and the divine and the practices that express that relation. Our First Amendment protects the free exercise of *that *relation. It does not protect a program to replace the Constitution, even if that program of replacement of governmental system gives itself the label of being a “religion.”


My perspective is broader; I hold that our relationship with God is not confined to prayer, fasting, and other rituals, beliefs, and doctrines about the relationship between God and man. My thesis is that man’s relationship with God is total – there is nothing, no action, no thought, no experience, no substance, no transaction that is separated in its participation with the very essence of God. From this universal perspective, one could argue that every act of life should be considered sacred and subject to the prescriptions of one’s religion. This is my stand. This means that Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Buddhism, and Hinduism are likewise combinations of the sacred and secular, and that the distinction between the two is functional and practical rather than intrinsic and fully separable.


The State is charged with regulating men’s external affairs, ensuring that men do not violate the rights given by God. The church is charged with teaching revealed knowledge about man’s relationship with God and conduct that pleases Him; nurturing and awakening the hunger for the divine and transforming the soul into His image; and teaching the limits and boundaries of relationships with fellow man. But the functions of the church are not fully distinct from the functions and purview of the State; regulating relationships between men is the function of the State, and the standard by which those relationships are judged is a moral judgment, which comes from some belief structure, preferably one that is true and optimal and revealed by God.


Because the church-state relationship depends on the God-man relationship, no strict/absolute/inviolable definition of the separation of church and state can be drawn. Acknowledging this unavoidable overlap is necessary to move this debate out of deadlock. Given that the State’s existence is authorized by God (Romans 13), the State must ensure its own survival. To do so, the State must examine the contents of every religion to understand the patterns of thought and teaching that each religion intends/desires/programs its adherents to impress upon the State. This universal sacred-secular unity makes it unavoidable for the State to examine every religion; it must do so to evaluate the threat its doctrines and practices pose to the State’s continued existence and order.


If a religion includes, as part of its central doctrines, beliefs and doctrines that are intrinsically different from the established political order, and if the practice of that religion advocates establishing that political order, then the State should scrutinize that program. The State should not prohibit or otherwise interfere with church indoctrination, but it should monitor the political activities of such groups to ensure they do not violate the criminal code; prosecuting unlawful conduct is the express purpose of the State. In short, the State should know the doctrine and monitor the activities of every religious group, especially those whose express doctrine includes overthrow of the established State. Again, such monitoring and intervening in unlawful actions should be considered within the purview of the State’s supervisory authority.


Burmawi argued that Islam’s program to replace the Constitution is not religious activity, as it is not a relation between a soul and God, and that Islam is instead a political organization and therefore should be monitored by the state. I disagree with his argument only to the extent that I believe every thought, word, and deed is intrinsically sacred. Everything we do is unavoidably a relation between soul and God. We must make the division between religious and political on other grounds. Some thoughts, words, and deeds are relations between the soul and the State, and they are likewise included in the soul’s relation to God. The workable division is functional: conduct between man and man falls under the State’s jurisdiction, whatever its religious motive; the State should not judge or enforce a particular relation between a man and God.


Burmawi used the example of communism to illustrate his point: if the Party declared Marx a prophet and the *Manifesto* holy writ, no serious person would grant Marxism-Leninism free-exercise protection to practice the precepts of its religion, which was seizing power. He argues that Islam contains, within its sacred texts and across its various sects, varying degrees of state-organizing doctrine. Islam is heterogeneous in this regard, from the Sufis to the Twelvers, each with its own political philosophy and state-influencing aspiration and program. As such, the State should monitor each group’s activities. The State is not prohibited from monitoring its citizens’ activities to identify threats to its existence.


As a normative claim (what ought to be done), the State has no right to take preemptive action against lawful acts (although this is not true in practice, as per TSA inspections). The concern is a Minority Report, pre-crime prosecution of ideas, thoughts, and intentions. This prohibition must be strictly enforced. The concern is Franklin’s warning of trading a liberty for a little present security. But neither a fully blind state that takes no action until the revolution has begun nor a total-surveillance state enforcing the current order is appropriate. The State should not interfere with lawful lobbying, promotion, rallying, educating, and organizing for peaceful demonstration and campaign, regardless of the speech, but it should monitor such efforts. The nation should be aware of the agendas and programs of the various factions and their proposed changes.


The Founders wrote the Free Exercise clause into the First Amendment to prevent the state from imposing a form of worship on people. This is entirely proper given that each man is responsible for forming his soul into God’s image. The State’s role is to judge violations of the relationship between man and man, not between God and man. The form of worship, doctrine, and belief about the proper way to shape the soul in God’s perfect image is the responsiblity of every man, and the State should not interfere forcefully with that relationship. But the state, as God’s representative, should guide and direct us in our relationships with one another, and as stated before, that standard of relationship is the substance of the teaching/revelation provided by the church and its underlying Scripture/teachings/revelations.


We must recognize that both “religion” and “Islamophobia” are being used to shield Islam from the supervision of the political portion of its program.


**Religion:** Free exercise of religion means the state may not forbid a man from praying, fasting, teaching his children his creed, building a mosque with his own money, wearing what his faith prescribes, changing his religion, or peacefully persuading others to change theirs.


 	- It does not mean that a legal system in which apostates lose their children is a protected religious practice.

 	- It does not mean that an organized effort to substitute its concept of the law of a god for the Constitution is beyond surveillance and scrutiny/examination by the State.

 	- It does not mean that demanding punishment for criticism of a prophet is a protected religious practice.

 	- It does not mean the state must not monitor a religion’s activities, study its texts, and know a movement’s goals, methods, and ends with regard to the State. It must not interfere with non-violent and lawful activities, but it is not required to maintain ignorance of them as a protected/forbidden domain of State security. Of course, such activity can lead to abuse, and this analysis examines the limits to determine what constitutes overreach. This intersects with issues of privacy and the interests of State survival. Monitoring lawful subversive activity serves the State’s interests, but the State may counter only unlawful subversive activity. Free speech is what is at issue.

 	- The question is: do we have the right to be anonymous and unwatched? Does monitoring a group’s public expression of doctrine violate the Fourth Amendment? Does monitoring the public pronouncements/agendas/goals of a religion fall outside the boundary of the right to be free from unreasonable search and seizure and the security of one’s papers and possessions?


**Islamophobia** is a word whose form, as Burmawi notes, was chosen to attach the *ideology* to the *persons*, so that criticism of Islam becomes equated with hatred of the person. To disentangle this, we must tell the truth about Islam while using the Christian standard of love. Contempt for Muslims is a violation of the Great Commandment to love neighbor as self. But, as Christians, we are under no compulsion to love an ideology or political philosophy, and we are commanded to hate evil. *“Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good.”* **(Romans 12:9)** The Islamic goal is to overthrow the worship of the God of the Bible and replace that with the worship of Allah, who is another god (i.e., the revelation recorded in the Qur’an of their god’s commands is not the same as the Bible’s revelation; thus the character and person/being of Allah and YHVH are distinct). Thus, following the program of Islam regarding State and person-relationship is a violation of the First Commandment. Thus, criticism of Islam is the ordinary criticism of a system of ideas. Such expression is the ordinary exercise of the mind God gave us, and it falls within the bounds of protected speech.

**The remedy, and the objection to it.** The remedy Burmawi raises is one that I have considered, and is perhaps the most contentious of his solutions. He asks the State Department to judge Islamic theology as subversive, revolutionary, and anti-constitutional. Of course, a government that judges the merits of religious principles and practices is judging a religion. The problem is that the government’s judgments reflect the sentiments and biases of the bureaucrats and officials charged with State security, and as staffing changes, the officials may use that policy and authority against Christianity. Religion need not be judged as good or bad. Burmawi’s point is that Islam is an organization that seeks to overthrow our Constitutional Republic and replace it with Sharia (in some of Islam’s various sects). To do so would fundamentally change the nature of the Republic. Christianity is arguably the foundation of the Constitutional Republic we have inherited from our Founders, and incorporating Islam into American jurisprudence, politics, and culture would move our jurisprudence and legislative standards radically away from the Founding principles. If we allow Islam to grow within our borders by fertility, immigration, conversion, and political competition, we could see the same population and cultural shift as Europe has experienced over the last decades.

We know that Godly cultures can change and reject God over generations and with a change in leadership. No culture is immune. Even the Israelites, who saw God’s miracles firsthand, forsook His worship and sacrificed offerings to golden calves during a few weeks of their leader’s absence (Exodus 32:1-6). With a change of administration, we risk changing from a Biblically based society to an Islamic one by bureaucratic mandate. Giving government the power to grade doctrine by executive/bureaucratic order assumes the graders will stay righteous.

Implementing the program I propose requires a mature and Godly people—a people committed to the person and principles of the God of the Bible as the foundation of the Republic. My proposal goes deeper than establishing an administrative rule or enacting legislation. I ask only that the state be willing to declare the obvious, disregard the criticism, and endure accusations of being politically incorrect for doing so. I ask the government to enforce against this revolutionary ideology the same restrictions on immigration and citizenship that would be applied to any other person entering the country who holds a revolutionary ideology. **I do not ask the state to judge a person’s religious practices. I ask it to judge his allegiance.** The fact that Islam wears the clothing of a religion—prayer, prophet, book, spirit—does not mean it should be given the full protection of a religion. Islam has as much right to practice the rituals of its religion as Christians, given that we desire a nation that honors freedom of thought and conviction. But given the expansionist, nation-conquering, and people-submitting nature of Islam, and its integration of this political program with its religious beliefs, I ask for two things: that the spread of this ideology be opposed and its people won to Christianity, and that naturalization and Federal hiring be closed to those who hold a revolutionary ideology, whether communist or Islamist. Swearing loyalty to protect and defend the Constitution is meaningless unless it is held sincerely, and such allegiance is required for naturalization and Federal employment.

Such a restriction will not solve all the problems of having Islamic revolutionaries already within our borders, but openly enforcing the restriction on Islamists attempting naturalization and Federal employment lets the Islamists know that the leadership of the nation does not accept the Islamic program of replacement of our liberal democracy with Shari’a. It gives moral courage to patriots who are willing to stand against a push to accept the worship of foreign Gods by signaling the willingness of the government to defend and hold harmless those who stand against the incorporation of Islamic ideology into American culture.

Within our borders, we already have a people who hold Islam as their guiding ideology. Not all Muslims, or sects of Islam, are revolutionary, but there are sects which do hold the ideals of world domination under the doctrine of dar al-Islam. As a government and people, we must oppose the efforts of Islam to overtake the West. The tools of Islamic domination include the ballot box, and we can oppose them by voting for Christian nationalists who value the culture and heritage of our forefathers. Another effort we can make is to witness the Gospel of Jesus Christ to our Muslim friends and neighbors. Above all, the witness of our lives must demonstrate our commitment to living Godly, moral, Christian lives. (The lack of moral advocacy and practice in modern Christianity has driven some to find stability and support in the strict discipline of Islam.) The fact that (some) sects of Islam are committed and working actively to replace our Christian culture gives urgency to voting, witnessing, and lobbying our friends in the same effort. We must, as a culture, acknowledge that Islam seeks to overpower/outpopulate/replace our system with theirs, and that we must push back by filling the ideological passion vacuum with a commitment to live as Godly Christian men and women and incorporate that into our daily relationships and our cultural fiber.  What Islam offers is an integrated system of lifestyle, governance, and worship. That is the kind of integration that we in Christendom must incorporate into our lives. We as Christians are not revolutionaries against the government; rather, we are the establishment, the conservatives, the ones who want to keep our liberal democracy. The fact that some Westerners are choosing to leave Christianity and embrace Islam means that there is a hunger for such discipline and holistic embrace of God into our lives.

**The Free Exercise Clause:**  On its surface, the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment appears to give every religion the right to practice as it sees best, with full immunity from government interference and scrutiny. ## I. Principles of Islam – Why People Choose Islam:

People convert to Islam for many different reasons, but at its core Islam offers a worldview, a relationship with God, a moral framework, and promises about life after death. Some of the central promises and teachings of Islam include:


**1. A Direct Relationship with God**

Islam teaches that there is one God (Allah in Arabic, meaning “God”) who created everything and is all-powerful, all-knowing, and merciful. Muslims believe every person can pray directly to God without needing a priest or intermediary. Many people find this appealing because it offers a simple and personal connection with the divine.

**2. Forgiveness and Mercy**

A major theme in Islam is that God is extraordinarily merciful. People who sincerely repent for their mistakes can be forgiven. Muslims often cite God’s mercy as one of the most comforting aspects of the faith. The Qur’an repeatedly emphasizes that no one is beyond forgiveness if they turn back to God sincerely.

**3. Purpose and Meaning**

Islam teaches that human beings were created with a purpose: to know, worship, and serve God, while living ethically and caring for others. For some converts, this provides a clear answer to questions such as:


 	- Why am I here?

 	- What’s the purpose of life?

 	- How should I live?


**4. Justice in the Afterlife**

Islam teaches that there will be a Day of Judgment when every person is held accountable for their actions. Many people are attracted to the idea that:


 	- Good deeds ultimately matter.

 	- Injustice will not go unanswered.

 	- Victims will receive justice.

 	- Everyone will be judged fairly.


**5. Paradise (Jannah)**

One of the most significant promises is Paradise, called Jannah.  Islam describes Paradise as a place of:


 	- Peace

 	- Joy

 	- Security

 	- Freedom from suffering

 	- Closeness to God


Muslims believe that those who sincerely believe in God and strive to live righteously may be rewarded with eternal life in Paradise.

**6. Guidance for Daily Life**

Islam provides guidance on:


 	- Prayer

 	- Charity

 	- Family relationships

 	- Business ethics

 	- Diet

 	- Personal conduct


Some people appreciate having clear moral and spiritual practices integrated into daily life.

**7. Community and Belonging**

Islam emphasizes the idea of a worldwide community of believers (the Ummah). Converts often mention:


 	- A sense of brotherhood and sisterhood

 	- Supportive friendships

 	- Shared values

 	- Feeling connected to a global faith tradition

People convert for very different reasons, including:


 	- Spiritual experiences or a growing belief in God.

 	- Admiration for Islamic teachings about discipline, charity, or family.

 	- Seeking structure, meaning, or purpose.

 	- Feeling that Islam’s teachings about God align with their own beliefs.

**Different Perspectives**

Muslims believe Islam is the final and complete revelation from God and that following it leads to success in this life and the next.

### Neither Islam nor Christianity maps neatly onto modern liberal democracy

The principles you listed:


 	- political equality of all citizens

 	- democratic transfer of power

 	- freedom of conscience

 	- rule of law


are largely products of a long historical development rather than the explicit founding program of either religion. Both Christianity and Islam predate modern liberal democracy by many centuries.

As a result, asking whether either religion “supports” those principles involves interpretation.

### Christianity’s historical record is mixed

Strong arguments suggest that certain Christian ideas contributed to later Western concepts of liberty, conscience, and human dignity. Many scholars point to concepts such as the equal worth of souls before God and limits on earthly rulers as influences on later democratic development. Christian democratic movements explicitly embraced democracy and constitutional government. [[en.wikipedia.org]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_democracy), [[mcdonaldce…b.ox.ac.uk]](https://mcdonaldcentre.web.ox.ac.uk/christianity-and-democracy)

At the same time, Christian societies also produced:


 	- state churches,

 	- religious persecution,

 	- inquisitions,

 	- restrictions on religious dissent,

 	- and monarchies claiming divine authority.


Historically, Christian civilization was not consistently liberal or democratic. [[acton.org]](https://www.acton.org/research/history-freedom-christianity), [[democracyweb.org]](https://www.democracyweb.org/study-guide/freedom-of-religion/history)

### Islam’s historical record is also mixed

Islam arose with religious, legal, and political dimensions from the beginning. Muhammad was not only a religious teacher but also a political leader, and classical Islamic civilization developed detailed theories of governance and law. [[en.wikipedia.org]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_and_Islam)

Some Muslim thinkers argue that concepts such as consultation (*shura*), consensus (*ijma*), justice, and public welfare provide foundations for democratic institutions. Many contemporary Muslim scholars advocate democracy, pluralism, and constitutional government as compatible with Islam. [[en.wikipedia.org]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_democracy), [[jstor.org]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/25818137), [[islamonline.net]](https://islamonline.net/en/forming-an-islamic-democracy/)

On the other hand, classical Islamic jurisprudence generally did not envision modern liberal equality in exactly the same way contemporary democracies do. Historically, Islamic states often distinguished among Muslims, Jews, Christians, and others in ways that would not satisfy modern notions of equal citizenship. Many critics argue that certain traditional interpretations remain difficult to reconcile with liberal democracy. [[en.wikipedia.org]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_and_Islam)

### If you force me to compare them

A historian might observe that modern liberal democracy emerged primarily within societies shaped by Christianity rather than within societies shaped by Islam.

That statement is broadly true historically.

However, it does **not** automatically prove:


 	- that Christianity inevitably produces liberal democracy,

 	- or that Islam cannot be reconciled with liberal democracy.


History is messier than that.

For example:


 	- Christian societies have sometimes been democratic and sometimes authoritarian.

 	- Muslim societies have sometimes been democratic and sometimes authoritarian.

 	- There are devout Christians who oppose liberal principles.

 	- There are devout Muslims who support them.


### On freedom of conscience specifically

This is probably the sharpest area of tension.

Modern liberal democracy says:
> A person may adopt, reject, change, or abandon a religion without state coercion.

Historically, both Christian and Muslim civilizations have struggled with that principle at various times. Neither tradition’s historical empires perfectly embodied modern religious liberty. [[democracyweb.org]](https://www.democracyweb.org/study-guide/freedom-of-religion/history), [[en.wikipedia.org]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_and_Islam)

Most Western countries today strongly embrace freedom of conscience. Some Muslim-majority countries do as well, while others maintain restrictions that critics view as incompatible with liberal democratic norms.

### So what does the historical evidence suggest?

If you ask:
> “Which tradition has been more closely associated with the historical development of modern liberal democracy?”

The answer is generally **Christian civilization**, particularly in Europe and North America. [[en.wikipedia.org]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_democracy), [[mcdonaldce…b.ox.ac.uk]](https://mcdonaldcentre.web.ox.ac.uk/christianity-and-democracy)

If you ask:
> “Does that mean Christianity always supports democracy and Islam inherently opposes it?”

The historical evidence does not support such a simple conclusion. Both traditions contain texts, ideas, and historical precedents that have been used to justify liberty and to justify authority. Both have produced pluralists and authoritarians.

The real debate today is not merely about the founding texts. It is about which interpretations gain influence within living communities and institutions. That’s where questions of integration, constitutional loyalty, religious liberty, and national identity become political rather than purely theological questions
