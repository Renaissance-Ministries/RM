## 1. Medicare - closed, with one trap

Thomas's read is right: Medicare isn't going to work. There may be narrow exceptions, but nothing that supports a business.

### The code that looks like an opening

Three codes pay real money. E0636 reaches over $12,000 across a 13-month rental - which looks, at first glance, like exactly the number wanted.

It's a rental code, and that makes it worse than no revenue at all right now.

Against a company that doesn't yet have a bank account, that's not a channel. It's a way to go under while technically succeeding.

E1399, miscellaneous DME. Priced case by case, no fee schedule, no predictability. Isak's assessment is the honest one: it wouldn't tell you what you can earn, only "what are we giving this away for - just the minimum we can take on it."

That's not a pricing strategy. It's a floor.

**Conclusion:** stop spending time on Medicare. Thomas's framing from yesterday applies - admit it, stop trying to get the federal government to fund the enterprise, and sell where the money actually moves.

## 2. Medicaid - a different question, and the team has been calling the wrong door

This is the most actionable finding in the call.

Medicaid is not bound by the Medicare determination. States routinely cover equipment Medicare won't price at all - standers and gait trainers among them. And the pediatric mandate is real: EPSDT requires states to cover any medically necessary equipment within the federal definition for beneficiaries under 21, even when it isn't in the state plan.

That's the same mechanism Rifton runs on. They bill everything under E1399 and publish Letters of Medical Necessity to support it.

## 3. The VA - correction to earlier advice

### What I got wrong

In the 28 August and 9 September memos I recommended pursuing VA Federal Supply Schedule 65 II F as a near-term channel, on the grounds that it needs no HCPCS code, no benefit category, and no DME enrollment. I flagged most-favored-customer pricing as a reason to hold a high price.

Isak's research shows the recommendation was premature, and I under-weighted the obligation side of MFC pricing. His conclusion should override mine.

### Why FSS is the wrong move now

FSS is a multi-year contract with permanent pricing obligations, negotiated as a discount from a published catalog price - for a company that doesn't have a published catalog, has no sales history to negotiate from, and has changed its price twice in three weeks.

The specific problem is the price reductions clause (FAR 538.272): if your commercial pricing later becomes more favorable to the customer the award was based on, that constitutes a price reduction you must report, and it flows to the government for the life of the contract.

That isn't a one-time negotiation. It permanently locks your commercial pricing into a fixed relationship with your FSS price. Isak's summary is exact: "the real constraint on a company still figuring out what it could charge."

### The escape hatch - and it validates the pricing rule already in use

Buried in the same regulation:

> A customer such as a dealer, distributor, OEM, or other reseller who receives the best price may perform certain value-added functions for the offeror that the government does not perform. In such cases, some reduction in the discount given the government may be appropriate.

A dealer price is defensibly different from a retail price because the dealer does work the buyer doesn't. That is the exact argument the team adopted on 10 September for why wholesale pricing doesn't cap the retail rate - and it turns out to be written into federal acquisition regulation already.

Worth knowing: the wholesale-versus-retail distinction isn't a clever workaround. It's codified.

### What to do instead - and it's simpler

Federal buyers have micro-purchase and simplified acquisition authority. A VA facility can buy equipment with no schedule contract at all. That's how most first sales to the government actually happen.

The sequence:

1. Register on SAM.gov - free, and the prerequisite for any federal sale
2. Talk to a VA prosthetics service about direct purchase
3. If volume materializes, then consider FSS - with real sales history and a real catalog behind you

No pricing lock-in, no multi-year obligation, no catalog required first.

One detail in your favor if you do eventually get there: FSS pricing is evaluated partly on anticipated aggregate volume and contract length. A small company offering a modest discount for a modest volume isn't out of line - you aren't expected to match a company like Medtronic's terms.

### Thomas's question, still open and worth asking early

Does the VA simply say: we always give people walkers, they cost about $300, we'll prescribe one?

That's the real risk, and it's cheap to find out. The VA may have a standard, inexpensive walker answer and no category at all for a $12,000 device. Ask before investing effort - a single conversation with a VA prosthetics service settles it.

## 4. Schools - one thing to not assume

Cooperative purchasing does not open the school channel.

State and local governments, including local educational agencies, can buy from FSS - but only within the information technology and security and protection categories. Medical equipment is in neither. A school district cannot buy a Traveler off an FSS contract.

The SELPA and IEP route is unaffected and remains the school channel. Just don't let anyone plan around FSS reaching schools, because it doesn't.

### Sales tax nexus - resolved, and it's a non-issue for now

Most states use $100,000 in sales as the economic nexus threshold. New York and Texas are at $500,000; Alabama $250,000; smaller states like South Dakota and Wyoming at $100,000.

At $12,000 per unit, roughly nine units in a single state triggers most thresholds. Isak's read is correct: selling $12,000 walkers rather than $20 widgets means you won't cross a threshold by accident, and by the time you're selling nine units into one state you'll have an accountant.

For now: California permit only. Collect California sales tax on California sales; don't collect on out-of-state sales. The customer may owe use tax in their own state, which is their obligation, not yours.

One exception worth confirming: prescribed DME is often sales-tax exempt. If it qualifies, you may not collect sales tax at all. Worth a specific question when the permit application goes in.
