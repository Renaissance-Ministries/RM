# 261115 - How Biblical Reasoning  Defeats the Sin of Legalism

# The Soul of the Law:


### How Scripture Reads Its Own Commandments

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 15 November 2026

## A Scroll with Two Sins Written on It

In this morning's study of Zechariah 5, I want to look past the strangeness of the vision to the way it handles God's law. The prophet sees a scroll flying through the air, enormous and unrolled:

*"Then I turned, and lifted up mine eyes, and looked, and behold a flying roll. And he said unto me, What seest thou? And I answered, I see a flying roll; the length thereof is twenty cubits, and the breadth thereof ten cubits."* **(Zechariah 5:1–2)**

The angel then tells him what is written on it:

*"This is the curse that goeth forth over the face of the whole earth: for every one that stealeth shall be cut off as on this side according to it; and every one that sweareth shall be cut off as on that side according to it. I will bring it forth, saith the LORD of hosts, and it shall enter into the house of the thief, and into the house of him that sweareth falsely by my name: and it shall remain in the midst of his house, and shall consume it with the timber thereof and the stones thereof."* **(Zechariah 5:3–4)**

A few details deserve notice. The scroll is twenty cubits by ten, about thirty feet by fifteen. Those are the dimensions of the porch of Solomon's temple **(1 Kings 6:3)**, the place where the law was read and the people gathered. The scroll is written on "this side" and "that side," as the two tablets of the covenant were "written on both their sides" **(Exodus 32:15)**. Many commentators have read this as a deliberate echo: the scroll is the covenant itself, now flying out from the place of worship into every house in the land.

Yet the scroll names only two sins. One side condemns the thief, which is the eighth commandment. The other condemns the man who swears falsely by the Lord's name, which is the third. Nothing is said about murder, adultery, idolatry, or covetousness.

I do not think this means only theft and perjury matter to God. I think it means two are enough. One sin against the neighbor and one sin against God stand for the two tables of the law. The scroll names representative cases, and through them it declares that the whole covenant is still in force. The curse does not merely punish the act; it settles inside the house and eats it from within, down to the beams and stones. Hidden sin does not stay hidden. It consumes the structure of the life that harbors it.

## Jeremiah at the Temple Gate

Zechariah was not the first prophet to argue this way. About a century earlier, Jeremiah stood at the gate of the temple and spoke to the crowds coming in to worship. He warned them first against a slogan:

*"Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these."* **(Jeremiah 7:4)**

Then he named what they were doing outside the building:

*"Will ye steal, murder, and commit adultery, and swear falsely, and burn incense unto Baal, and walk after other gods whom ye know not; And come and stand before me in this house, which is called by my name, and say, We are delivered to do all these abominations?"* **(Jeremiah 7:9–10)**

Jeremiah lists theft (the eighth commandment), murder (the sixth), adultery (the seventh), false swearing (the third), and the worship of other gods (the first). Again, this is not a complete inventory. It is a sample drawn from both tables, chosen because the sample is enough to prove the charge. The people were treating the temple as a safe house. They believed that standing in the right building, performing the right rites, protected them no matter how they lived the rest of the week.

God's answer through the prophet was a question: *"Is this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?"* **(Jeremiah 7:11)**. Six centuries later, Jesus quoted that very line when He drove the money changers out of the temple **(Matthew 21:13)**. A robber's den is the place a thief retreats to after the crime, where he feels secure. The people had made worship into a hideout. The form of religion was intact; its purpose had been emptied out by the lives of the worshipers.

## Jesus and the Heart of the Commandment

Jesus continued the same pattern in the Sermon on the Mount, and He began by closing off a misunderstanding:

*"Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. **For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled."* **(Matthew 5:17–18)**

Then He took two commandments and showed where they reach:

*"Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment: But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment."* **(Matthew 5:21–22)**

*"Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery: But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart."* **(Matthew 5:27–28)**

Jesus did not lower the standard, and He did not replace it. He showed what the standard was protecting all along. Murder is forbidden because the neighbor's life is precious, and unjust anger and contempt are the first movements of a heart that has stopped treating that life as precious. Adultery is forbidden because the covenant of marriage is sacred, and the deliberate, lingering look of desire is the first betrayal of that covenant, carried out in the one place no court can see.

The outward act remains forbidden. What Jesus revealed is the reason the act is forbidden. Once the reason is seen, it is plain that the inner disposition producing the act already stands against the same duty.

## The Reason of the Law

Lawyers have an old name for what these three passages are doing. The Latin phrase *ratio legis* means "the reason of the law": the purpose a law was written to serve. An old legal maxim puts it this way: *ratio legis est anima legis*, "the reason of the law is the soul of the law."

A body without a soul is still a body, but it is dead. A law cut off from its reason is still a law, but it becomes a dead form. A man can satisfy every word of it while defeating everything it was meant to protect. He can avoid the act of murder while nursing hatred. He can avoid the act of adultery while feeding on lust. He can stand in the temple every Sabbath while robbing his neighbor every weekday.

Jesus applied this principle openly when He was accused of breaking the Sabbath: *"The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath."* **(Mark 2:27)**. The Sabbath command was given for a reason: rest, restoration, and fellowship with God. Reading it in a way that forbade healing a man or feeding a hungry disciple turned the command against its own purpose.

I want to be careful here, because this principle is easy to abuse. Knowing the reason of a law does not release anyone from its letter. The letter is binding. But the letter is never self-interpreting. It must always be read in light of the duty it serves, and that reading usually makes the letter clearer and more demanding, not looser.

## Greater and Lesser

The same passages show a second kind of legal reasoning. Lawyers call it *argumentum a fortiori*, "the argument from the stronger reason." In plain words, it says: if something holds in one case, it holds all the more in a case where the reason for it is even stronger. It runs in two directions.

**From the greater to the lesser** (*a maiore ad minus*). If a great duty is binding, every smaller duty contained within it is binding too. Jesus gave us the greatest duties when He was asked which commandment was first:

*"Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets."* **(Matthew 22:37–40)**

If the whole law hangs on these two, then every specific commandment is already contained in them. Theft and false swearing are particular ways of failing to love the neighbor and to honor God. Murder and adultery are particular ways of failing the same love. What the greater duty requires, every lesser application of it requires.

**From the lesser to the greater** (*a minore ad maius*). If a small or representative case is enough to establish a principle, the larger category is included by even stronger reason. This is how the prophets' short lists work. Zechariah's scroll does not need to list every sin; two are enough to show that the whole covenant stands. Jeremiah's five sins are enough to show that temple attendance cannot cover a life that violates both tables of the law.

Paul made the move explicitly, listing several commandments and then adding a clause that reaches every case he did not list:

*"For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law."* **(Romans 13:9–10)**

Jesus used the same reasoning when the Pharisees objected to His healing on the Sabbath. *"What man shall there be among you, that shall have one sheep, and if it fall into a pit on the sabbath day, will he not lay hold on it, and lift it out? How much then is a man better than a sheep? Wherefore it is lawful to do well on the sabbath days."* **(Matthew 12:11–12)**. If mercy to a sheep is lawful, mercy to a man is lawful by greater reason.

This way of reading is older than the Latin terms. The rabbis called it *qal va-chomer*, "light and heavy," and placed it first among the rules of interpretation attributed to Hillel. Scripture was using it long before anyone named it.

## Why the Reason Is Love

If the reason of the law is love, it is fair to ask why love should be the reason. Why should the law of the universe be built on the duty to love God and neighbor, and not on some other foundation?

My answer is the conviction at the center of everything I teach. God is not watching our lives from a distance. He lives them from the inside. Every person is a place where God experiences the world. When I act on my neighbor, I am acting on someone whose life God is living, and God feels what my neighbor feels. I have laid out the argument for this in the [Premise Change handout](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/10/31/260725-premise-change-handout/) and in more detail in [Reconciliation of Man with the Father](https://renaissance-ministries.com/2026/07/25/260725-reconciliation-of-man-with-the-father/).

Jesus said as much in His description of the judgment: *"Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me."* **(Matthew 25:40)**. When the risen Christ met Saul on the Damascus road, He did not ask why Saul was persecuting His followers. He asked, *"Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?"* **(Acts 9:4)**. What is done to the neighbor is done to God, because God is present in the neighbor's experience.

Seen this way, the two sins on Zechariah's scroll make deep sense. The thief takes from his neighbor what the neighbor labored for, and God feels that loss in the one who suffers it. The man who swears falsely by the Lord's name does something worse than lie. He calls on the God who lives inside every life to stand as witness to a falsehood, using the name of truth itself as a cover for deception.

This is why the law reaches the heart. Anger, contempt, and lust are not private matters, because there is no truly private place. God experiences the inner life of the one who harbors them as well as the outer life of the one they are aimed at. We keep God's law not because a distant ruler demands it, but because we love Him, and He feels every choice we make.

## How This Defeats Legalism

By legalism I mean the habit of treating the letter of the law as the whole of the law: if I have not done the forbidden act as it is technically defined, I am clean. The legalist asks, "What is the least I must do to stay inside the words?" He never asks, "What were the words meant to protect?"

Jesus named this error directly:

*"Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith: these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone."* **(Matthew 23:23)**

Notice the last clause. Jesus did not tell them to stop tithing their garden herbs. He told them to do the weightier things *and* not leave the lesser undone. That one verse holds the whole balance, and it guards against two opposite errors.

The first error is legalism itself: keeping the letter while ignoring its reason. This is the people of Jeremiah's day chanting "the temple of the LORD" while they robbed and murdered. It is the man who has never committed adultery but feeds on lust in his heart. Legalism is defeated when we ask the reason of each commandment, because the reason always reaches further than the letter. It shows us that we have broken the law in places we thought we were safe.

The second error runs the other way: using "the spirit of the law" as an excuse to set the letter aside. A man says, "God cares about love, not rules," and then does what the rule forbids. But the argument from greater to lesser closes that door. If the great duty of love is binding, every specific commandment that expresses it is binding by necessary consequence. Love never requires what the commandment forbids. The spirit does not dissolve the letter; it explains it.

So the right reading keeps both. The letter tells us concretely what love does and does not do. The reason tells us why, and carries the duty into the cases the letter could never list. No law can name every situation. Sound reasoning asks what larger duty the words serve, and whether the case in front of us falls within that duty by greater or lesser reason.

The laws of God protect real goods: life, faithfulness in marriage, property, truthful speech, and the true worship of God. When we lose sight of those goods, we can obey every technical word and still miss the point. When we keep them in view, the letter becomes clearer, not weaker.

## The Scroll Is More Searching, Not Less

The flying scroll does not become less severe because it names only two sins. It becomes more searching, because those two stand for the whole law, and the curse they carry goes into every house where any part of that law is broken in secret. The Sermon on the Mount does not relax the commandments against murder and adultery; it shows that they already reach the heart. The temple does not become a safe house because people stand inside it; its meaning is emptied when the life outside contradicts the duties it was built to uphold.

This is how Scripture teaches us to read its own law. The letter remains. The reason gives the letter its life. A short list of representative sins can stand for the whole obligation, and the whole obligation can be seen in each concrete case. Behind it all stands a God who is not a distant lawgiver but the One who lives every life, feels every wrong, and asks us to keep His commandments because we love Him.
