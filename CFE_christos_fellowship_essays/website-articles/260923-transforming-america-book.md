## Chapter 2 — Systems Without Nuance: Capitalism and Communism


**THOMAS:** It’s a really good topic. Let me riff on the ideas you brought up. You raised a broad spectrum of issues, and they all need to be addressed in a holistic way.


Capitalism is important to look at first. All of these systems (capitalism, communism, monarchy) are each the equivalent of a single Conscious Point Physics rule. They have no nuance to them. Capitalism in its unnuanced form is this: whatever makes the most money, you invest in, and when it stops making money, you redeploy your resources to do something else that makes money. It is goal-driven. It has no particular concern for the environment unless you price in the cost of fixing the environment. When you do that, it actually becomes a moral system, and a quantifiable one. How much does it cost to care for the environment? This is how much we have to spend on cleanup, replanting, and so on.


The communist system has a different goal. In its pure form, everybody does everything he possibly can to produce, but there is no real means of distributing or pricing what is produced. So everybody has to use his own judgment, decide what is needed, and put a price on it, without any real idea what the price should be or how much he should make. There is no incentive to take risks and do all the optimization required to get the maximum production from the minimum work and cost. People are supposed to be altruistic saints who just do things for others and work their hardest, with no more reward than the man who stays home and eats bonbons.


So communism suffers greatly from the pricing problem, from the lack of any mechanism for determining need, and from a motivation problem. People don’t work, because there is no payoff for working or for excelling. It all pays the same. Everybody becomes a government worker, punching a clock and getting away with doing as little as he can, because nobody is supervising. It’s just “the people.”


And when you say, “But somebody will start poking you if you’re not working hard enough,” you have begun to incorporate elements of capitalism and fair play, and those are Christian values. So you have had to incorporate Christianity to overcome the deficits of communism. You have had to incorporate capitalism to make up for them.


Socialism is a stepping point toward the pole of communism. It still has some remnant of pricing in it, some element of management, some element of accountability.


At the other end, it’s every man for himself. We get individual rewards and payoffs. We have management. Everybody specializes. The market determines the price, how much we produce of something, how much people will pay for it, and whether a business survives at all, because there isn’t enough demand or its costs are too high. There is an element of automatic adjustment. There’s an internal governor inside capitalism. But the problem you mentioned was monopolies, cartels, and one other thing—

# Addendum to Chapter 2 — Property: Owner, Steward, and the One Who Owns It All


*[New material — drafted for Thomas’s review. Append to the end of Chapter 2, “Systems Without Nuance: Capitalism and Communism,” after Thomas’s description of capitalism’s internal governor and before the turn to monopolies, cartels, and cults.]*

**THOMAS:** Before we go on to the perversions of capitalism, I want to go back to one of the questions in your list, because it sits underneath everything I just said about pricing and motivation. Communism abolishes private property. Capitalism is built on it. Your draft asked whether Conscious Point Physics has anything to say about that, and you said you weren’t sure the physics argument needed to be made at all. I think you were partly right, and I want to show you which part.


### What the physics says, and what it doesn’t


Your draft posed the question as a choice between two pictures. In one, every conscious point has a definite place in the lattice, with boundaries, so perhaps having a place of one’s own is built into existence itself. In the other, everything is God’s substance, so perhaps ownership is an illusion because it all belongs to the Creator.


When I look at the actual picture, both are half right, and the two halves fit together.


Here is the picture. Each conscious point perceives what is within its own perceptual sphere. It computes, from what it perceives, where it should go. Then it displaces itself. Perceive, compute, displace. It acts on the grid point it occupies, and on nothing else directly. It does not perceive the whole universe, and it does not move anything but itself. So each point is a real, bounded locus of perception and action. It has its own viewpoint and its own move to make, and nobody makes that move for it. That much of your first picture is true: individuality is not an illusion. It is built into the lowest level of what exists.


But the conscious point does not own the grid point it sits on. The lattice is common. Points pass through the same locations, and even two can land on the same place for a moment before it resolves. And the point itself is not self-made. It is a point of God’s own consciousness. It exists because He is, and it acts by the rules He gave it. So your second picture is true too: in the ultimate sense, everything belongs to the Creator, the points included.


Put those together and you get neither absolute private ownership nor the abolition of the individual. You get stewardship. A real, bounded domain of agency, with real responsibility for one’s own move, inside a whole that belongs to Someone else.


**[Editor’s note:** This is a picture, not a derivation. CPP has no concept of “property,” and the argument for property below rests on Scripture and experience, not on the physics. The physics shows that the stewardship picture is consistent with the structure of reality as CPP describes it; it does not prove it. That was Isak’s instinct, and it is correct.**]**


So the physics does not settle the political question. What it does is dissolve a false choice. The communist says that because everything is common, nothing is yours. The libertarian says that because it’s yours, you answer to no one. The picture at the bottom of reality says both are wrong. You have a place and a move that are truly yours, and you answer for them to the One whose substance you are.


### What Scripture says


Scripture says exactly the same thing, and it says it more plainly than the physics does.


On one side: “The earth is the Lord’s, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein.” God says of the land of Israel, “The land shall not be sold for ever: for the land is mine; for ye are strangers and sojourners with me.” The silver is His and the gold is His. Nothing we hold is ours in the final sense.


On the other side, the same Bible guards private property fiercely. “Thou shalt not steal” makes no sense unless some things are rightly yours and not your neighbor’s. “Thou shalt not remove thy neighbour’s landmark,” the boundary stone of his field. The prophets’ picture of peace is “every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid,” not every man under the collective’s vine. And when King Ahab wanted Naboth’s vineyard and Naboth would not sell his father’s inheritance, the king’s wife had Naboth killed and the king took the land. God sent Elijah to pronounce judgment on the king for it. The most powerful man in the nation could not rightly take a small farmer’s field.


And notice what happened in the early church, which people love to cite as Christian communism. In Jerusalem the believers sold their possessions and shared everything. But when Ananias sold his land and lied about the price, Peter said to him, “While it remained, was it not thine own? and after it was sold, was it not in thine own power?” The sharing was voluntary. Ananias’s sin was not keeping part of the money. It was lying to God about it. The church held all things in common because hearts were given, not because property was abolished. That is the whole difference between the Kingdom and communism. The Kingdom produces generosity by changing hearts. Communism tries to produce it by taking the property, and it cannot, because you cannot confiscate a heart.


The parables complete the picture. In the parable of the talents, the master gives his servants real money and real authority over it, and then leaves. They are free to trade, invest, or bury it. When he returns, he holds each of them accountable for what he did with what was entrusted to him. That is stewardship: real property, real freedom, real accountability to the true owner.


### Why a steward needs property


This isn’t only theology. It is how things work in practice, and it connects to what I said about communism’s pricing and motivation problems.


A steward needs a domain he answers for. If everything belongs to everyone, then nothing is anyone’s responsibility. The common pasture is overgrazed because every herdsman gains by adding one more cow and no herdsman bears the cost alone. The public restroom is dirtier than the one in your house. Nobody washes a rented car.


We have run this experiment many times, and it always comes out the same way. The Plymouth colonists began with a common store: everyone worked the common land and drew from the common supply. By Governor Bradford’s own account, it bred confusion and discontent, and the young men resented working for other men’s wives and children without recompense. In 1623 they assigned each family its own parcel, and the harvest improved so much that women who had pleaded weakness went willingly into the fields with their children. In the Soviet Union, the small private plots that collective farmers were allowed to keep made up a tiny share of the farmland and produced a disproportionately large share of the food.


This is also why property is inseparable from pricing. A price is what happens when owners say yes or no. When I own something, I decide what I’ll accept for it, and you decide what you’ll pay, and that exchange tells everyone what things are worth. Abolish the owners and you abolish the yes and the no, and then the planner has to guess. That is why the Soviet planner never knew how many shoelaces to make.


### The limits on property


But property is not absolute either, and here I part company with the libertarian. God’s law placed duties on the owner.


The farmer was not to reap the corners of his field, or gather the gleanings, or strip his vineyard bare. Those were left for the poor and the stranger, who came and gathered them with their own hands. Notice the design. The owner was not taxed and the proceeds handed out by an official. He was required to leave room for the poor to work for their own bread on his land. The land rested every seventh year. And in the Jubilee, land returned to the families it had originally been given to, so that no family could be permanently dispossessed and no man could accumulate field after field forever. “Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place, that they may be placed alone in the midst of the earth!”


So the steward’s property is bounded by two duties. The first is charity: “If any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith,” and what we do for the least of these, we do for Christ. The second is the duty not to use one’s property to destroy other men’s stewardship. That second duty connects directly to what I am about to say about monopolies and captains of industry. The man who buys up every mousetrap company so that no one else can build a better mousetrap is not exercising his property right. He is using it to abolish everyone else’s.


So the question to ask of any arrangement of property is this: Does it enable faithful stewardship, or does it concentrate control in ways that prevent stewardship by others? The communist arrangement fails that test by concentrating everything in the state. The monopoly fails it by concentrating everything in a company. The Jubilee passes it. So does the small farm, the family business, and the home a man owns outright.


### Taxation, and the quieter kinds of theft


Where does taxation fit? Christ said, “Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar’s.” The government has a legitimate claim to pay for the things it is actually for: the common defense, the courts, the good roads everybody wants. A just tax for the group good is not theft.


But when the government takes from one man in order to give to another, not because the second man is helpless but because he votes, that is the Pleasantville robbery done by proxy. The citizen hires the government to do the robbing he would be ashamed to do himself. The Bible’s corrective to greed is not confiscation. It is voluntary generosity, family obligation, and the gleaning principle.


There is a quieter theft as well. The Bible condemns “divers weights and divers measures,” the merchant with one weight for buying and another for selling. When a government expands the money supply faster than the goods it measures, every dollar a man has saved buys less. His property has been taken without a law being passed or a tax being levied. It is the ancient practice of clipping coins, done by a printing press. A steward cannot plan to be faithful if the measure itself is dishonest.


### The answer to your question


So, is ownership an illusion because everything belongs to God? No. It is real, but it is delegated. The illusion is not that the field is yours. The illusion is that it is *only* yours, that you answer to no one for it, and that there is no poor man with a right to glean its corners.


And is property built into the structure of existence? In the sense that matters, yes. Each of us has a place and a move that are truly his, and no one can perceive, choose, and act for him. That is why the collective cannot replace the individual, and why a system that tries to abolish the individual’s domain ends in the planner’s guess, the empty shelf, and the gun. But the place is on loan, and so is the one who holds it.


Stewardship holds both halves together: the dignity of ownership and the humility of accountability. It is the same pattern we will keep finding. Every system without nuance takes one true thing and makes it the only thing. The communist takes “it all belongs to God” and gives it to the state. The libertarian takes “this is mine” and forgets Whose it is. Christ’s way keeps both.

## Chapter 3 — The Perversion of Centralization: Cartels, Monopolies, Captains of Industry, Government, and Cults


**THOMAS:** Cults. I have a particular definition of a cult. I don’t know whether it’s the correct one, because it’s the definition we used when I was in a cult. We said we weren’t a cult because we didn’t have a guru over us; we were a religion. But we did have a man over us, and we were doing exactly what he wanted. In a sense it was a perversion of the religion as it was practiced in India, although in another sense it was more pure. We were actually doing the religion that the people who claimed to practice it were supposed to be doing. We weren’t reformed. We were unreformed purists. There was a battle cry: someone would call out “Bole So Nihal,” and everyone would reply “Sat Sri Akal.” I don’t even know what it meant, but we were gung-ho. We did everything perfectly. We were fanatic religionists, unreformed. The Indians had been doing it for three, four, five centuries, and their attitude was, “That didn’t do us much good. We’ll do it, sort of.”


**[Editor’s note:** The call-and-response is the Sikh *jaikara*, “Bole So Nihal, Sat Sri Akal.”**]**


The cult was what Claude named as a pathology that develops out of capitalism. The essence of a cult, for me, is a single-minded idea of how things should be. In a healthy group, direction is homogenized because there are so many people. In a cult, there is a singularity of “do it this way.”


Because the metaphor switched, I would put the list a different way. A cartel is a group of companies all going in the same direction. A monopoly is one company making everybody go in one direction. And instead of “cult,” the third item should have been the captain of industry: the Carnegies, Mellons, Morgans, and Rockefellers. That was the equivalent. I had to figure out what the essence behind “cult” was, and “captain of industry” captures it better: a singularity of direction pushing people.


We should add one more pole to that list, and that is government. So we have government, cartel, monopoly, and captain of industry. Those are the four poles that people can be pulled toward. Communism is the pole where government takes over. Government directs how many shoelaces to make, how many ballpoint pens, how many computers, because it is in control.


The characteristic perversion in each of them is centralization. All of the poles you identified are centralization poles, and they are the opposite of capitalism. In each case—government, cartel, monopoly, and captain of industry—competition has in some way been suppressed. In the captain-of-industry case, you lowball, or you use force of personality, or contracts, or intimidation, or alliances. In some way you pervert the competitive market that would have let somebody else build a better mousetrap. You say, “I’ll just buy up your mousetrap company. Then I’ll keep jacking up the price of my mousetrap, because now I own yours, and I won’t sell yours. Or I’ll make yours and charge high prices for it.” So it is usually some perversion of the free market, carried out by a group or by one man.


There is another place for the cult as well: the eco-fascist cult. We will run all the water from the Sierra Nevada into the Pacific Ocean because the delta smelt might not have enough habitat to live in, and so there is no farming in the San Joaquin Valley because the water went out past Sacramento to keep the fish alive. The Northwest equivalent is the spotted owl. No logging. No forest maintenance. We will let everything grow wild, and the result is so much overgrowth that when there is a fire we lose millions of acres. We can’t stop it anywhere. There are no clear-cut firebreaks.


**[Editor’s note:** The fish at the center of the California water dispute is the delta smelt. The snail darter, which Thomas named, was the subject of the Tellico Dam dispute in Tennessee (*TVA v. Hill*, 1978).**]**


Eco-fascism is a cult, and it is completely unreformed. It has not incorporated the other aspects of reality: there is a time for growing and a time for harvesting, a time for clearing undergrowth, and places for leaving natural, full-spectrum growth. There should be islands of it, rather than an entire world of it.


All of these (captains of industry, monopolies, cartels, governments, and eco-cults or special-interest cults) pervert the market and do not allow competition. All of them say, “My way is the right way.” That is the realm of the religious cult, where acting one particular way is the only right way: an ideological cult.


The interesting thing is that there *is* a particular way to think that is right. I think it was Coolidge who said everybody wants good roads. That’s just a fact. Nobody wants a road with chasms in it, or boulders that fell across it from the cliff.

**THOMAS:** I want to go back to the cancer metaphor in your list, because I answered it by changing the list, and I didn’t answer the question underneath it. Your draft asked this: self-organizing systems produce order without central command, but they also produce cancer, cells that follow their own rules at the expense of the organism. Is cancer a violation of the intrinsic rules, or is it the intrinsic rules operating without moral alignment? And if it’s the second, doesn’t that map directly onto capitalism without conscience?


It’s a good question. The answer is that it is both, at two different levels, and seeing the two levels answers the question about capitalism too.


### Two levels of law


Start at the bottom. A conscious point cannot break its rules. It perceives, it computes, it displaces, every moment, exactly as it was made to. A particle cannot decide to ignore electromagnetism. At that level there is no such thing as a violation. Everything that happens, happens lawfully.


Now go up to the cell. A cancer cell doesn’t break chemistry either. Every reaction inside it obeys the same laws as the reactions inside a healthy cell. Nothing in a tumor violates physics. So at the lowest level, the answer to your question is the second one: cancer is the rules operating without alignment.


But the body has a second kind of law, the law of the whole. A healthy cell lives inside a web of signals from its neighbors and from the body. They tell it when to divide and when to stop, when to specialize, and when it is damaged beyond repair and should die for the body’s sake. A healthy cell is constantly listening to the whole and adjusting itself to it. That law is real. The body’s life depends on it. And a cancer cell breaks it. So at the level of the organism, the answer is the first one: cancer is a violation.


That is exactly the situation of a man in a society. The laws of economics work in him the way chemistry works in a cell. Supply and demand, incentive, scarcity: a monopolist obeys them perfectly. The captain of industry who buys up his competitors breaks no law of economics. But there is a second law over him, the moral law, the law of the whole: love your neighbor as yourself. That law he breaks.


So when your draft says “capitalism without conscience,” it has put its finger on the right thing. Capitalism is a set of rules like chemistry. It is lawful and powerful, and by itself it has no idea what the organism is for. It does not produce tumors because its rules are broken. It produces them because the men operating it have stopped listening to the law of the whole.


### What a cancer cell actually does


Look at how cancer works, because the parallel is closer than a figure of speech. Cancer researchers describe a set of things a cell must do to become a tumor. I’ll give you the ones that matter here, with their social equivalents, and you will recognize every one of them from what we just discussed.


A healthy cell stops dividing when it touches its neighbors. This is called contact inhibition. The neighbor’s presence says “enough,” and the cell listens. A cancer cell ignores its neighbors and piles up on top of them. That is the man who hears his brother say “You’re getting out of line” and keeps going. It is “just a little more.”


A healthy cell that is badly damaged destroys itself for the sake of the body. A cancer cell refuses to die. In an economy, a business that fails is supposed to fail, and its people and capital go somewhere they are more useful. When a failing giant arranges to be kept alive at everyone else’s expense because it is too big to fail, it has done what the tumor does.


A tumor can’t grow beyond a small size on its own. To grow, it has to send out signals that make the body build new blood vessels to feed it. It captures the common supply and redirects it to itself. That is the cartel or the monopoly that lobbies for subsidies and favorable regulation, and the special interest that gets the water or the timber or the tax money routed to its cause.


The body has an immune system that patrols constantly and destroys aberrant cells, most of them long before anyone would ever know they existed. A successful tumor learns to hide from it. It displays the signals that say “I am self; leave me alone.” That is the cartel dressed up as free enterprise, and the zealot dressed up as the church. The disguise is always the same: the perversion wears the clothes of the healthy thing it is destroying.


And finally, a tumor spreads. It leaves its place and sets up colonies in other organs. That is the concentration that starts in one market and moves into the next, and into the press, and into the government.


Now notice what all of these have in common. Every one of them is centralization. The healthy body is made of trillions of cells, each in its place, each limited by its neighbors, and none of them in charge. A tumor is one line of cells that has decided to become the whole. That is what I was describing when I said the perversion in every case (government, cartel, monopoly, captain of industry) is centralization, the suppression of competition. Cancer is what a monopoly looks like in flesh.


### The perception failure


There is one more connection to the physics, and I think it is the deepest one.


A conscious point computes its move from what it perceives. What it can perceive determines what it can take into account. Now think about what the cancer cell has lost. It has not lost its ability to act; it acts with enormous energy. It has lost its ability to perceive the whole. The signals from its neighbors are still arriving, but it no longer responds to them. It computes its move as if it were alone.


Greed is the same failure in a man. It is not mainly a failure of will. It is a narrowing of perception. The greedy man computes his next move from a sphere that has shrunk down to himself. His neighbor is still there, still sending signals: “I need to eat,” “You’re hurting me,” “That was mine.” He no longer takes them into account.


That is why the remedy cannot be only a set of external constraints. You can wall the tumor in, and cut it out, and poison it, and you should when you must. But health is a body whose cells are all listening to the whole. And that is what “love your neighbor as yourself” is. It is a command to widen the sphere of perception until your neighbor’s good is part of what you compute when you decide your move. The conscience is not a regulator bolted onto the economy from outside. It is the cell’s receptor for the signals of the body.

### Where the analogy breaks, and why that matters


The analogy breaks at one point, and the break is important.


A cancer cell does not choose. It becomes cancerous through damage to its genes, by accident or by toxic exposure, not by a decision. A person with cancer has done nothing wrong by having it, and nothing I am saying suggests otherwise.


A man does choose. That is the one thing humans have that conscious points and cells do not: the ability to override the law of the whole, to ignore conscience. So the social tumor is worse than the biological one. The monopolist is not a victim of mutation. He knows his neighbor is there. He has decided not to count him. That is why we speak of greed as a sin and not as a disease, and why the remedy includes repentance and not only treatment.


It is also the answer to your constitutional question from the start of our conversation: why does a self-organizing universe need a written constitution when conscious points don’t? Because conscious points cannot defect, and cells defect only by damage. Men can defect on purpose. A people that can choose to stop listening to the whole needs the whole written down, and needs neighbors, juries, and courts that will say “enough” when the conscience fails.


### How a body stays healthy


So how does a body keep cancer from taking over? Not mainly by chemotherapy. Chemotherapy is a necessary and sometimes lifesaving tool, but it is a blunt one. It attacks every rapidly dividing cell, healthy and sick alike, and the patient suffers for it. The main defense is the one that never stops: the immune system, distributed everywhere, recognizing the aberrant cell early, while it is small, and removing it before it becomes a tumor. The more modern cancer treatments increasingly work by stripping away the tumor’s disguise so that the body’s own immune system can see it and do its job.


Society works the same way. When the tumors are already large, the big central remedies have their place: antitrust law, breaking up the monopoly, prosecuting the fraud. But those remedies are chemotherapy. They are blunt, they harm the healthy along with the sick, and a government big enough to use them is itself at risk of becoming the largest tumor of all. That is what happened with the G-men.


The real health of a society is distributed. It is a market with many competitors, where no one gets large enough to capture the blood supply. It is neighbors who say “enough” early, while the problem is small. It is juries who can see through a disguise. It is churches and families that form men whose perception includes their neighbor. And it is a public that can recognize the tumor when it is wearing the clothes of healthy tissue, and name it.


### “Growth for its own sake”


Last, your worry that capitalism has to keep growing, has to keep going out and getting more. The writer Edward Abbey said that growth for the sake of growth is the ideology of the cancer cell. He was right, but notice what he did not say. He did not say growth is cancerous. A child grows. A healthy body grows to its proper size, and then it keeps renewing itself: cells die and are replaced every day, and the body stays alive by constant, orderly production.


An economy is supposed to be like that: growth that serves real needs, and then steady, faithful production that maintains a good life. What makes growth cancerous is the same thing that makes a cell cancerous: when it no longer serves the whole and serves only itself. When “how much is enough?” gets the answer “just a little more.” That is not the free market. That is the free market with its conscience cut out.


So the answer to your question is this. Self-organization does not fail because its rules are bad. It fails when the beings operating within it stop perceiving the whole. For conscious points that can never happen. For cells it happens by accident. For men it happens by choice, and so for men there is a remedy that cells don’t have: they can repent, widen their sight, and choose again.

# Addendum to Chapter 6 — Safeguards: Who Decides What “Christianity Done Right” Is?


*[New material — drafted for Thomas’s review. Append to the end of Chapter 6, “The Rule Set: Love as I Have Loved You,” after Isak’s words about purity of group and collective mission.]*

**THOMAS:** You asked two questions at the start of our conversation that I haven’t answered yet, and they deserve an answer, because they are the first questions any honest person asks about what I’m proposing. What safeguards keep a Christian-majority government from overriding the rule of law? And who decides what “Christianity done right” is?

### The fear is justified by history


Every tyrant claims to be preserving the good. Your framework put it well: in the physics, God decides which configurations to freeze, and His freezing is benevolent because He is good. In politics, men decide, and the history of men deciding what to preserve and what to destroy is largely the history of political abuse.

Nearly every man on them was a professing, church-going Christian. Many of them believed they were defending a Christian social order. If I had stood at the courthouse door with a test that asked, “Do you hold the Bible as your moral standard?”, every one of them would have said yes, and every one of them would have been seated.

That means there is no safeguard that works by checking labels. Any answer to your question that amounts to “put Christians in charge and trust them” is not an answer. It is exactly what the Inquisition’s defenders said.


So here is what I actually propose. There are safeguards at four levels: in the definition of the faith itself, in the limits on what the state may use its sword for, in the structure of the government, and in the character of the people who lead it. None of them is sufficient alone. All of them together can still fail. I’ll come to that at the end.


### The first safeguard: the faith defines itself by its fruit, and its fruit cannot be coerced


Who decides what Christianity done right is? The first answer is that Christ already decided it, and He did not leave it vague. “A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you.” That is the calibration. We know what the love looks like because we have seen how He loved. He did not conscript anyone. He healed, He taught, He rebuked, He invited, and when the rich young ruler walked away, He let him go. He told Peter to put away his sword.

And the simplest form of that test, for anything done in the public square, is this: Would a neighbor on the receiving end of this act feel loved by it? Not flattered, not indulged, and not necessarily pleased, because correction is not always pleasant. But loved: treated as a child of God whose good is being sought.


A lynching fails that test instantly. So does the Inquisition. So does forced conversion, forced baptism, and the burning of heretics. None of them can be called Christian nationalism done badly. They were not Christian at all. They were the captain-of-industry spirit, the cartel spirit, the will to dominate, wearing the church’s clothes.


Coercion is ruled out for a deeper reason, built into the faith rather than bolted on from outside. God gave man free choice. He did not have to. He could have made beings that obeyed Him automatically, the way a conscious point obeys its rules. He made us able to refuse Him, because love that cannot refuse is not love. A forced conversion is no conversion. A man who bows because a gun is at his back has not repented; he has only been frightened. So a government that tries to compel faith is not merely cruel. It is working against the very thing it claims to want. The Christian standard carries its own brake.


That is not true of every creed. A creed whose founding texts command conquest has no brake of that kind; its zealots are being faithful to it. A creed whose founding text tells its followers to overcome evil with good, to love their enemies, to pray for those who persecute them, and to forgive seventy times seven has a brake written into its first principles. When its followers become persecutors, they have to disobey their own book to do it. That is why the right response to Christian cruelty is not less Christianity. It is more of the real thing, and the first people who should stand up and say “That is not Christ” are Christians.


### The second safeguard: the sword has a limited job


The second answer to your question is about what the state may do at all, whatever its majority believes.


I have never argued that the government should prosecute every sin. Thomas Aquinas argued centuries ago that human law should not try to prohibit every vice, because the attempt produces worse evils than it prevents. He was right. Most sin is the business of the sinner, his family, his church, his friends, and his God. It is not the business of the man with the badge.


Here is where I draw the line for the sword, and I’ll state it as precisely as I can.


First, the state punishes the sins that make victims: murder, rape, the abuse of children, theft, fraud, violence. The things that took my stuff or hurt my person. On these, as I said, the atheist and the priest agree.


Second, the state never compels a man to participate in what he believes is sin. A baker should not be forced to make the cake; a photographer should not be forced to photograph the wedding. When the state punishes a man for declining, it is enforcing a creed by law. That is wrong when the creed is secular, and it would be equally wrong if the creed were mine.


Third, the state judges men by their actions, never by their doctrine. A man’s view of baptism, of the end times, of which day is the Sabbath, whether he is Catholic or Baptist or neither, none of it should ever carry the force of law. The intra-Christian imposition of doctrine is as much a sign of immaturity as anything the secularists do. It is also what made the Founders nervous in the first place. The American founding’s reticence about religion was not mainly unbelievers against believers. It was Christians afraid of one another. Baptists and Quakers had been jailed by Christian magistrates, and they were among those who fought hardest for the separation of church and state.


Fourth, speech is free, and the state may regulate it only where it becomes action in embryo: a threat, an incitement, a fraud. A man advocating a way of life I think is wrong is not threatening me. Under my rule he is left alone.


So the answer to “Who decides what Christianity done right is?” has a second part: where Christians disagree, on doctrine, the state is silent. Where they agree, on the moral law, the commandments and the two great commandments on which they hang, the nation has a conscience. That is what John Adams meant when he told Jefferson that independence had been won on “the general principles of Christianity.” A Baptist and a Catholic can both stand under that. They cannot both stand under each other’s catechism, and the state should never ask them to.


### The third safeguard: power is broken into pieces and handed to neighbors


The third answer is structural, and here the American founding did something remarkable. It did not trust anyone with all the power. Not the king, not the legislature, not the courts, not the majority.


Think back to your physics question. In the Conscious Point Physics, one Being freezes the configurations, and it works because that Being is omniscient and good. No man is either. So in politics, many hands have to do the freezing, and none can do it alone. The Constitution divides power among three branches that check each other. It divides it again between the nation and the states. It reserves a list of rights that no majority can vote away without an enormous effort. And it makes the Constitution itself very hard to change: two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of the states. That high barrier is itself a safeguard. You do not freeze a configuration into the nation’s foundation unless nearly everyone agrees. It means the amendment I have argued for, declaring the nation’s allegiance to the God of the Bible, will almost certainly not pass in my lifetime. I know that. The same barrier that keeps my amendment out keeps out the amendments of people who would do far worse.

Federalism is the second structural safeguard, and it is underrated. When power sits mostly in the states and the counties, a failure in one place does not become a failure everywhere. A man who cannot live under one state’s laws can move to another. And when a community proves unfit to govern itself in some matter, the discipline can be scaled to the failure. The county’s judgment can pass up to the state, or the state’s to the nation, for as long as the failure lasts. I agree with that kind of discipline. When the Southern juries would not convict lynchers, the nation was right to take those cases into federal court. That was a wider community’s conscience overruling a narrower one. My objection is only that the discipline was made permanent and universal. A father who takes the car keys from the son who drove drunk is right. A father who never gives the keys back, to any of his children, is no longer disciplining. He has decided his children are serfs.


The jury is the third structural safeguard, and I consider it the cornerstone. Every other check on the government runs through the government. Courts review legislatures, legislatures fund courts, the executive appoints judges. The jury is the one place where a neighbor sits in judgment on the law as applied to his neighbor, and where his acquittal cannot be appealed. If a Christian-majority legislature passed an unjust law, and some day one will, twelve citizens could still look at the man in the dock and say, “This law, applied to this man, is wrong, and we will not be the instrument of it.” John Adams said it was not only the juror’s right but his duty to find the verdict according to his own best understanding, judgment, and conscience, even in direct opposition to the direction of the court.


That power still exists. No juror can be punished for an acquittal. What was taken away is the jury’s knowledge of it. Since *Sparf v. United States* in 1895, judges have not been required to tell juries they hold it, and a prospective juror who says honestly that he would use it is struck from the panel. I have friends who told the truth in jury selection and were sent home for it. So we are teaching our citizens that they may judge the law only if they hide that they would. I would restore the instruction and stop punishing candor. And I would do it knowing that the power can be abused, as it was in the South, because the alternative is a people with no hand on the law at all.


The last structural safeguard is the vote, and not only at elections. What I have called the Christos Voting Network is simply this: every citizen informed, every citizen voting, every citizen putting his argument into the public conversation with his name attached. Opinion begets consensus. A government that knows exactly what its people think, and why, is harder to capture than one that hears from them every two years.


### The fourth safeguard: leaders who can be corrected


The fourth answer is about the men who lead. A leader who cannot be wrong cannot be corrected. So the manner in which a Christian holds public power is itself a safeguard, or its absence is a danger.


I have tried to practice what I am describing. I take positions, and I give my reasons, and I hold each position open to a better argument. I have said I cannot claim to speak for the Lord in every position; I can say I am trying to. That admission is not modesty for its own sake. It is structural. Now the hardest part. All of these safeguards can fail. They failed in the lynching South. The courts were there, the Constitution was there, the jury was there, the churches were there, and still innocent men died, and their killers walked free. Structure did not save them, because structure is only as good as the people who operate it.


So the final answer to your question is not an institution. It is the heart of the people. The jury box, the courthouse, the legislature, the amendment are all downstream of what the nation actually loves. A people whose hearts are right will operate even a poor structure justly, and a people whose hearts are wrong will corrupt the best structure ever devised. That is why the work I am calling for is not mainly political. It is the formation of men, by fathers, churches, teachers, and neighbors, one heart at a time. The government cannot do that work. It can only get out of its way, or stand in its way.


And when a nation will not listen to its prophets, God corrects it through its history. Every nation that walked the road of cruelty in His name eventually learned, at great cost, what it would not learn by persuasion. Spain learned it. The South learned it. That is not a comfort, but it is a safeguard of the last resort, and I believe it is real.


So, who decides what Christianity done right is? Christ decided it and showed us. The state enforces only the small part of it on which all Christians agree and which protects victims. The rest is carried by persuasion and example. And the people who most need to say “That is not Christ” when Christians do evil are Christians, first and loudest.


That is the only Christian nationalism I have ever meant. If it turns into anything else, it has stopped being what I am describing, and I will be among the first to say so.

# Addendum to Chapter 7 — The Economics of Enough: What Would Happen If America Stopped Overindulging?


*[New material — drafted for Thomas’s review. Append to the end of Chapter 7, “Where the Controversy Lies: Sexuality and the State,” after Thomas’s words about bondedness (“…because we’re all doing the right thing together.”). This answers the question Isak asked in that chapter: “What would it look like economically if overindulgence went away? Would we be rich?”]*


---


**THOMAS:** You asked a question a while ago that I passed over, and it deserves an answer, because it’s the question an economist would throw at everything we’ve been saying. You asked what would happen economically if overindulgence went away. If people stopped eating too much, drinking too much, buying clothes they don’t need, spending money they don’t have, would we be rich? Or would the whole thing fall apart?


Let me take the objection first, because it’s a serious one.


### The objection: won’t the economy collapse?


Consumer spending is roughly two-thirds of the American economy. So the argument goes: if Americans stopped overconsuming, businesses would sell less, lay off workers, those workers would spend less, and the whole economy would spiral down. Economists even have a name for it: the paradox of thrift. What’s prudent for one family, saving instead of spending, becomes harmful when everyone does it at once, because one man’s spending is another man’s income.


There’s a real point in that. If every American suddenly stopped buying anything but necessities tomorrow morning, there would be a shock. Stores would close and people would lose jobs. I’m not going to pretend otherwise.


But the argument has three weaknesses, and they’re fatal to it.


The first is that money not spent doesn’t disappear. It gets saved, and savings are what investment is built from. The dollar you don’t spend on a fourth pair of shoes goes into a bank, a business, a home, or a child’s education. It becomes a machine, a workshop, a house, a skilled worker. Consumption uses things up. Investment builds the capacity to make more things. The nations that rebuilt fastest after the Second World War, like Germany and Japan, and later South Korea, did it with very high rates of saving. Their people deferred consumption, and their savings built the factories.


The second is that the change we’re talking about isn’t sudden. It’s a change of culture, one heart and one household at a time, over a generation. An economy adjusts to that the way it adjusts to every other change in what people want. When people stopped buying typewriters, the economy didn’t collapse. It moved.


And the third, which Adam Smith put better than I can: what is prudence in the conduct of every private family can scarce be folly in that of a great kingdom. If it’s wise for your household to live within its means, avoid debt, save for the future, and not waste, it can’t be foolish for the nation of which your household is a part. A nation is only its households added together.


### What temperance doesn’t take away, it moves


Temperance doesn’t mean people stop spending. It means they spend differently. The ice cream becomes vegetables. The money doesn’t vanish from the economy; it changes what it’s spent on.


Think about what a temperate household spends its money on. It buys fewer things, but better ones: the tool that lasts thirty years instead of the one that breaks in three, which means more craftsmen and fewer landfills. It owns its home and fixes it instead of leasing everything. It gives more, because people who aren’t drowning in their own appetites have something left over for others. It invests in education and skills. And most of all, it has children and raises them. Family is the largest “consumer” of a temperate life: food, clothing, housing, schooling, time. A nation of intact, fruitful families does not lack for demand. It is building the next generation of workers, taxpayers, and parents, which is the one thing a nation cannot import or borrow.


And notice what happens to debt. American households owe more than a trillion dollars on credit cards alone. “The borrower is servant to the lender.” A nation of temperate households is a nation out of servitude. Every dollar not spent on interest is a dollar that can be spent on something real or saved for something that matters.


### The ledger of intemperance


Now look at the other side of the ledger, which the objection never counts: what intemperance already costs us.


These are rough figures from government and research sources, and they come from different years. They should be checked before this goes to print. But the order of magnitude is what matters:


Excessive drinking has been estimated to cost the United States around 250 billion dollars a year in health care, lost productivity, crime, and car crashes. Smoking, more than 600 billion a year once lost productivity is included. Medical care for obesity-related illness, around 170 billion a year. Diabetes, most of it the type that is largely preventable through diet and activity, more than 400 billion a year in medical costs and lost work. The opioid epidemic has been estimated at well over a trillion dollars in a single year when lives lost are counted. New sexually transmitted infections cost billions in lifetime medical care every year. Family breakdown, meaning divorce and childbearing outside marriage, has been estimated to cost taxpayers more than 100 billion dollars a year through welfare, courts, and the other programs that step in when fathers leave. And something like a third of all the food produced in this country is wasted.


Add it up. Even allowing for overlap between those numbers, you are looking at well over a trillion dollars a year, every year, spent cleaning up the consequences of appetite that wasn’t controlled.


I’ve practiced as a naturopathic physician for many years, and I can tell you what those numbers look like from the inside. Most of the chronic disease I’ve treated came from how people ate, drank, smoked, slept, and moved. Much of it was preventable, and much of it was reversible when the patient was willing. The American health care system is mostly a system for managing the consequences of intemperance, and it is the largest single sector of the economy.


That’s the ambulance at the bottom of the cliff, and we pay for it whether or not we ever need it ourselves, in premiums, in taxes, and in the lost work of people who should have been healthy.


### The broken window


This is where the objection turns inside out.


A French economist named Frédéric Bastiat told a story about a boy who breaks a shop window. The townspeople console the shopkeeper: at least the glazier will get work. The glazier spends his money, and so on, and the broken window looks like it has stimulated the economy. But Bastiat pointed out what isn’t seen: the shopkeeper was going to spend that money on a new pair of shoes. Now he has a window he already had, and no shoes. The town is poorer by one pair of shoes.


Intemperance is a nation breaking its own windows. The rehab clinic, the divorce lawyer, the prison, the dialysis unit, and the bariatric surgeon all show up in the national accounts as production. They count toward the economy’s size exactly the way the glazier’s work did. But they aren’t wealth. They are repairs. A nation that spent a trillion dollars less a year cleaning up after itself might even show a smaller economy on paper for a while, and be far richer in fact: more money in savings, more in real goods, more in children, more years of healthy working life, and more time on the porch.


It’s the same distinction we drew with cancer. A tumor grows fast. Growth is not the same thing as health. A temperate economy grows the way a healthy body does: to meet real needs, and then it maintains itself by steady, faithful production. It doesn’t need to keep swelling to stay alive.


### Who would lose


If temperance spread, some businesses would shrink, and I think we should name them honestly. The businesses that depend on people not controlling themselves: the gambling apps designed to be addictive, the predatory lenders, the pornography industry, the makers of food engineered to be impossible to stop eating, and the marketers whose whole trade is making people feel they need what they don’t. A great deal of their profit comes from a small number of people who have lost control.


When those businesses shrink, that is the tumor shrinking. The people who work in them would need other work, and a healthy economy would have it for them, because the money their former customers are no longer losing would be spent and invested somewhere else. There would be real hardship in the transition, and a Christian nation should help people through it. But no one should mourn an industry whose profits depended on other people’s ruin.


### What “rich” means


So would we be rich? Yes, but I want to be careful about what that word means.


We would be richer in money. The trillion dollars a year we spend cleaning up after ourselves would be saved, invested, given, or spent on things that last. Families would carry less debt and own more of what they use. Government would be smaller, because there would be fewer messes to clean up. That is the opposite of the G-men: a government that shrinks because there’s less wrongdoing.


But we would also be rich in the way that matters more. “Godliness with contentment is great gain.” Temperance is one of the fruits of the Spirit, listed with love, joy, and peace, and I don’t think that’s an accident. The man who can say “this is enough” is the only man who can ever be satisfied. The man who always needs “just a little more” is poor no matter what he owns.


God taught Israel this in the wilderness with the manna. Every family gathered what it needed for the day. “He that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack.” The ones who tried to hoard it found it rotten in the morning. And on the sixth day He gave them twice as much, so they could rest on the seventh. That was the first lesson in the economics of enough: gather what you need, trust God for tomorrow, and rest.


“There is treasure to be desired and oil in the dwelling of the wise; but a foolish man spendeth it up.” That is the whole answer to your question. The temperate nation isn’t the one that has stopped enjoying life. It is the one that has something left over: money in the bank, oil in the lamp, time for its children, strength in its old age, and the peace to sit on the porch and say, “This is good. This is enough.”

# Addendum to Chapter 9 — The Department of Morality: How It Would Work, and What It Must Never Become


*[New material — drafted for Thomas’s review. Append to the end of Chapter 9, “A Department of Morality,” after Isak’s words on forgiveness (“Seventy times seven… It means don’t stop.”). The counseling method follows Thomas’s “Effective Counsel — Christos Diagnostician” (RM, February 26, 2026). The design assumes option (a) in the decision note to Point 1: the nation declares and counsels; it does not prosecute consensual adult conduct.]*


---


**THOMAS:** I threw out the phrase “Department of Morality,” and I said it isn’t a man with a flashlight shining into the backs of cars. But a phrase like that frightens people, and it should, until they see what it means. Orwell’s Ministry of Love was a torture chamber. The Soviet Union put dissidents in psychiatric hospitals and called their disagreement a mental illness. China today runs camps it calls re-education and vocational training. Every one of those regimes said it was helping people become better. So if I’m going to propose anything like this, I owe you a clear account of how it would work, and an even clearer account of what it could never be allowed to do.


### What it is for


Start with the purpose. I said the state is supposed to promote the general welfare, and that “do what you like” is not conducive to a stable social order. I said hard hearts are a public cost: every broken marriage, every abandoned child, every addiction, every heart made callous by being used and discarded is paid for by all of us, in prisons, in welfare, in children who grow up without fathers and pass the wound on. The government spends enormous sums treating the results of moral collapse, and nothing at all encouraging the character that prevents it. It is the most expensive way possible to run a country: pay nothing for the fence at the top of the cliff and everything for the ambulance at the bottom.


So the purpose of the Department of Morality is simple. It is the nation saying, out loud and with its resources, “We are on your side if you’re trying to be good. And if you’re not, we’re still pulling for you, and there is help.”


We already have a model for this, and it works. The Surgeon General has no police. He can’t arrest a smoker or fine a restaurant that serves bacon. What he has is a voice, and the standing to use it. When the Surgeon General reported in 1964 that smoking causes cancer, and put a warning on every pack, nobody was jailed. But over the following decades the share of American adults who smoked fell by more than half. That happened by persuasion, by a nation telling the truth with one voice and making help available to anyone who wanted to quit. The Department of Morality is that, applied to the heart.


### Its five functions


Here is what I think it would actually do.


First, it would **declare**. The nation would state, in plain words, the moral law it holds in common: the commandments on which all Christians agree, and the two great commandments on which they hang. Not doctrine, not the teaching of any church about baptism or the end times, but the moral floor. Do not murder. Do not steal. Do not bear false witness. Honor your father and mother. Keep your vows. Love your neighbor as yourself. The Department would be the nation’s conscience speaking in public, the way the Surgeon General is the nation’s physician speaking in public.


That voice would speak to the government as well as the people. The Bible’s prophets were not chaplains to the king; they were the ones who told the king what God thought of his laws. So the Department would publish, for any major bill, a plain moral assessment: whom does this help, whom does it harm, and would the neighbor on the receiving end recognize it as love? It would have no veto. Its only power would be the power Nathan had with David, the power to say “Thou art the man” and let the conscience do the rest.


Second, it would **teach**. It would produce materials for families, for schools that choose to use them, for churches, and for anyone else: on marriage, on raising children, on handling money, on controlling appetite, on forgiveness. “Just say no” was ridiculed, but it put a simple truth in front of a generation of children. The Department would do that across the whole of life, in the way we’ve been talking: not “because we said so,” but “here is why, and here is what happens when you don’t.”


Third, it would **train counselors**, and this is the heart of it. Most people who are going wrong don’t need a lecture. They need somebody who can see what is actually happening in their soul, and tell them the truth in a way they can hear. I’ve practiced that for years, and I believe it can be taught. The method is rapport, confrontation, and change. Rapport first: a man has to trust you before he’ll hear you. Then confrontation, but the precise kind, a rifle shot rather than a shotgun: “When you do this, here is what it does to you and to the people you love.” And then an invitation to change. The diagnosis opens the door; he has to walk through it. The Department would train ordinary people in that skill: pastors, teachers, coaches, older men and women in every community. It would certify them, and then get out of their way.


Fourth, it would **offer help to anyone who asks**. A couple on the edge of divorce. A young man who can’t stop drinking. A father who wants to be a better father and doesn’t know how, because his own father left. A woman who was used and discarded and has hardened her heart so it can’t happen again. The help would be free and available close to home, provided mostly through churches and community counselors in the small groups where people actually know each other, the hundred-and-fifty-person cells I’ve talked about. And it would be voluntary. You could walk in, and you could walk out.


Fifth, it would **help rehabilitate the convicted**. For a man who has actually committed a crime, stolen or assaulted or defrauded, the court could offer or require counsel as part of his sentence, alongside restitution to his victim. That is the rehabilitation I talked about in Chapter 11: sitting in the corner, but for the purpose of coming back softened, not for the purpose of being put away. A prison that releases a man harder than it received him has failed at the one thing that would actually protect the next victim.


### What it must never do


Now the other side, and this matters more than everything I just said, because the history of this idea is written in blood.


It must never **watch**. No surveillance, no files on anyone’s private life, no informants, no reports from neighbors about what goes on in a man’s bedroom or his home. The flashlight shining in on the backseat of the car is exactly what it is not. The moment it keeps a list, it has become the Stasi.


It must never **compel**. No one can be required to receive its counsel unless a court has convicted him of a real crime against a real victim. No one can be penalized for refusing its help, disagreeing with its teaching, or telling it to go away. A forced conversion is no conversion, and forced counseling of a free man is just a softer prison.


It must never **enforce**. It has no police, no power to arrest, no power to fine, and no power to deny anyone a job, a license, or a benefit. The sword belongs to the courts and is limited to the crimes that make victims. The Department holds a voice and an open door, and nothing else.


It must never **inform**. What a man tells a counselor stays with the counselor, protected the way what a man tells his priest or his lawyer is protected. If a person seeking help fears his confession will reach a prosecutor, he will never come, and the whole purpose fails. The only exceptions would be the ones the law already recognizes: a child in danger, or a clear threat to someone’s life.


It must never **impose doctrine**. It speaks the moral law on which Christians agree and leaves everything else to the churches. When a man is ready to go further, it sends him to his own church, or to one of his choosing. It never tells a Baptist what to believe about baptism or a Catholic what to believe about the Mass.


And it must never **grow** beyond its purpose. Remember the G-men. We created them to stop the robbers, and once the robbers were caught, they needed something to do, so they started watching everybody. Every agency wants to grow. This one would have to be built small and kept small. A small federal office to state the standard and certify training; delivery by states and counties, mostly through volunteers, churches, and community organizations; and a requirement to show its fruit. Are fewer marriages breaking up? Are fewer children growing up without fathers? Are fewer men returning to prison? If after a fixed number of years it can’t show that fruit, it expires. A Department of Morality that couldn’t prove it was making people better would be the most hypocritical agency ever created, and it should not be allowed to outlive its failure.


### Why a department, and not just the church?


You might ask: if what I’m describing is what the church is supposed to do, why not just let the church do it? Why involve the government at all?


The honest answer is that the church should do it, and mostly isn’t. I said that plainly. And the state isn’t neutral now. It has allied itself with “do what you like.” Through its schools, it teaches our children that there is no moral center. It pays for the consequences of the collapse and says nothing about the causes. So the choice is not between the state teaching morality and the state being neutral. The state is already teaching. The only question is what it teaches.


But the order of responsibility matters. The family is first. The church is second. The community is third. The state is last, and its job is to strengthen the first three, not to replace them. The best measure of the Department of Morality would be how much of its work it could hand off: to fathers who have learned to be fathers, to churches that have learned to counsel, to communities that have learned to say “enough” to their own members early and in love. If it succeeded completely, it would work itself nearly out of a job. That is the opposite of the G-men, and the opposite of every Ministry of Love that ever existed. They needed more wrongdoers to justify themselves. This would need fewer.


### What it looks like when it works


Let me make it concrete. A young husband goes to a counselor in his own town, somebody trained through the Department, maybe a deacon in his church, maybe a retired teacher. He says his marriage is falling apart and he’s thinking of leaving. The counselor builds rapport and listens. Then he tells him the truth: “You’re not getting your needs met, and neither is she, and neither of you has ever asked the other for what you need in a way the other could hear.” They work on it, as I describe in the next chapter: asking for what you need without demanding, serving by requiring service. Maybe the marriage survives. Maybe it doesn’t. But no one watched him, no one reported him, no one forced him to come, and no one punished him for leaving. The nation simply made it easy for him to find help, and made it plain what it hoped he’d choose.


Multiply that by a million homes, and you have a different country.


That is what I mean. The state pulling for you. The nation telling the truth with one voice, and then holding the door open. It’s what Hosea did for Gomer, and what God has done for every one of us: not throwing us away, but coming after us, over and over, seventy times seven, without ever forcing us to come home.

# Addendum to Chapter 11 — Systemic Injustice: Inherited Wounds, Personal Guilt, and the Only Remedy That Breaks the Chain


*[New material — drafted for Thomas’s review. Append to the end of Chapter 11, “A Kingdom, Not a Nanny State,” after Isak’s question: “How do we stop, forgive, and move forward?” The frame follows Thomas’s February 2026 Fellowship response to Charles Eisenstein (“The Malware and the Patch”), which accepted that evil is both personal and systemic.]*


---


**THOMAS:** You just said you see evil compounding from one decision: the lynching, then the eye-for-an-eye, then the next one. That is the last question on your list that I haven’t touched, and it’s the right place for it. Your draft asked, “What does CPP do with systemic injustice?” and then stopped. I want to give it a real answer, because it is a phrase that means two very different things, and one of them is true.


### Two meanings of one phrase


The first meaning is simply this: sin doesn’t stay where it’s committed. It gets built into laws, customs, institutions, and families, and it goes on doing damage long after the people who committed it are dead. That is true. The Bible taught it long before any sociologist did.


The second meaning is the one you’ve been taught to hear in the phrase. It says that every difference in outcome between groups is proof of oppression, that people are guilty or innocent according to the group they were born into, and that the world divides into oppressors and oppressed, colonizers and colonized. That meaning is not true. It is the worldview without a center I described to you, dressed up as justice.


I want to affirm the first as strongly as I reject the second. Christians who deny the first because they don’t like the second have made a serious mistake, and they have handed the whole subject to people who will use it badly.


### What the Bible says about inherited sin


God said in the Ten Commandments that He visits “the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me.” I read that first as a description of how sin actually works, not as an arbitrary punishment. Abuse creates abusers. A father who abandons his children raises sons who don’t know how to stay. A community that was robbed stays poor, and poverty has its own consequences. The wound is passed on.


The Bible also recognizes sin written into law. “Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees, and that write grievousness which they have prescribed; to turn aside the needy from judgment, and to take away the right from the poor of my people.” That is systemic injustice in the most literal sense: injustice made into the system. The prophet Amos condemned Israel for selling the righteous for silver and turning aside the poor in the gate, where the courts sat. The Bible calls the whole of it “the world,” the system of human society organized apart from God, and says the whole world lies in wickedness.


But the same Bible draws a line that the modern ideology erases. Ezekiel says, “The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him.” Consequences are inherited. Guilt is not. The child of a thief may inherit poverty because his father’s stolen goods were confiscated, or wealth because they weren’t. But the child is not a thief. You cannot put a man in the dock for what his great-grandfather did, or for what people who looked like him did.


That one distinction settles most of the argument. The consequences of injustice are real and they travel down the generations. The guilt belongs to the man who committed it, and to no one else.


### America’s own record


You told me you’re cynical about this country’s history, and I want to be honest with you, because you deserve honesty and not a flag-waving answer.


America has written injustice into its law. Slavery was protected by the Constitution itself, through the fugitive slave clause and the three-fifths clause. After slavery ended, the Southern states wrote Jim Crow into their statute books. As I’ve said, lynchers were acquitted by juries of professing Christians for the better part of a century. In the 1930s the federal government’s own mortgage programs marked black neighborhoods as bad risks and refused to insure loans there, so a generation of families couldn’t build wealth through their homes the way their white neighbors did. Treaty after treaty with the Indian nations was signed and broken. Those were not the private sins of bad individuals. They were the law. They were the system.


But here is the other half, which the ideology leaves out. The people who ended those systems were overwhelmingly Christians, acting on the Bible. The abolitionists were evangelicals quoting Scripture. The civil rights movement was led by preachers out of black churches. Martin Luther King Jr. did not quote Marx. He quoted Amos: “Let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.” The remedy for America’s systemic sin came out of the same book that people now blame for it.


That is the pattern. When Christians do evil, they have to disobey their own book to do it, and the book is what convicts them. There is no other civilization that produced its own abolition movement out of its own scriptures in quite that way. So I don’t answer your cynicism by denying the sins. I answer it by pointing to where the repentance came from.


### Why injustice outlives the unjust


Your draft asked what CPP has to say. Here is the one thing I think it adds.


The order of the universe is conservative. Disorder does not heal itself with time. It persists until a remedy is actually applied to it. This isn’t only physics. It’s the principle behind everything I’ve said about the cross: nothing is simply forgotten. A debt doesn’t disappear because the creditor died. A wound doesn’t close because the man who inflicted it is gone.


**[Editor’s note:** The “conservative order” is used here as it is in “The Architecture of Mercy,” as a principle of Thomas’s framework applied by analogy, not as a derived result about societies.**]**


That is why systemic injustice outlives the unjust. The slaveholders are dead, the Jim Crow legislators are dead, the lynch mobs are dead. But the disorder they introduced didn’t die with them. It is still moving through families, neighborhoods, and the ways people look at each other. Waiting won’t fix it. Something has to be done.


And it’s why, as Eisenstein saw, political fixes alone keep failing. When the roles are available, someone steps in to fill them. You can abolish one unjust arrangement, and if hearts are unchanged, another grows in its place, the way the tumor grows back when the cancer is still in the body.


### The remedies that make it worse


There are two wrong remedies, and they are mirror images of each other.


The first is denial. “That was a long time ago. It has nothing to do with me.” It is true that it isn’t your guilt. But it isn’t true that it has nothing to do with you. You inherited a country, and a country is responsible for its own laws. Pretending the consequences aren’t there is not righteousness. It’s the priest and the Levite crossing to the other side of the road.


The second wrong remedy is the one you described: the eye-for-an-eye. It punishes the children for the sins of the fathers. It assigns guilt by race, which is exactly the thing it claims to oppose. It turns grievance into an identity, a permanent anger that no longer seeks to fix anything because the anger has become the self. It redistributes by group, which is the Pleasantville robbery in a new form. And it produces a new generation of wounded people who feel, correctly, that they were punished for something they didn’t do. So the chain continues. Every injustice done to remedy an old injustice is simply the next link.


This is what you meant when you said you don’t see a right side in so many conflicts. Once both sides have committed injustice in the name of redressing injustice, each side has a true grievance. Each can point to the other’s latest link. Arguing about who struck first stops being useful. The only question left is who will stop.


### The remedies that work


So what does a Christian nation actually do about systemic injustice? I see six things.


First, it repeals unjust law and applies the law equally. This is the part that truly belongs to the state, because the state is responsible for its own laws. “Ye shall not respect persons in judgment; but ye shall hear the small as well as the great.” One law for all, the same at the courthouse door for every man, whatever his color or his bank account. That is the systemic remedy for systemic injustice, and it is the government’s job.


Second, it makes restitution where the victim and the wrongdoer can actually be identified. When Zacchaeus met Christ, he didn’t say “I’ll be a better man going forward.” He said, “If I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold.” Where a particular institution defrauded particular people, and the record shows it, justice requires repayment. What justice does not require is charging people who did nothing for debts owed by people they never knew.


Third, it confesses its national sins. Nehemiah and Daniel both prayed on behalf of the nation: “We have sinned, and have committed iniquity… we and our fathers.” They didn’t claim personal guilt for what their fathers did. They confessed that the nation had sinned, and asked God to restore it. I’ve said America must repent for its national sins, and this is part of what I mean. A nation that confesses the sin of slavery, of broken treaties, and of the lynching tree is not humiliating itself. It is doing what Nehemiah did.


Fourth, it rejects every form of racial nationalism, which answers the worry you raised about the people who want the black and brown people out. God “hath made of one blood all nations of men.” In Christ “there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free.” He “hath broken down the middle wall of partition.” A nationalism built on race is not Christian nationalism. It is the same idolatry of the tribe that the gospel came to destroy. The Christian nation is defined by allegiance to Christ and to His law, not by blood.


Fifth, it transforms the relationships inside the system from within. Paul didn’t lead a slave revolt. He sent Onesimus, a runaway slave, back to his master Philemon with a letter saying to receive him “not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved.” Within a few generations, a church that treated slaves as brothers had undermined the whole idea of one man owning another. When master and slave kneel at the same table, the institution cannot survive the meal.


Sixth, and underneath all the others, it forgives. And forgiveness is not the same as forgetting or excusing. It is the decision to absorb the blow instead of passing it on.


### Who will stop


That brings us back to your question: how do we stop, forgive, and move forward?


Somebody has to take the blow and not return it. That is the only thing that ends a chain of retaliation. It is what Joseph did when his brothers, who had sold him into slavery, came to him in Egypt, and he had all the power in the world to repay them. He said, “Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good.” The chain stopped with him.


And that is what Christ did on a scale no one else could. He took the whole weight of human injustice, the systemic and the personal, the fathers’ and the sons’, into Himself on the cross, and He did not pass it on. “Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.” That is the remedy from outside the system. It is the patch the system cannot write for itself, because the system only knows how to pass things along.


So the Christian answer to systemic injustice is not to deny it and not to repay it. It is to repeal the unjust law, restore what can be restored, confess what the nation did, refuse every idol of blood, change the relationships from within, and then let the chain end with us, because it ended with Him first.

# Addendum to Chapter 12 — The Biggest Kid on the Playground: Empire, Resources, and Naboth’s Vineyard

**THOMAS:** I’ve told you how I look for the moral side in a war. Now I want to turn that same test around and point it at us, because that was the real question you asked me at the start, and I haven’t answered it yet.


You said capitalism seems to need to keep growing, a growing economy needs oil, and so America goes out and gets resources. You said we’ve been the biggest kid on the playground, stealing lunch money: snuffing out political cultures we didn’t like, funding regimes that crushed their own people’s movements, and letting civilians pay the price for a few bad men at the top. And you asked: at what cost that I’m ignorant of? Are the good guys really the good guys?


If I’m going to say “look for the moral side” about Russia and Ukraine, I have to be willing to say it about America. So here it is.


### What the record actually shows


Some of what you suspect is true, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise.


In 1953, after Iran’s elected prime minister nationalized the country’s oil industry, the CIA and British intelligence helped organize the coup that removed him and strengthened the Shah. The CIA has since acknowledged its role. The following year, the United States backed the overthrow of Guatemala’s elected president after his land reform threatened the holdings of an American fruit company. In Chile in the early 1970s, the United States worked to destabilize an elected Marxist government. During the Cold War we supported more than one regime that tortured its own citizens, because those regimes were against communism. And in 2003 we invaded Iraq on the claim that it had weapons of mass destruction, and the weapons were not there.


That is the playground bully part of the record, and a Christian cannot look away from it.


But it isn’t the whole record, and if you only see that part, you’ll see something false. After the Second World War, the United States held more power than any nation in history. It did not annex Germany or Japan. It rebuilt them, and within a generation they were free, prosperous, and our friends. The Marshall Plan fed and rebuilt Western Europe, including our former enemies. The Berlin airlift flew food and coal into a blockaded city for more than a year rather than fight for it. And for most of the last eighty years, the American navy has kept the world’s sea lanes open to everyone’s ships, not only our own. Empires in history have not usually behaved like that. The Romans didn’t rebuild Carthage. They salted it.


So the honest answer to “Are the good guys really the good guys?” is this: America has done both. It has done great good, and it has done real wrong. The wrongs follow a pattern, and the pattern is worth seeing clearly.


### The sin has a name: covetousness


Every one of those wrongs has the same root. It’s the tenth commandment: thou shalt not covet thy neighbour’s house, nor his field, nor anything that is thy neighbour’s. James tells us where wars come from: “From whence come wars and fightings among you? come they not hence, even of your lusts that war in your members? Ye lust, and have not: ye kill, and desire to have, and cannot obtain.”


Remember what I said about property. King Ahab wanted Naboth’s vineyard. Naboth wouldn’t sell, so the king’s wife arranged Naboth’s death, and the king took the vineyard. God sent Elijah to him: “Hast thou killed, and also taken possession?” The most powerful man in the nation had no right to a small farmer’s field.


Nations are held to the same law. When a powerful nation overthrows a smaller nation’s government because that government decided its own oil or its own land belonged to its own people, that is Naboth’s vineyard. It doesn’t matter that the smaller nation’s decision was foolish, or that the owners had a contract, or that the new government might be friendlier. A nation that takes by force, or by a coup it arranged, or by a puppet it installed, what it could not obtain by fair trade, has done what Ahab did. Paul says God “hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation.” The borders of nations are not ours to rearrange for our convenience.


That is also the cancer picture. A tumor sends out signals that make the body build blood vessels to feed it. It captures the common supply for itself. An empire of plunder does the same thing beyond its own borders: it arranges for other nations’ resources to flow to it on its terms, not theirs. And like a tumor, it doesn’t stay put. It spreads.


### Trade is not tribute


Here is the distinction I want you to hold onto, because it answers your worry about capitalism.


Capitalism doesn’t need conquest. It needs trade. Trade is two owners each saying yes. You have oil and want tractors; I have tractors and want oil; we agree on a price, and both of us are better off. That is the free market working exactly as it should, across a border just as across a street. It’s the good roads principle applied to the whole world. Everybody wants the sea lanes open, and a nation that keeps them open for everyone is doing something like building good roads.


Tribute is different. Tribute is when the stronger party sets the terms by force, or arranges for the other side’s yes to be spoken by someone it installed. That isn’t capitalism. It’s the captain of industry’s method, buying up the mousetrap company, done with gunboats instead of contracts. It’s the same centralizing perversion we talked about, at the scale of nations.


So your worry is aimed at the right thing but assigns it to the wrong cause. The problem isn’t that a growing economy needs resources. The problem is a nation that stops trading for what it needs and starts taking it.


And it’s worth noticing that the premise has changed. For a generation, the argument was that America had to secure foreign oil because it didn’t have enough of its own. That is no longer true. The United States is now the largest oil producer in the world and has become a net exporter of petroleum. And we have domestic energy we’ve barely begun to use: nuclear power, whose so-called waste is mostly unused fuel that could be recycled. A nation that produces its own energy has no excuse for resource wars. If we ever go to war again, we can’t pretend it’s for survival. We’ll have to state the real reason, and that’s exactly what should happen.


### When fighting communism, the ends did not justify the means


You also raised the Cold War: funding regimes that squashed socialism or communism in their own countries. That one is harder, and I want to be fair to both sides of it.


Communism was not a harmless difference of opinion. It killed tens of millions of people in the twentieth century, through famine, purges, and camps. Resisting it was right. Keeping it from spreading was right. The people of Eastern Europe, when they were finally free to speak, said so.


But Paul warns against saying “Let us do evil, that good may come.” He says the condemnation of people who reason that way is just. A good end does not sanctify an evil means. When America armed men who tortured prisoners, disappeared dissidents, and massacred villages because those men were anti-communist, it was doing evil so that good might come. And it planted exactly the resentment that anti-American movements have fed on ever since. The shah we restored in 1953 fell in 1979 to the Islamic revolution, and we have been dealing with that regime for nearly half a century. Evil means don’t just stain the good end; they tend to destroy it.


### The purchase of policy


Why does a nation that has done so much good keep doing these things? I think the deepest reason is the one I gave in the Pleasantville story: we pay the government to rob for us, and then the robbers get paid by others too.


Weapons makers profit when there’s war. Intelligence agencies grow when there’s an enemy. Politicians need campaign money, and some of that money comes from people whose business depends on conflict, and some of it comes from foreign governments’ friends and lobbies. When a legislator trades his vote on war for campaign money, that is prostitution, the sale of what must never be sold. America’s foreign policy may be advised by friends, but it must be purchased by no one. An alliance is a friendship between sovereigns, not a power of attorney.


So the reforms I’ve proposed are concrete. No sustained military campaign without a congressional declaration or authorization that states its objective in one sentence, its conditions for ending, and a date on which it expires. If the objective can’t be written in a sentence, nobody knows what winning is. War-industry profits audited and capped while hostilities last, so that no company’s quarterly earnings ever again depend on a war continuing. The revolving door between the Pentagon, the agencies, and the contractors closed. And foreign-influence laws enforced equally against every nation’s lobby, friend and adversary alike.


But I have to tell you what I believe most deeply about it. A bought government is the symptom. A buyable people is the disease. The lobbyist’s check only has power over a man who has a price.


### The test for American power


So what should America do with its power? The same thing I’d tell any strong man to do with his strength.


Use it to protect, not to take. Keep the sea lanes open for everyone. Defend the weak when they’re attacked. Keep your word to your allies and to your enemies, because a nation’s word is a moral act: the righteous man “sweareth to his own hurt, and changeth not.” Count the cost of every war before you start it, as Christ said a king must, and if you can’t win it, send an embassy and ask for terms of peace. Trade, don’t take. And when you’ve done wrong, say so.


And apply the same test to every act abroad that I apply at home: would the neighbor on the receiving end recognize this as love? Not flattery, and not always pleasant. Correction isn’t always pleasant, and neither is defending a nation against an aggressor. But love: the Iranian student, the Guatemalan farmer, the Russian conscript, and the Ukrainian mother, treated as children of God whose good is being sought. The coup in 1953 fails that test. The Marshall Plan passes it.


Tocqueville is supposed to have said that America is great because America is good, and that if America ever ceases to be good, it will cease to be great. Whoever said it first, it’s true. America doesn’t need to take other nations’ lunch money to be great. It needs to be good. A good nation does not covet its neighbor’s vineyard, and it does not feed meat grinders.


So, are the good guys the good guys? Sometimes. Our job is to make it true more often, starting by telling the truth about the times it wasn’t. That’s what repentance is for a nation. It’s the same thing it is for a man.

# Addendum to Chapter 13 — The Unreached: Those Who Never Heard, and Those Who Heard Only the Noise


*[New material — drafted for Thomas’s review. Append to the end of Chapter 13, “Believing We Are Right,” after “Well, let’s go pass it on.” The theology below follows Thomas’s positions in “The Architecture of Mercy” (Christos Fellowship essay, July 10, 2026), including the corrections he made there to the July 8 exploration.]*

**THOMAS:** Since we’re talking about passing it on, I want to answer one more question from your list, because it is the question behind every mission. Your draft put it this way: CPP says the moral law is real and discoverable, and I say Jesus in your heart is the alignment mechanism. But most humans who have ever lived never heard of Jesus. The woman I told you about, who is as fervent a Christian now as she was a communist before, had to encounter Christianity to come into alignment. What about the billions who never did?


That question isn’t new to me. It is the question that first sent me searching through the religions of the world. I’ll tell you where I have come to rest, and where I have deliberately stopped.


### Two truths the careless collapse into one


The first truth is that there is only one way. “No man cometh unto the Father, but by me.” I hear that verse first as a statement about architecture, before it is a statement about religion. It says there is one and only one way the thing can work: one mediating reality through which the Father’s life reaches a creature. There is no second door, no Buddhist door and Muslim door and Christian door leading to the same room. The Son is the interface.


The second truth is that the signal of the Son has been going out since the beginning. John calls Him “the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world.” Every man, not every man who has heard a sermon. Paul says the Gentiles who never had the law of Moses still “do by nature the things contained in the law,” because the work of the law is “written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness.” The early church father Justin Martyr spoke of the seeds of the Word scattered in every people, and said that Socrates, when he reasoned his way toward the good, was responding to the Logos without knowing its name. And when Paul stood in Athens before an altar inscribed “To the Unknown God,” he did not tell the Athenians their worship was nothing. He said, “Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you.”


So when a sincere seeker in any land perceives something true and good and turns toward it, he is responding to the real thing, whether or not he can name it. His moral knowledge is real. His conscience is real. That is why your framework was right that the moral law is discoverable. It is discoverable because the One who wrote it has been speaking into every heart all along.


Look at Cornelius in the book of Acts. He was a Roman centurion, not a Jew, who had never heard the gospel. But he feared God, he gave generously, and he prayed. And God did not tell him, “You’re fine as you are.” God sent an angel to tell him to send for Peter, and at the same time gave Peter a vision to prepare him to go. That is the pattern. The seeker who responds to the light he has is not left where he is. God arranges for him to hear the name. The woman I told you about is the same. The fervor was always in her. She gave it to communism because communism was what she had been shown. When she met Christ, the same hunger found its true object.


### Why the name still matters


Here is where I have to correct something, including something our own earlier exploration concluded. It would be easy to say: if the seeker can pass through the Son without knowing His name, then the name doesn’t really matter, the missionaries can stay home, and it will all work out. That doesn’t follow, and two hard facts forbid it.


The first is that entrance is a relationship. The sharpest word Jesus ever spoke was not aimed at pagans. It was aimed at people whose works were impeccable, who prophesied in His name, cast out devils, and did many wonderful works. To them He said, “I never knew you.” Whatever good works diagnose, they do not by themselves constitute the one thing He is seeking, which is a soul He knows. And a relationship is entered with open eyes.


The second is that the signal can be misread. The seeker who perceives the good without knowing its source can bend it, add to it, and build a religion around the bent version. The seeds of the Word are real, but seeds grow into whatever the soil allows. That is why Paul did not leave the Athenians at the altar. He preached the resurrection to them.


So the honest position is narrower than the comfortable one, and truer. The signal is universal. It is genuine wherever it is received. The interface is the Son, named or not. And none of that is a warrant to stop preaching the Name. The Great Commission is two things at once. It is cartography: we carry the fullest map of a terrain that many people are already stumbling across. And it is rescue: some of them are walking toward the edge of a cliff, and we are sent to pull them back.


### What we are not appointed to do


What about the ones who never heard in time? Here I stop, and I stop on purpose.


I am certain of one thing: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?” God will not condemn a man for failing a test he was never given. The Judge is exactly fair. But what He does with each soul is His to decide and not mine to publish. There are threads in Scripture that lean toward a wider hope than the tradition usually allows, that God desires all men to be saved, that in Christ He is reconciling all things to Himself. I hold those threads, and I hold them as hope. I will not preach them as a promise. A man who is told he will certainly arrive has been given a reason never to set out. I think God left those passages as hints rather than headlines for that very reason.


And I have to be honest that the physics does not settle this either. Your framework asked the question in CPP terms, and I want to be clear about what CPP can and cannot say. In our first exploration of these questions we reasoned from the physics to a confident universalism, as though the good would spread through the whole lattice automatically, the way a crystal grows through a supercooled liquid until none of the old phase is left. That was an error. The order of the universe is conservative: disorder does not heal itself. It persists until a remedy is applied to it, and the remedy is applied only where it is willingly received. So the physics tells us something about how the remedy works. It tells us nothing about how many will receive it. That silence is honest, and I intend to leave it there.


We are not appointed to adjudicate the edge cases. We are appointed to go.


### The unreached in our own country


Now let me bring this home, because it matters for the book, and not only for missions overseas.


It matters first for how a Christian nation treats those among its citizens who are not Christians. Remember what I said about good roads, and about the atheist and the priest agreeing that you shouldn’t take my stuff or hurt my person. Why do they agree? Because the law is written on every heart. The atheist knows theft is wrong, not because he has read Exodus, but because the Light that lights every man has lit him too. That is why a nation can hold the moral law in common without requiring every citizen to be a believer, and why the civil law of a Christian nation can be just to the Muslim, the Hindu, and the unbeliever. It asks of them only what their own conscience already knows. They are our neighbors and our fellow citizens, and they are owed love, not coercion. They are not enemies of the Christian nation. They are its mission field.


And second, America has its own unreached, and they are not in the jungle. They are the millions who have heard the name of Jesus all their lives, as a curse word, as a cliché, as the brand of a political party, or as the religion of their grandmother’s church, and have never once heard the gospel. They are in some ways harder to reach than the man who never heard the name at all, because they think they already know what it means. They have been inoculated with a weak dose of the thing, and the weak dose protects them from the real one.


That is the field for *Transforming America*. We are not trying to reach America by taking over its government. We are trying to reach the unreached who live on our own streets, with the real thing instead of the caricature.


### Why it can’t wait


You gave the Fellowship something important when we discussed this in the summer, and I want to put it here where it belongs. You pointed out that the urgency of the Commission isn’t only about the life to come. “The kingdom of heaven is at hand” is present tense. A soul that is out of alignment is suffering now, in this life, in the stress and self-destruction that misalignment produces. You know that from your own experience, the fish chasing the hook, and so do I. And the way back to harmony is open now.


So evangelism is not fire insurance sold by threat. It is a physician’s urgency joined to a shepherd’s. “You are suffering, and you need not be.” And also: “You are near the edge, and I love you too much to watch you fall.” Both are true. They are the same love looking at the same person over two horizons.


That is why it only takes a spark. The seeker is already there, with the light he has, looking for its source. Our part is to be the Peter who goes when the Cornelius sends for him, and to go even before we’re sent for. We bring people along, and we teach them to bring people along. That is what changing the world is. And that is the only answer I can give you about the billions: God is fair to every one of them, and He has told us to go.
