# An Academic Assessment of Denver Snuffer's 500th-Anniversary Lecture 2

## Historical Accuracy and Biblical Consistency

**Fellowship Discussion Essay | August 10, 2026**
**Source:** Denver Snuffer Jr., second lecture of the eight-lecture series delivered on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of the Protestant Reformation, given in Dallas, Texas. The series and its supporting materials are published at https://www.christianreformation500years.info and at https://www.learnofchrist.org. Readers are encouraged to hear or read the lecture in full.
**Method:** As in the assessment of Lecture 1, this essay is an academic evaluation only. Each substantive claim is extracted and assessed on two axes: (1) **historical accuracy** against the documented record and (2) **biblical consistency** against the text of Scripture (King James Version). Claims are paraphrased; quotation is minimal; no judgment is offered on rhetorical importance or persuasive weight. Restoration-scripture provenance is identified where a claim presented on biblical footing originates in the Book of Mormon, the Book of Moses, or the Doctrine and Covenants. Claims already assessed in the Lecture 1 essay are cross-referenced rather than re-argued.
**Transcript condition:** The available transcript is corrupted from approximately the 56-minute mark: audio from Lecture 1 is interleaved with the Dallas talk, producing composite sentences. Assessments in §§23–27 below are confined to claims recoverable with confidence from the uncorrupted portions of those passages; where a claim cannot be reconstructed with confidence, it is not assessed.

---

## I. Claims Concerning the Biblical Text and the Doctrines of the Reformation

### 1. Creation, dominion, and the sustaining power of Christ

**Claims as delivered.** God created heaven and earth and owned them (Genesis 1:1); He gave dominion to Adam and Eve (Genesis 1:26); the Word did the creating (John 1:3–4); in Him we live and move and have our being (Acts 17); "another prophet" taught that God preserves us from day to day, lending us breath, so that we are less than the dust of the earth; because we borrow Christ's power to live, He knows our every deed and thought and can judge us perfectly.

**Biblical assessment.** The Genesis, John, and Acts citations are accurate (Genesis 1:1, 26–28; John 1:3–4; Acts 17:27–28 — the words are Paul's at Athens, recorded by Luke, so "Luke explains in the book of Acts" is acceptable attribution). The sustaining-power doctrine is biblically consistent: "by him all things consist" (Colossians 1:17); "upholding all things by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3); "in whose hand thy breath is" (Daniel 5:23). The perfect-knowledge and perfect-judgment claims are likewise consistent (Psalm 139:1–4; John 2:24–25; Hebrews 4:12–13; 1 Corinthians 4:5, which adds the motive dimension the lecture stresses — "will make manifest the counsels of the hearts").

**Source identification.** The "another prophet" passage is **Mosiah 2:21, 25** (King Benjamin) in the Book of Mormon, read without attribution to a general Christian audience. Its content (dependence on God for breath and motion; man as dust) closely parallels Acts 17:25, 28 and Genesis 3:19 and is not doctrinally objectionable; the unattributed sourcing is noted for the record, continuing the pattern documented in the Lecture 1 assessment (§§1, 18, 22 there).

### 2. Against "grace alone": baptism is required for salvation

**Claims as delivered.** The Lutheran principle of grace alone was not part of original Christianity, because original Christianity required baptism for salvation. In support: Christ's repeated "follow me"; Christ's own baptism "to fulfil all righteousness," after which the Father declared Himself well pleased; Christ's disciples baptizing (John 4); Saul converted on the Damascus road, healed, renamed Paul, and immediately baptized; baptism tied to resurrection (Romans 6), to putting on Christ (Galatians 3), to the one faith and one baptism (Ephesians 4), and to salvation (1 Peter 3).

**Biblical assessment of the citations.** The catena is accurately cited: Matthew 3:15; Matthew 3:17; John 4:1–2; Acts 9:18; Romans 6:3–5; Galatians 3:27; Ephesians 4:5; 1 Peter 3:21. Two corrections to the narrative details. (a) Saul was not "renamed Paul" at or following his conversion: the text introduces "Saul, (who also is called Paul)" years later, at the outset of the first missionary journey (Acts 13:9); Saul (Hebrew) and Paulus (Roman) were the double names of a Hellenistic Jew and Roman citizen, and no renaming by Christ or at baptism is recorded — the conversion chapters call him Saul throughout (Acts 9). (b) The inference that the Father's commendation was *contingent on* the baptism goes beyond the narrative sequence of Matthew 3:16–17. It should also be noted that 1 Peter 3:21 carries its own qualifier — "not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God" — and that Luke 23:43 records salvation pronounced on the unbaptized thief.

**Historical assessment of the target.** The argument misstates the doctrine it opposes. *Sola gratia* in Lutheran teaching is opposed to salvation by human merit, not to the necessity of baptism. Luther taught baptismal regeneration emphatically (Small Catechism, Part IV: baptism "works forgiveness of sins, delivers from death and the devil, and gives eternal salvation"), and the Augsburg Confession, Article IX, states that baptism is necessary to salvation — the Lutheran church condemned the Anabaptists on precisely this point. The proposition "original Christianity required baptism, therefore grace alone was not original Christianity" is thus an argument against a position Lutheranism does not hold; as history of doctrine, the contrast is inaccurate.

### 3. Against "faith alone": progression in knowledge, obedience, and virtue

**Claims as delivered.** Faith alone was not original Christianity, because the original Christians believed in progressing in godliness: Paul denounced continuing in sin (Romans 6:1–4); Peter taught progression through the virtues until one's calling and election is made sure (2 Peter 1:4–10).

**Biblical assessment of the citations.** Both passages are accurately quoted (Romans 6:1–4; 2 Peter 1:4–10), and both genuinely teach that saving faith issues in transformed life and growth in virtue.

**Historical assessment of the target.** As with §2, the doctrine opposed is misstated. *Sola fide* is a doctrine about the ground of *justification* — that the sinner is accounted righteous through faith apart from the merit of works (Romans 3:28; 4:5; Ephesians 2:8–9) — not a denial of sanctification. The Lutheran confessions teach that faith necessarily produces good works and condemn antinomianism by name (Augsburg Confession VI, XX; Formula of Concord IV); Luther's preface to Romans describes faith as "a living, busy, active, mighty thing" that cannot exist without works following. The Reformers' proof-texts and Snuffer's proof-texts here are the same texts read the same way. The claim that progression-in-virtue distinguishes original Christianity *from* faith-alone Christianity is therefore inaccurate as a description of the doctrine at issue; the genuine historical dispute (whether works are the *fruit* of justification or part of its *ground*) is not the dispute the lecture describes.

### 4. Against "scripture alone": Luther's translation, illiteracy, and the absence of a New Testament

**Claims as delivered.** (a) Luther translated the Bible "from a second language that was not commonly spoken — that is, Latin — into the common language of Germany." (b) If scripture alone defines the faith, then fifteen centuries of illiterate Christians, and any literate Christian who could not read a dead language, were by definition incapable of salvation. (c) There was no New Testament during the era of original Christianity; the idea of compiling one originated with a second-century heretic excommunicated for apostasy; the only scriptures used or cited in original Christianity were the Old Testament; the canon was not settled until the fourth century, by which time many writings had been altered.

**Historical assessment.** (a) is factually incorrect on a well-documented point: Luther translated the New Testament (1522) from the *Greek* text of Erasmus's second edition, and the Old Testament from the *Hebrew*, precisely in order to bypass the Latin Vulgate; translation from the original tongues rather than from Latin was the distinguishing mark of his work and of Reformation translation generally (Tyndale likewise from Greek and Hebrew). (b) misconstrues the doctrine: *sola scriptura* asserts that Scripture is the supreme *norm* of doctrine — that no pope, council, or tradition may bind the conscience contrary to it — not that private literacy is a condition of salvation; the Reformers' own practice (catechisms, preaching, liturgy in the vernacular) presupposed that the illiterate receive the word by hearing (Romans 10:17). The inference "scripture alone, therefore the illiterate are damned" does not follow from the doctrine as its adherents defined it. (c) contains one accurate element and two inaccurate ones. Accurate: Marcion (c. 140), excommunicated as a heretic, produced the first known fixed collection resembling a canon, and the formal settlement of the twenty-seven-book New Testament belongs to the fourth century (Athanasius's festal letter, 367; councils of Hippo 393 and Carthage 397). Inaccurate: that "the only scriptures used or cited" in the apostolic era were the Old Testament — the New Testament itself contradicts this, for Peter classes Paul's epistles with "the other scriptures" (2 Peter 3:15–16), and 1 Timothy 5:18 cites the saying of Luke 10:7 as "scripture" alongside Deuteronomy 25:4; and the sub-apostolic writers (Clement of Rome c. 96, Ignatius c. 107, Polycarp c. 110) quote gospel material and Pauline epistles as authoritative a generation before Marcion. The four-gospel collection is defended as settled by Irenaeus (c. 180). The accurate statement is that the *boundary* of the canon was settled late; the claim that the *books* were absent or unused in original Christianity is contradicted by the sources, including the two canonical texts just cited. The alteration claim is assessed at §15 below.

### 5. The "doctrine of Christ" reading

The lecture again reads **3 Nephi 11:31–40** in full as "the doctrine of Christ," without identifying its Book of Mormon source. The content parallels and the assessment of the exclusivity clause ("more or less than this") are as given in the Lecture 1 essay, §18, and are not repeated; the same self-referential tension applies here with added force, since this lecture *adds* doctrines (premortal existence of human spirits, §17 below; new canonical scripture, §22 below) within the same address that declares additions to the doctrine to come of evil.

## II. Claims Concerning Church History

### 6. Catholicism in 1517 as a monopoly; "a millennium and a half of Catholic hegemony"

**Claims as delivered.** In 1517 Catholicism was a religious, economic, land, and military monopoly in Europe; market control produced laziness and abuse; the church had held hegemony for a millennium and a half.

**Historical assessment.** As a description of Latin Christendom on the eve of the Reformation, the monopoly characterization is broadly defensible: the Roman church was the sole licit religious institution of Western and Central Europe, a vast landholder (commonly estimated at a quarter to a third of the land in many territories), and a wielder of political and occasionally military power. Two qualifications are required for accuracy. (a) The monopoly was Western: the Eastern Orthodox communion, outside Roman jurisdiction since 1054, comprised a large portion of Christendom, and Hussite Bohemia had maintained institutional independence from Rome for a century before Luther. (b) "A millennium and a half of hegemony" overstates the duration by roughly a third: Christianity itself was only some fifteen centuries old in 1517, and for its first three centuries the church was an intermittently persecuted minority with no hegemony of any kind; Roman ecclesiastical dominance of the West is datable, at the earliest, from the fourth century. The abuse claim is common ground with Catholic historiography of the period (see §7).

### 7. Cardinal Dolan on Luther

**Claim as delivered.** Cardinal Timothy Dolan, Archbishop of New York, acknowledged that the Catholic Church cannot deny it had become corrupt, and said Luther was responsible for striking a match that created a bonfire whose flames still burn.

**Historical assessment.** The general representation is verifiable: in the anniversary year Dolan publicly and appreciatively engaged the Reformation commemoration, including preaching at Fifth Avenue Presbyterian Church on Reformation Sunday 2017 with warm words for Luther's reforming sincerity, and Catholic officialdom in that year (following the 1999 Lutheran–Catholic *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* and the 2013 report *From Conflict to Communion*) freely acknowledged the corruption of the late-medieval church. The specific match-and-bonfire wording could not be verified against a documented source in the course of this assessment; the sentiment attributed is consistent with Dolan's documented remarks, but the quotation should be treated as unverified.

### 8. Luther's predecessors, the printing press, and "history's first bestseller"

**Claims as delivered.** Luther was not the first open critic of Catholic abuses; earlier critics were burned at the stake; Luther's timing was aided by Gutenberg's press; the Ninety-five Theses became a pamphlet that was history's first bestseller.

**Historical assessment.** Accurate in substance. The predecessor claim is documented (Hus burned 1415, Savonarola executed 1498 — both named in Lecture 1 and verified in that assessment, §II). The press claim is standard and sound. The "first bestseller" claim is a popularization drawn from modern print-history scholarship (notably Andrew Pettegree's *Brand Luther*, 2015, which styles Luther the first mass-market author); as a claim about the Theses specifically it compresses the record — the Theses circulated explosively in Latin and German printings, but it was Luther's vernacular pamphlets of 1518–1520 that constituted the unprecedented sales phenomenon. Defensible as popular history; imprecise in its object.

### 9. "The Reformation did not attempt to restore an original Christianity"

**Claims as delivered.** After fifteen centuries of institutional Christianity, the Reformers could not even imagine non-institutional Christianity; their aim was only to reform a corrupt institution into something marginally better; the Lutheran church mimicked its Catholic mother; reconsidering institutional Christianity and returning to the original form "was not even attempted in the Protestant Reformation"; a return to original Christianity required a restoration beginning with Joseph Smith in 1820.

**Historical assessment.** The claim is accurate for the magisterial Reformation's *ecclesiology* in part — Luther retained liturgy, clergy, and territorial church structure, and Lutheran worship does bear visible continuity with its Catholic inheritance — but the universal negative ("not even attempted") is contradicted by the documented record of the Reformation era itself. The Radical Reformation, beginning with the Swiss Brethren in Zurich in 1525, explicitly pursued *restitutio* — the restoration of primitive Christianity — as its program: believers' baptism, voluntary congregations, separation from the state, congregational discipline, and in the Hutterite case community of goods on the model of Acts 2 and 4. Sixteenth-century writers themselves distinguished *reformatio* from *restitutio* (the title of Menno Simons's tradition's aspiration, and literally the title of works in that stream). Later movements inside Protestantism — English Separatism, the Baptists, and the nineteenth-century Restoration movements — likewise made primitive-church restoration their stated aim. One may argue those attempts failed or were incomplete; the claim that no attempt occurred before 1820 is historically false. It is also to be noted, as a matter of the lecture's internal consistency, that the magisterial Reformers *did* claim to restore original *doctrine* (the apostolic gospel), locating apostolicity in teaching rather than polity; the lecture's negative holds only if "original Christianity" is defined in advance as a matter of polity and practice — which is the conclusion the argument exists to establish.

### 10. The character of the first-century church: no organization, no hierarchy, leadership by "deaconois"

**Claims as delivered.** Early Christians (the ecclesia) were not institutional and not hierarchical; the first century had no formal organization and no central control; Christians met informally in homes or public places, in small groups led by men and women called *diakonoi*, a word meaning servant.

**Biblical assessment.** Partially accurate, materially incomplete. Accurate: the assembly met in homes (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15) and public places (Acts 2:46; 19:9); *diakonos* means servant; women are attested in the diaconal role (Phoebe, "a servant [diakonos] of the church which is at Cenchrea," Romans 16:1). Incomplete to the point of inaccuracy: the New Testament church possessed defined offices and trans-local authority from the beginning. The apostles exercised governing authority (Acts 5:1–11; 1 Corinthians 5:3–5; 2 Corinthians 10:8); elders were *appointed* "in every church" (Acts 14:23) and "in every city" (Titus 1:5); the Philippian church is addressed "with the bishops and deacons" (Philippians 1:1); qualification lists for bishop and deacon are legislated (1 Timothy 3:1–13); and the Jerusalem council issued a decision binding on Gentile congregations across provinces (Acts 15:22–29 — "it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us"), which is central coordination by any definition. The accurate statement is that the first-century church was non-monarchical and non-professionalized relative to later centuries, not that it was without organization, office, or hierarchy of authority. The identification of *diakonoi* as the leaders of the house meetings also inverts the New Testament's own terminology, in which the presiding offices are the elders/bishops (*presbyteroi/episkopoi*, Acts 20:17, 28; 1 Peter 5:1–2) and the deacons serve (Acts 6:2–4).

### 11. The Book of Mormon model of unpaid ministry; no professional clergy

**Claims as delivered.** Original Christians had no professional clergy and operated akin to the method described in the Book of Mormon (priests laboring with their own hands, teacher no better than the learner, substance imparted to the poor); Christianity today ought to be practiced without professional clergy diverting tithes from the poor.

**Assessment.** The Book of Mormon passage is **Alma 1:26–27**, and on this occasion the source *is* identified to the audience — noted for the record as a departure from the unattributed pattern. The historical claim that the apostolic church had no supported ministry was assessed in the Lecture 1 essay, §16, and the finding stands: it is contradicted by explicit apostolic texts (Luke 10:7; 1 Corinthians 9:7–14; Galatians 6:6; 1 Timothy 5:17–18; Philippians 4:15–18), in which ministerial support is called the Lord's own ordinance, alongside the church's undisputed poor relief. Paul's tentmaking (Acts 18:3; 20:34) parallels the Alma model and shows the *waiver* practiced; the waiver presupposes the right (1 Corinthians 9:12, 15).

### 12. Justin Martyr's description of early worship

**Claims as delivered.** Justin Martyr (110–165) described sub-apostolic worship: meetings in homes with no church buildings; baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as the entrance; prayer and the kiss of greeting; the sacrament of bread and wine mixed with water, blessed with extended thanksgiving; reading of the scriptures and memoirs of the apostles; collection and distribution of donations to the poor.

**Historical assessment.** Substantially accurate. The dates are conventional (c. 100–165); the worship description tracks *First Apology* 61 (baptism), 65 (kiss, wine mixed with water, thanksgiving), and 67 (weekly assembly, reading of "the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets," collection for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners, and strangers). Two omissions bear on the lecture's use of the passage. (a) Justin's chapter 67 describes the assembly gathering "on the day called Sunday" of "all who live in cities or in the country... to one place," and (b) the same chapter is structured around an officer Justin calls the *president* (ὁ προεστώς), who preaches, offers the eucharistic thanksgiving, and *receives and stewards the collection* — with deacons distributing the elements. That is, the very source cited for informal, leaderless, house-based worship itself attests a presiding officer, an ordered liturgy, a fixed day, and centralized handling of funds within the congregation. The citation is accurate; the use is selective.

### 13. The taxonomy of early Christianities

**Claims as delivered.** The earliest believers were divided into Pauline Christians (grounded in Paul's mission field, guarding his doctrine), Matthewian Christians (centered in Antioch, mediating Jewish and Gentile Christianity), Johannine Christians (emphasizing individual relationship with Christ, the indwelling Spirit, premortal existence of man's spirits, and divinization), Petrine Christians (emphasizing authority, shepherds and sheep, the root of later hierarchy), Gnostics (centered in Egypt, claiming John, salvation through hidden knowledge), Syriac Christianity, and Thomas Christianity in India and Asia; there was no one in charge and no attempt to standardize; almost all knowledge of the earliest practices was erased by destruction of records.

**Historical assessment.** The taxonomy reproduces, in simplified form, a modern scholarly heuristic — the reconstruction of "apostolic-figure communities" associated with Raymond Brown (*The Churches the Apostles Left Behind*, 1984), the Antioch hypothesis for Matthew's gospel, Johannine-community scholarship, and Ehrman's popularizations. As delivered, hypothesis is presented as settled description: these "communities" are scholarly inferences from the emphases of the documents, not entities attested by census or self-designation, and the model is contested within the guild (the "community" paradigm for gospel origins has been substantially challenged, e.g., Richard Bauckham's *The Gospels for All Christians*, 1998). Individual elements vary in standing: Syriac Christianity and the Thomas tradition in India are genuinely attested (the Mar Thoma churches; *Acts of Thomas*), though the Indian mission's historicity is traditional rather than proven; Gnostic use of John is documented (the Valentinians; the first known commentary on John is Heracleon's). Two specific claims are inaccurate. (a) That the Johannine believers taught "the pre-earth existence of man's spirits": the Johannine corpus teaches the preexistence of *Christ* (John 1:1–2; 8:58; 17:5) and nowhere the premortal existence of human spirits; no Johannine text is cited because none exists, and the two passages the lecture reads in support — 1 John 3:1–2 and Revelation 3:20–21 — teach, respectively, present adoption with future conformity to Christ at His appearing, and the overcomer's future enthronement; neither says anything of existence before birth. (b) That divinization was "lost to Western Christianity" and survives only vestigially: deification (*theosis*) is a continuously confessed doctrine of Eastern Orthodoxy from Irenaeus and Athanasius ("He became man that we might become god," *De Incarnatione* 54) through Gregory Palamas to the present, and has Western attestation as well (Augustine, *Sermon* 192: "God became man that man might become god" in the sense of adoptive participation). What the patristic doctrine teaches is participation in the divine nature by grace (2 Peter 1:4) — creatures glorified in Christ — not the ontological ascent of uncreated spirits; the biblical texts quoted (1 John 3:2; Revelation 3:21) are consistent with the former and do not teach the latter.

### 14. Origen and the preexistence of souls

**Claims as delivered.** Origen, an early Christian, claimed the original teachings of Christ included that Christ came into the world possessing knowledge from before creation; other spirits, less faithful, some fallen, are also involved with this world; Joseph Smith likewise testified all of us existed as spirits before the creation; each soul is at a different point of progression; a veil of forgetfulness limits premortal memory so that faith may be developed.

**Historical assessment.** The Origen attribution is substantially accurate: Origen (c. 185–254) taught, principally in *De Principiis* I–II, the preexistence of rational souls, their differing conditions in this world as consequent on premortal conduct, and the Son's eternal possession of the Father's knowledge. Three qualifications govern the use of the datum. (a) Origen presents much of this expressly as speculative inquiry where the church's rule of faith was silent (*De Principiis*, preface), not as "the original teachings of Christ." (b) The teaching did not merely fade: the preexistence of souls was formally anathematized in the sixth century (the anti-Origenist anathemas associated with the Second Council of Constantinople, 553) — so its later absence from Christian doctrine is a matter of documented conciliar rejection, not silent loss. (c) One early teacher's speculation is evidence that the idea existed early, not that it was apostolic; no first-century source attests it.

**Biblical assessment.** No biblical text teaches the premortal existence of human spirits. Texts bearing on the question cut the other way or are neutral: "The first man is of the earth, earthy" and "that was not first which is spiritual, but that which is natural" (1 Corinthians 15:46–47); God "formeth the spirit of man within him" (Zechariah 12:1); the spirit "shall return unto God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7) speaks of origin from God, not conscious premortal history; Jeremiah 1:5 ("Before I formed thee... I knew thee") predicates the foreknowledge of *God*, not the pre-existence of Jeremiah; Job 38:7 (the sons of God shouting at creation) identifies the heavenly host, not unborn humanity, in its context (cf. Job 1:6; 2:1). The veil-of-forgetfulness and progression doctrines are Restoration teachings (cf. the lecture's own appeal to Joseph Smith) without biblical attestation. As with §13(a), the chain "John taught it — it lingered — Origen held it — Joseph Smith restored it" fails at its first link.

### 15. The corruption of the New Testament text: the voice at the baptism

**Claims as delivered.** Second- and third-century doctrinal struggles altered the scriptures; Bart Ehrman documented this in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*; the original text of the voice at Christ's baptism read "Thou art my son; this day have I begotten thee" (Psalm 2:7) and was changed because it supported adoptionism, a doctrine the proto-orthodox were defeating; but when Paul wrote Hebrews he quoted Psalm 2:7, and "the editors missed one."

**Historical assessment.** The bibliographic reference is accurate: Ehrman's *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993) is a genuine scholarly monograph arguing that some scribal alterations were theologically motivated. The specific example, however, is materially overstated in three respects. (a) *Scope.* The variant exists in **Luke 3:22 only**. The manuscript tradition of Matthew 3:17 ("This is my beloved Son...") and Mark 1:11 is not in question; so "when Christ was originally baptized, the voice did not say 'This is my beloved son'" is false as a statement about the Gospel record generally even on Ehrman's own position. (b) *Status.* The Psalm 2:7 reading of Luke 3:22 is a Western reading (Codex Bezae, some Old Latin witnesses, and citations in Justin, Clement of Alexandria, and others); Ehrman argues for its originality, but the great majority of text critics and all standard critical editions (Nestle-Aland, UBS) print the "Thou art my beloved Son" reading as original, judging the Psalm 2:7 form a scribal assimilation to the Psalm. The lecture presents a contested minority position as established fact. (c) *Mechanism.* "The editors missed one" presupposes a central editorial authority conducting a coordinated purge — a mechanism contradicted by the wholly decentralized manuscript transmission of the second and third centuries (thousands of copies, no authority capable of recalling them), contradicted by the survival of the Bezae reading itself, and in tension with the lecture's own claim (§10, §13) that early Christianity had "no central control." It is further contradicted by the fact that the allegedly suppressed proof-text was never suppressed: Psalm 2:7 stands in every Bible, and its application to Christ is *preserved* in the New Testament at Acts 13:33, Hebrews 1:5, and Hebrews 5:5 — where the begetting is referred to the resurrection and exaltation, which is why it posed no adoptionist difficulty. A subsidiary inaccuracy: the lecture attributes Hebrews to Paul without qualification; the epistle is anonymous, and doubt about Pauline authorship is ancient — Origen, whom the lecture elsewhere cites as a witness (§14), famously concluded that "who wrote the epistle, God only knows" (in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.25).

**Biblical assessment.** The general claim that the text of Scripture was wholesale corrupted in transmission is contradicted by the scale of the evidence Ehrman himself works within: the variations documented are identifiable precisely because the abundant, geographically dispersed manuscript tradition preserves the competing readings for comparison. The doctrinally significant variants are a small, catalogued set, none of which — on the testimony of the standard critical editions — removes or establishes any doctrine otherwise absent from the undisputed text.

### 16. Orthodoxy and heresy as the victors' labels; suppression and killing

**Claims as delivered.** Original Christianity had no orthodoxy or heresy; these terms were adopted once the proto-orthodox sensed victory; those who disagreed were excommunicated, and once the proto-orthodox gained confidence, killed, and their scriptures burned; the proto-orthodox taught that men could be saved in ignorance so long as they accepted sacraments from those with authority; Christ was displaced by allegiance to an institution.

**Historical assessment.** The labeling claim reproduces the Bauer framework assessed in the Lecture 1 essay (§7), where the counter-evidence (the *regula fidei* attested across provinces by c. 180–230; creedal formulae within the New Testament) is set out; that assessment applies here unchanged, with this addition: the vocabulary of exclusion is not a late invention but apostolic — "heresies" (*haireseis*) among the works of the flesh (Galatians 5:20; cf. Titus 3:10, "a man that is an heretick... reject"; 2 Peter 2:1, "damnable heresies"), and doctrinal exclusion practiced in the first generation (1 Timothy 1:19–20; 2 John 10; Revelation 2:2, 14–16, 20). The killing claim is anachronistic for the period described: in the second and third centuries the proto-orthodox possessed no coercive power — they were themselves the intermittently persecuted party — and the first known execution of a Christian for heresy by Christian instigation is Priscillian of Avila in 385, half a century after Constantine; the burning of dissident books likewise belongs to the imperial period (beginning with Constantine's edict against Arius's writings). Applied to the pre-Nicene centuries the charge is unsupported; applied to the post-Constantinian church it is documented. The saved-in-ignorance characterization is an overdrawn description of late-medieval sacramental practice retrojected onto the early period; no second- or third-century source teaches salvation in ignorance — the catechumenate of that era (attested in Justin, *First Apology* 61, and Hippolytus's *Apostolic Tradition*) required extended instruction *before* baptism.

### 17. Nicaea, homoousios, and "the birth of the Trinity"

**Claims as delivered.** The hotly debated topic was the nature of God; Constantine, having adopted Christianity, called the council with no idea of the debate's depth; the dispute was between Eusebius teaching *homoiousios* (similar substance) and Athanasius teaching *homoousios* (same substance); the council claimed no revelation or inspiration but only voted, adopting Athanasius's definition and giving birth to the Trinity, which became the litmus test of orthodoxy ever since; the council got it wrong.

**Historical assessment.** The narrative contains a cluster of documented inaccuracies alongside its accurate frame. Accurate: Constantine convened the council (325); he initially underestimated the gravity of the dispute (his letter urging Alexander and Arius to reconcile over what he called a trifling question is preserved in Eusebius, *Life of Constantine* 2.64–72); the council's decision was reached by deliberation and subscription — a vote in that sense — and *homoousios* was adopted; Trinitarian confession thereafter functioned as the boundary of orthodoxy. Inaccurate or garbled: (a) The dispute at Nicaea was not *homoiousios* versus *homoousios*. It was the Arian question — whether the Son is a creature, "there was when he was not" — against the Son's full deity. *Homoiousios* as a party formula belongs to the *post*-Nicene controversies of the 350s (Basil of Ancyra's party), a generation later; reading it back into 325 conflates two stages of the controversy. (b) The party assignments are wrong on both sides: Eusebius of Caesarea was not the champion of an opposing formula at the council — he proposed his baptismal creed and subscribed to *homoousios* (with explanatory reservations recorded in his letter to his church); and Athanasius attended only as a deacon in Alexander of Alexandria's retinue — the formula's conciliar sponsors were Alexander and Hosius of Cordova with the emperor's backing; Athanasius became its great *defender* in the following decades. (c) "Giving birth to the Trinity" is inaccurate as history of doctrine: the term and doctrine long predate the council — *trinitas* is in Tertullian (*Against Praxeas*, c. 213) and the Greek *trias* in Theophilus of Antioch (c. 180), and triadic baptismal confession is commanded in Matthew 28:19 and attested in the Didache 7 and Justin, *First Apology* 61; the creed of 325 itself contains only a single clause on the Holy Ghost, the fuller article coming at Constantinople in 381. Nicaea defined the Son's consubstantiality; it did not originate Trinitarian faith. (d) "Did not claim to have revelation or inspiration" is true in the sense that no new revelation was claimed, but the council understood itself on the model of Acts 15:28 ("it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us") — guided judgment on the deposit already delivered, which is the category the lecture's framing omits.

**Biblical assessment.** The lecture's biblical case against Nicaea is assessed at §20 below, where it is shown to engage a position (identity of the persons) that Nicaea did not assert.

### 18. The Florida development and the three-percent claim

**Claims as delivered.** The LDS Church is undertaking approximately a trillion-dollar real-estate development in Florida, building infrastructure to house over half a million people near Disney World on what was a 133,000-acre cattle ranch; that church owns about three percent of the State of Florida.

**Historical assessment.** The underlying project is real and the details are traceable: the church's Deseret Ranches submitted the North Ranch Sector Plan (approved 2015) covering roughly 133,000 acres in Osceola County, with build-out projections on the order of half a million residents by the 2080s. Two figures do not survive checking. (a) No documented valuation supports "approximately a trillion dollars"; contemporary reporting described a multi-decade master plan without any such price, and a trillion-dollar figure — on the order of the annual GDP of Florida at the time — appears to be rhetorical rather than sourced. (b) "About 3% of the state": Florida comprises roughly 34.7 million acres; Deseret Ranches proper is approximately 290,000 acres (about 0.8%); including the church's 2013–14 AgReserves purchase of approximately 383,000 acres of former St. Joe Company timberland in the panhandle, total identified church holdings at the time of the lecture were on the order of 670,000 acres — just under 2% of the state, and the largest private landholding in Florida. The claim's direction is right (very large holdings); the stated figure overstates the documented acreage by roughly half.

### 19. Joseph Smith rejected and killed by Mormons

**Claim as delivered.** Mormons were responsible for persecuting, rejecting, and ultimately killing Joseph Smith; the LDS Church's claims to authority are hollow; Joseph never finished his work and his last year shows a direction different from where the Mormon churches arrived.

**Historical assessment.** This is a stronger form of the causation claim assessed in the Lecture 1 essay (§12), and the documented record given there governs: dissenting former followers precipitated the crisis (the *Nauvoo Expositor*, June 1844), but the men who stormed Carthage jail and killed Smith were an anti-Mormon mob drawn substantially from the Warsaw militia, and the five defendants tried in 1845 were non-Mormons. "Mormons... ultimately killing him" is not sustained by the record; "Mormon dissenters set the chain of events in motion" is. The claims about the LDS Church's subsequent fidelity and about the trajectory of Smith's final year are the contested theses of the lecturer's own historiography (*Passing the Heavenly Gift*) and of its critics; they are interpretive positions, not established facts, and are noted here as such without adjudication, since adjudicating intra-Mormon historiography exceeds the two axes of this assessment.

## III. Claims of Biblical Doctrine

### 20. The nature of God: separate persons, the oneness of John 17, Elohim, and "gods many"

**Claims as delivered.** The Gospels and common sense present Jesus as a mortal man whose Father, a separate being, spoke from heaven at the baptism and the Transfiguration; nothing in the New Testament makes Christ and His Father the same personage; even "I and my Father are one" is explained by John 17, where the disciples are to be one "as we are"; the earliest Christians saw nothing improper in the Father and Son being separate and distinct; the Old Testament begins with plural gods — *Elohim*, the plural of *El*, and "let us make man"; Paul contemplated gods many and lords many (1 Corinthians 8:5–6); the Trinity adopted at Nicaea was wrong; John 17:3 defines eternal life as knowing both the only true God and Jesus Christ whom He sent, which separates the Father from the Son.

**Biblical assessment.** The citations are accurate as citations: the Father speaks as distinct from the Son at the baptism and Transfiguration (Matthew 3:17; 17:5); John 17:11, 21–23 does pray that the disciples be one "as we are one"; John 17:3 reads as quoted; 1 Corinthians 8:5–6 reads as quoted; *elohim* is morphologically plural; Genesis 1:26 says "let us make man."

The controlling finding, however, is a category error in the argument's target. The proposition defended throughout — that the Father and the Son are *distinct persons*, not "the same personage" — is not the point at issue between the lecture and Nicaea, because Nicene Trinitarianism *asserts* the distinction of the persons. The doctrine that Father and Son are one personage is **modalism (Sabellianism)**, and it was condemned as heresy by the same tradition that produced Nicaea (Tertullian's *Against Praxeas* is a sustained pre-Nicene refutation of it; Sabellius was excommunicated c. 220). Every text the lecture adduces for the distinctness of the persons — the baptismal voice, the Transfiguration, John 17 — is affirmed by, and indeed constitutive of, Trinitarian doctrine, which confesses one divine essence in three distinct persons. Demonstrating that the Father and Son are distinct persons therefore refutes Sabellius, not Athanasius. What would engage the Nicene claim is the question of the Son's *nature* — creature or consubstantial deity — and on that question the Johannine corpus the lecture relies upon supplies the pro-Nicene evidence: "the Word was God" (John 1:1); "I and my Father are one" (John 10:30, provoking the charge "thou, being a man, makest thyself God," 10:33); "before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58); Thomas's confession "My Lord and my God" received without correction (John 20:28).

On the supporting exegesis: (a) *Elohim.* The noun is morphologically plural, but when it denotes the God of Israel it governs *singular* verbs — including in the very first verse cited: *bara* ("created," Genesis 1:1) is singular. Hebrew grammarians standardly analyze this as an intensive or majestic plural; "the Old Testament begins with plural gods" is not a tenable reading of a text whose verb is singular and whose canonical frame is "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD" (Deuteronomy 6:4). The "let us" of Genesis 1:26 is variously taken (divine deliberation, the heavenly council, an adumbration of plurality within the one God); it will not carry a plurality of *gods* against the explicit denials of the same canon: "before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me" (Isaiah 43:10); "Is there a God beside me? yea, there is no God; I know not any" (Isaiah 44:8). (b) *1 Corinthians 8:5–6.* The passage is quoted accurately but against its argument: Paul's "gods many, and lords many" describes the pagan pantheon as *"called* gods," in a context whose premise is "an idol is nothing in the world, and... there is none other God but one" (8:4); verse 6 then assigns the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ jointly to the one-God side of the ledger — a formulation widely recognized as Paul's christological expansion of the Shema. The text is evidence against, not for, a populated heaven of gods. (c) *John 17:3.* Knowing both the Father and the Son is equally required by Trinitarian confession; the verse distinguishes the persons (as does Nicaea) and does not adjudicate the nature question. The verse's "only true God" language, pressed as subordinating the Son, must be read with John 1:1 and 20:28 in the same corpus.

**Finding.** The argument's biblical materials establish personal distinction, which is common ground with the doctrine it opposes; the materials offered for a plurality of gods are contradicted by the grammar and context of the passages cited and by the explicit monotheism of Isaiah and Deuteronomy; the Nicene question (the Son's nature) is not engaged by the texts marshalled, while the Johannine texts bearing on it run against the lecture's position.

### 21. "A change to his religion suggests God was no longer in charge"; the apostasia

**Claims as delivered.** Christianity changed over the first two centuries; since God is the same yesterday, today, and forever, change to His religion signals that God was no longer in charge; during this time an *apostasia* — rebellion — took place; apostasy implies a sudden event and deliberate rebellion.

**Biblical assessment.** The unchanging-God-therefore-unchanging-forms argument was assessed in the Lecture 1 essay (§14): Hebrews itself distinguishes the modes of God's dealing across eras (Hebrews 1:1–2), and the constancy of God's character (Malachi 3:6; Hebrews 13:8) does not entail the constancy of administration — the same canon records God changing covenant administration at Sinai and again in Christ (Hebrews 8:13). The lexical claim is accurate — *apostasia* (2 Thessalonians 2:3) denotes defection or rebellion — but the gloss "implies a sudden event" is not a property of the word, and it stands in tension with the lecture's own narrative, which describes a *gradual* transformation across two centuries ("Christianity changed over the first two centuries"; converts "unacquainted with the original beliefs" arriving over generations). The New Testament predicts defection and corruption within the church (Acts 20:29–30; 2 Timothy 4:3–4; 2 Thessalonians 2:3) and simultaneously promises the church's preservation (Matthew 16:18; 28:20; Ephesians 3:21); the total-apostasy reading takes the first set as exhaustive and the second as void, which is a selection the texts themselves do not authorize. This finding matches Lecture 1 assessment §15.

### 22. A new dispensation, new scriptures, and the "A Bible! A Bible!" passage

**Claims as delivered.** A new dispensation began with Joseph Smith in 1820 and continues today; more revelations and more scripture have been given; a new three-volume edition of scriptures (Old Covenants; New Covenants; Teachings and Commandments) is in preparation; the Book of Mormon foretold that the Gentiles would reject new scripture, saying "A Bible! A Bible! We have got a Bible"; God's work is not finished and He has caused more to be written.

**Assessment.** The publication claims are accurate as description of the movement's own project (the Restoration Edition scriptures were subsequently published as described). The passage read is **2 Nephi 29:3–10**, identified to the audience as Book of Mormon — attribution noted. The doctrinal question — whether continuing divine activity entails continuing canon-bearing revelation — was assessed in the Lecture 1 essay (§22), and that finding governs: the continuation of God's speech and works is biblically affirmed, while every claimant to new canonical revelation is subject to the content tests of the once-delivered faith (Jude 3; Galatians 1:8–9; Deuteronomy 13:1–3; 1 John 4:1; Acts 17:11); the 2 Nephi 29 passage answers the *psychological* objection ("we have enough") but not the *criterial* one (how a new claimant is to be tested), which remains where Scripture places it — on conformity of content. It may be added that the biblical texts governing addition are not about the physical Bible's completeness but about fidelity: "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you" (Deuteronomy 4:2); "Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar" (Proverbs 30:6). These do not settle canon closure by themselves, but they place the burden of proof on the addition, not on the hearer's willingness.

## IV. Claims in the Corrupted Portion Recoverable with Confidence

### 23. The rich young ruler

**Claims as delivered (recoverable).** Luke calls him "a certain ruler," Matthew "the young man," Mark "one who came running"; John does not mention the incident; had he obeyed, he would have accompanied Christ through the final two weeks — the triumphal entry, the temple denunciations, the anointing, the supper, the resurrection appearances; faith requires sacrifice, which is what the young man declined.

**Biblical assessment.** The synoptic details are accurate: "a certain ruler" (Luke 18:18); "the young man" (Matthew 19:20, 22); "there came one running" (Mark 10:17); the account is absent from John. The placement of the incident on the final approach to Jerusalem is consistent with all three synoptic settings (each is followed shortly by the passion prediction and the Jericho approach), so the two-week counterfactual is chronologically plausible as homiletic reconstruction, though the "final two weeks" figure is an inference, not a datum. The general principle that faith is demonstrated and perfected in sacrificial obedience is biblically consistent (Hebrews 11:8, 17; James 2:21–22; Luke 14:33). The stronger formulation delivered in this series — that it is "the order of heaven" that sacrifice is the necessary condition of faith — is the signature doctrine of the sixth of the *Lectures on Faith* (1835), a Restoration source; the biblical texts support sacrifice as faith's fruit and proof, which is adjacent to, but not identical with, the Lectures' necessary-condition formulation.

### 24. Sabbath on the first day

**Claims as delivered (recoverable).** In answer to a Seventh-day Adventist's question: the speaker keeps the Sabbath on the first day of the week; the rationale offered involves the disturbance of the original creation calendar at the Fall and Christ's resurrection repairing the loss, so that the day of resurrection is properly observed.

**Assessment.** The *practice* has first-century and sub-apostolic attestation: assembly on the first day of the week (Acts 20:7; 1 Corinthians 16:2), "the Lord's day" (Revelation 1:10), and explicitly Sunday in Didache 14 and Justin, *First Apology* 67. The *rationale* delivered — an Adamic calendar disturbed at the expulsion and repaired by the resurrection's timing — has no attestation in Scripture or in any early Christian source known to this assessment; the early sources ground first-day observance directly and simply in the resurrection (Justin, *First Apology* 67) and in creation's first day of light. The practice is historically continuous; the offered explanation is idiosyncratic and unattested.

### 25. "Those who demand their authority be respected are antichrist"

**Claim as delivered.** Christ came as a servant, invited rather than demanded, and submitted to abuse; those who demand that their authority be respected are antichrist, because they oppose the glory of Christ's example.

**Biblical assessment.** The servant-leadership content is soundly biblical (Matthew 20:25–28; John 13:13–15; Philippians 2:5–8; 1 Peter 5:3, "neither as being lords over God's heritage"). The application of the term *antichrist*, however, departs from its biblical definition: John, who alone uses the word, defines antichrist by christological denial — "he that denieth that Jesus is the Christ... that denieth the Father and the Son" (1 John 2:22); "every spirit that confesseth not that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh" (1 John 4:3; 2 John 7) — not by the demanding of deference. Authority-demanding is condemned in Scripture under other heads (Diotrephes, "who loveth to have the preeminence," 3 John 9; Matthew 23:6–12); labeling it *antichrist* substitutes a rhetorical for the lexical-biblical sense. An internal observation belongs to the record: the same lecture states that the speaker's fellowship possesses "authority to baptize" which it will "share" with those who accept its practice — a claim of exclusive-but-shareable ordinance authority that sits uneasily beside the principle as formulated.

### 26. The purpose of prophecy

**Claims as delivered (recoverable).** Prophecy is not given to enable prediction for advantage (markets, timing); its purpose is to ratify God's foreknowledge once the event occurs — either a date without an intelligible description, or a description without a date; the speaker states he was inspired to write a description of a future event.

**Biblical assessment.** The ratification function is genuinely biblical: "And now I have told you before it come to pass, that, when it is come to pass, ye might believe" (John 14:29; likewise 13:19; Isaiah 46:9–10; 48:5). It is not, however, the whole of the biblical account: prophecy also warns to present repentance (Jonah 3:4–10; Revelation 2–3) and its verification serves as a public *test of the prophet* — "when the word of the prophet shall come to pass, then shall the prophet be known" (Jeremiah 28:9; Deuteronomy 18:22). The speaker's claim to have written an inspired description of a future event is, like the vision claim assessed in the Lecture 1 essay (§21), not historically checkable; Scripture assigns it the tests just named, together with the content tests of Galatians 1:8–9 and Deuteronomy 13:1–3, under which predictive success alone is insufficient.

### 27. Mutual acceptance among Christians

**Claim as delivered (recoverable).** Christians ought not dismiss, belittle, or discard one another's Christianity; if one thinks he can improve another's understanding, he should assume the burden of meek persuasion; doctrinal killing among early Christians was nonsense to be guarded against.

**Biblical assessment.** Consistent with the biblical instruction on the manner of correction: "in meekness instructing those that oppose themselves" (2 Timothy 2:24–25); "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15); "with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15); Romans 14 on receiving without doubtful disputation. Scripture pairs this manner with the duty of doctrinal discernment (Titus 3:10; 2 John 10; Jude 3), which the lecture's own practice of extended doctrinal critique presupposes. No inconsistency is recorded on this point.

## V. Summary of Findings

Assessed on historical accuracy: the following are accurate or substantially accurate — the sustaining-power and baptism citations as citations (§§1–2), the Marcion datum and fourth-century canon settlement (§4c in part), the monopoly characterization of 1517 Latin Christendom with the stated qualifications (§6), Dolan's appreciative anniversary engagement in substance (§7), the Hus/press/bestseller cluster with the stated imprecision (§8), the Justin Martyr worship description (§12), the Syriac and Thomas traditions as attested traditions (§13), the Origen preexistence attribution with its qualifications (§14), the existence and thesis of Ehrman's monograph (§15), Constantine's convening and underestimate of the controversy (§17), the North Ranch project's existence and acreage (§18), the rich-young-ruler synoptic details (§23), and first-day observance as early practice (§24).

The following are inaccurate or materially overstated: Saul "renamed" Paul at conversion (§2); grace-alone and faith-alone as excluding baptism and sanctification — misdescriptions of the doctrines opposed, contradicted by the Lutheran confessional documents themselves (§§2–3); Luther translating from Latin (§4a); the illiteracy inference from sola scriptura (§4b); "only the Old Testament was used or cited" in original Christianity, against 2 Peter 3:15–16 and 1 Timothy 5:18 (§4c); "restoration was not even attempted" before 1820, against the Radical Reformation's documented restitutionism (§9); the first-century church as without organization, office, or central decision, against Acts 15, Philippians 1:1, and the Pastorals (§10); leadership located in *diakonoi* (§10); the no-supported-ministry claim, as previously found (§11); the leaderless use of Justin against Justin's own president and collection (§12); the Johannine premortality attribution and the lost-divinization claim (§13); the generalized baptismal-voice corruption claim, its minority text-critical status presented as fact, the central-editor mechanism, and the unqualified Pauline authorship of Hebrews (§15); pre-Nicene killing of heretics (§16); the homoiousios/homoousios framing of Nicaea, the party assignments to Eusebius and Athanasius, and "the birth of the Trinity" at Nicaea (§17); the trillion-dollar figure and the three-percent Florida figure (§18); and "Mormons... ultimately killing" Joseph Smith (§19).

Assessed on biblical consistency: consistent — the sustaining-power and perfect-judgment doctrines (§1), the baptismal catena as evidence of baptism's commanded place (§2), the sanctification catena (§3), servant leadership (§25), the ratification function of prophecy (§26), and mutual meekness among Christians (§27). Inconsistent or unsupported — the premortal existence of human spirits and the veil of forgetfulness (§§13–14); the plurality-of-gods exegesis of Elohim and 1 Corinthians 8, against the singular grammar, Deuteronomy 6:4, and Isaiah 43–44 (§20); the separate-persons argument as an argument against Nicaea, found to be a category error engaging modalism rather than Trinitarianism (§20); the change-proves-abandonment premise, as previously found (§21); the antichrist definition (§25); and the exclusivity clause of the doctrine-of-Christ passage as deployed, per the Lecture 1 finding (§5).

As a matter of source criticism, this lecture reads Restoration scripture to the audience three times: Mosiah 2:21, 25 unattributed as "another prophet" (§1), 3 Nephi 11:31–40 unattributed as the doctrine of Christ (§5), and — in a departure from the pattern — Alma 1:26–27 and 2 Nephi 29:3–10 with attribution (§§11, 22). The lecture's structural argument runs: the solas were not original Christianity (§§2–4), original Christianity was non-institutional and diverse (§§10, 13), the text and creed were corrupted by the emerging institution (§§15–17), the Reformation never attempted restoration (§9), therefore restoration through Joseph Smith and a continuing dispensation (§§19, 22). The assessment finds the first premise to rest on misdescriptions of the Reformation doctrines, the second to be partially accurate but contradicted at its strong form by Acts 15 and the Pastorals, the third to overstate a minority text-critical case and to misdate both the parties and the vocabulary of Nicaea, and the fourth to be falsified by the Radical Reformation; the conclusion inherits the defects of the premises.
