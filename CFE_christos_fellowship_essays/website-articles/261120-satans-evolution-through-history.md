# 261120 - Satan's Evolution Through History

# Did Satan Evolve?


### A response to the reel on the history of Satan (Religion for Breakfast)

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** · 20 November 2026

I recently watched a[ reel by Dr. Andrew Mark Henry](https://www.msn.com/en-us/news/other/satan-wasn-t-always-the-devil-then-religion-turned-him-into-evil-itself/vi-AA22U6r1?uxmode=ruby&ctsrc=dgst&ocid=edgntpruby&pc=BINBAN&cvid=6afdc70126624ab7a7b4ba89c7c467fd&ei=62), a scholar of religion who runs the YouTube channel *Religion for Breakfast*. It opens with a clip from *O Brother, Where Art Thou?* in which a character describes Satan as red, scaly, fork-tailed, and carrying a hay fork. Henry rightly sets that cartoon aside and turns to his real subject: the idea that Satan is a fallen angel, the ruler of demons, the chief tempter, and the archenemy of God. His thesis is that this idea "has a history" — that it was not present in ancient Israel, grew during and after the Babylonian exile under Persian influence, matured in the Jewish writings of the centuries before Christ, and arrived in the New Testament already formed.

Much of his history is sound. But I stand on the side of the conclusion he leaves open: Satan is a real, personal, created being who rebelled against God, and the Bible's growing clarity about him over the centuries is the disclosure of a reality, not the invention of a character. Below I lay out what the reel gets right, where its history needs correcting, what it leaves out, and why I hold the position I hold. I also name the places where the evidence is uncertain.

## What the reel argues

Henry builds his case in five steps.


 	- **The Hebrew word *satan* means "adversary" or "accuser."** In the older parts of the Hebrew Bible it describes ordinary human opponents. He cites 1 Samuel 29:4, where the Philistine commanders worry that David may become their *satan* — their adversary — in battle.

 	- **Later texts use it as a job title.** In Job and Zechariah, "the satan" is a member of God's heavenly court who acts like a prosecutor. He is not a prince of demons, not a fallen angel, and not God's enemy.

 	- **The Persians changed the conversation.** After Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, the Judean elite lived under Babylonian and then Persian rule. Zoroastrianism, the Persian religion, taught a cosmic war between a good god (Ahura Mazda) and an evil spirit (Angra Mainyu). Henry cites the scholar Albert de Jong for evidence of Zoroastrian influence on later Jewish writings, and he notes that the demon Asmodeus in the Book of Tobit takes his name from a Persian demon.

 	- **The Jewish writings between the Testaments filled in the picture.** The Dead Sea Scrolls describe a Spirit of Light and an Angel of Darkness, and a final war in which Michael leads the Sons of Light against Belial and the Sons of Darkness. First Enoch tells of rebel angels led by Shemihazah or Asael who fell from heaven. Jubilees has Mastema, a chief of evil spirits who is called "the satan."

 	- **The New Testament inherited this world.** Jesus' followers spoke of Satan or the *diabolos* (the Greek word from which we get "devil") as tempter, ruler of demons, and cause of sickness and death. Christians then read that figure back into the Old Testament — making the serpent of Eden into Satan, and making the fallen "day star" of Isaiah 14 into Lucifer, though Isaiah was describing the king of Babylon.


He closes by saying his aim was "not to debunk Christian theology" but to teach us to think historically, using the "five C's" taught to history students: change over time, context, causality, complexity, and contingency.

## What the reel gets right

The following are facts:


 	- The red, horned, pitchfork-carrying devil is a product of medieval and Renaissance art, not of Scripture.

 	- *Satan* is a Hebrew common noun meaning adversary, and it is used of human beings.

 	- In Job 1–2, the Hebrew says *ha-satan*, "the satan" — a title, not a personal name. He appears among the heavenly beings who present themselves before the LORD.

 	- The Jewish literature of the Second Temple period (roughly 515 BC to AD 70) is full of named evil leaders: Belial, Mastema, Shemihazah, Asael. The early Christians lived in that world, and Paul even uses the name Belial: *"And what concord hath Christ with Belial?"* **(2 Corinthians 6:15)**

 	- The Greek *diabolos* is the standard translation of the Hebrew *satan*.

 	- The immediate subject of Isaiah 14 is the king of Babylon. The name "Lucifer" comes from the Latin Vulgate's rendering of the Hebrew *helel*, "shining one" or "day star."

 	- Christians do read the Old Testament through the lens of the New. I do so deliberately, and I will say why below.


The reel is also honest about the limits of its own argument. Henry says that scholars who know both the Jewish and the Zoroastrian sources well are "vanishingly rare," that "influence" is a simplistic word for a complex process, and that we cannot pin down cause and effect. I respect that candor.

## Where the history needs correcting

Several details in the reel are wrong or overstated. None of them alone overturns his thesis, but together they make the Hebrew Bible look emptier than it is.

**The word *satan* is used of a heavenly being long before Job.** The reel says the earliest uses refer only to human adversaries. But in the story of Balaam, the adversary is the angel of the LORD himself: *"and the angel of the LORD stood in the way for an adversary against him."* **(Numbers 22:22)** The Hebrew word there is *satan*. Critical scholars and traditional readers date Numbers differently, but on any dating this text is part of the Torah, and it shows a heavenly being acting as a *satan* well within the Hebrew Bible's own frame.

**The reel omits the clearest Old Testament use of Satan as a name.** First Chronicles retells David's sinful census: *"And Satan stood up against Israel, and provoked David to number Israel."* **(1 Chronicles 21:1)** Here the word appears without the article, and most translators render it as a proper name. The earlier account of the same event says the LORD "moved David" **(2 Samuel 24:1)**. The Chronicler, writing after the exile, clarifies who did the inciting while God permitted it. This supports part of Henry's timeline, since Chronicles is post-exilic. But it also shows the pattern the Bible holds all the way through, from Job to the Gospels: the adversary acts, and God permits and bounds his action. That is the biblical relation between God and Satan, not a Persian one.

**"The Satan" uses the definite article, not a direct object marker.** Henry says the Hebrew in Job marks *ha-satan* with a "direct object marker." The *ha-* is the definite article, "the." (Hebrew's direct object marker is a separate word, *et*.) His point — that "the satan" names a particular recurring figure — still stands.

**The dates are off.** The Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon in 539 BC and permitted the Jews to return about 538 BC, not 550 BC. Alexander the Great took control of Judea in 332 BC; 323 BC is the year he died.

**The date of Job is not settled.** The reel treats Job as a sixth-century text. Many scholars date it to the exile or later, but others date it earlier, and both Jewish and Christian tradition long placed its setting in the age of the patriarchs. The date matters to the reel's argument, so it should have been presented as disputed.

**The direction of Persian influence is harder to establish than the reel implies.** Most of the Zoroastrian writings that describe a fully developed war between the good and evil spirits — the Pahlavi books such as the *Bundahishn* — were written down in the ninth century AD, more than a thousand years after the exile. The oldest Zoroastrian hymns, the *Gathas*, are much earlier but say comparatively little. Scholars therefore reconstruct earlier Persian belief partly from later texts, and that makes it difficult to know who influenced whom, or whether both drew on older ideas. Henry concedes the scarcity of experts; I would add the scarcity of early sources.

Most importantly, the Hebrew Bible itself answers Persian dualism directly. Isaiah addresses Cyrus, the Persian king, by name, and then God says: *"I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things."* **(Isaiah 45:7)** This is a deliberate refusal of a universe ruled by two rival powers. There is one God, and everything else — including whatever darkness exists — is under His authority. The Dead Sea Scrolls the reel quotes keep the same line: the Community Rule says the God of Israel *created* the spirits of light and darkness. In Zoroastrianism, Angra Mainyu is an independent rival. In Scripture, Satan is a creature, never God's equal, and always on a leash. Whatever Jewish thinkers learned from their Persian neighbors, they did not adopt the core of Persian dualism. That is the decisive difference, and the reel passes over it.

## What the Hebrew Bible already holds

The reel says the notion of a supreme evil opponent of God is "completely absent" from the Hebrew Bible. I would say the Old Testament is reticent about the adversary, not silent. Several threads run through it that the reel does not mention.


 	- **The serpent of Eden** speaks against God's word with intelligence and intent: *"Ye shall not surely die: For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil."* **(Genesis 3:4-5)** God then announces a long war between the serpent's seed and the woman's seed **(Genesis 3:15)**. A talking animal that lies about God and is cursed into a multi-generational conflict is not an ordinary snake, even read on its own terms.

 	- **Azazel.** On the Day of Atonement, Aaron casts lots over two goats: *"one lot for the LORD, and the other lot for the scapegoat."* **(Leviticus 16:8)** The Hebrew for "scapegoat" is *la-azazel*, "for Azazel" — apparently a figure in the wilderness set over against the LORD. First Enoch's rebel angel Asael (also spelled Azazel) builds on this text; it did not come from nowhere.

 	- **Demons.** Moses rebukes Israel because *"They sacrificed unto devils, not to God; to gods whom they knew not"* **(Deuteronomy 32:17)**. The Hebrew word is *shedim*, spirits. Psalm 106:37 repeats the charge.

 	- **Hostile spiritual powers over nations, with Michael opposing them.** An angel tells Daniel, *"But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me one and twenty days: but, lo, Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me"* **(Daniel 10:13)**. Daniel also names the "prince of Grecia" **(Daniel 10:20)**. The War Scroll's picture of Michael leading the forces of light against the forces of darkness has an obvious source inside the Hebrew Bible.


These passages do not give a full doctrine of Satan. But they show that the elements the Second Temple writers drew together — a deceiver at the beginning, a figure in opposition to the LORD, spirits behind false worship, and an angelic war over the nations — were already present in Israel's Scriptures.

## The question under the question

The reel's real question is not about dates. It is this: if our idea of Satan changed over time, does that mean Satan is an idea and not a being?

I do not think it follows, and my own field of physics shows why. The idea of the atom has a history of about 2,400 years. Democritus imagined indivisible particles. Dalton gave them chemical weights. Thomson found the electron inside them, Rutherford found the nucleus, Bohr gave the electrons orbits, and quantum theory replaced the orbits with fields of probability. Every one of the five C's applies to that history. Yet no physicist concludes that atoms evolved into existence when our concept of them improved. The concept changed because the reality was slowly being disclosed. Or consider Neptune. In 1846 Urbain Le Verrier calculated where an unseen planet had to be in order to explain the wobbles in the orbit of Uranus, and astronomers found it where he said. People had seen the effects before they could name the cause.

I read the Second Temple writers that way. They were noticing the wobbles — the organized, intelligent, persistent character of evil — and trying to name the cause. Some of their names and stories were speculative, and the church did not accept First Enoch or Jubilees as Scripture (with the exception of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church). But the New Testament did not simply inherit their speculation. It confirmed the reality behind it and cut away much of the mythology around it.

Scripture describes itself as exactly this kind of gradual disclosure: *"God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son"* **(Hebrews 1:1-2)**. Historians call it "change over time"; theologians call it "progressive revelation." Both describe the same evidence. The difference lies in what one believes about the source.

This is the limit of the historical method, and it should be stated plainly. The five C's are good tools for describing how human beings thought. By their own rules they cannot tell us whether what people thought about was real. Henry says his aim was not to debunk, and I take him at his word. But many viewers will leave the reel believing that Satan was invented, and the method he used cannot establish that.

## Why Jesus settles it for me

The strongest witness to Satan's reality is not Enoch or the Dead Sea Scrolls. It is Jesus. He did not speak of Satan as a figure of speech. He addressed him directly in the wilderness **(Matthew 4:1-11)**. He said, *"I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven."* **(Luke 10:18)** And He described Satan's character more precisely than any Second Temple writer: *"He was a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth, because there is no truth in him.** When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it."* **(John 8:44)**

If Satan is only a symbol of evil, then Jesus was either mistaken about the spiritual world or knowingly spoke in the categories of a myth. Neither fits the One I believe to be the Creator who entered His own creation. The apostles followed Him: *"For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places."* **(Ephesians 6:12)** And Revelation makes the link the reel attributes to later Christian interpretation: *"that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world"* **(Revelation 12:9)**. Identifying the serpent of Eden with Satan was not a medieval innovation. It is in the New Testament itself.

This is also why I read the Old Testament through the New. If Jesus is who He said He is, His reading of Israel's Scriptures is the authoritative one. The historian brackets that question; the believer cannot.

## Why God permits an adversary

If Satan is real, the harder question is why God allows him to exist and to act. Here is how I understand it.

God created everything good, Lucifer included. Ezekiel's lament over the king of Tyre reaches beyond any human king: *"Thou art the anointed cherub that covereth ... **Thou wast perfect in thy ways from the day that thou wast created, till iniquity was found in thee."* **(Ezekiel 28:14-15)** That seems to contradict Jesus' statement that Satan was a murderer "from the beginning." I resolve it this way: the "beginning" of Satan is the moment Lucifer chose to make himself like the Most High. God creates possibilities, including the possibility of refusal. The creature who chooses refusal is the one who makes evil actual. God is the author of the option, not of the choice.

Why create beings who could refuse? Because God is love, and love cannot be satisfied by a compelled response. Love needs others who are free — genuinely capable of saying no. And a real "no" requires real access to the alternative. So God did not annihilate the angels who rebelled. He set them apart — their existence continued, but their fellowship with Him ended. The creation we live in is the region the Son made and holds, where free beings can stand close enough to evil to choose against it, and where the adversary is permitted to act within limits. Job shows those limits: *"Behold, all that he hath is in thy power; only upon himself put not forth thine hand."* **(Job 1:12)**

There is one more piece, and it is central to everything I teach. God does not watch our lives from outside. He lives them with us, from inside. Every temptation the accuser presses on a man, God feels in that man. *"In all their affliction he was afflicted"* **(Isaiah 63:9)**. So when the adversary accuses us before God, he accuses us before One who knows our weakness from within: *"For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities"* **(Hebrews 4:15)**. That is why the accuser's case fails at the Cross. The One who felt every sin from inside took its liability on Himself, and the accusation found nothing in Him to hold.

## Why the concern remains plausible

Henry cites a 2009 Barna survey in which about 40 percent of American Christians strongly agreed that Satan is not a living being but a symbol of evil. I take that as a warning, and I think the warning is reasonable, for several reasons.

**Evil looks organized.** Ordinary human selfishness explains a great deal, but it struggles to explain evil that is patient, strategic, and coordinated across generations and cultures — the way cruelty seems to find the most vulnerable, the way lies are tailored to each culture's weak point, the way whole societies are drawn step by step into what they once found unthinkable. That is the "wobble" the Second Temple writers saw, and it has not gone away.

**Invisibility is the adversary's best strategy.** Scripture's signature description of Satan is deception: he "deceiveth the whole world" **(Revelation 12:9)**, and *"Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light"* **(2 Corinthians 11:14)**. If that is true, the most useful thing for him would be for people to think of him as a red cartoon with a pitchfork, or as a metaphor. Pope Francis made the same point in the line the reel quotes: thinking of the devil as only a symbol "would lead us to let down our guard." Peter put it more sharply: *"Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour"* **(1 Peter 5:8)**.

**Contingency does not rule out providence.** Henry rightly warns historians against teleology — writing history as though it were bound to arrive at the present. As a method for historians, that is good discipline. But the believer sees in the same string of contingent events — Babylon's conquest, the exile, Cyrus's decree, the return — the hand of a God who works through human choices without overriding them: *"But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good"* **(Genesis 50:20)**. The historian and the believer are looking at the same events. One is describing them; the other is interpreting them.

**The areas of real uncertainty.** I do not claim to know how much the Persian encounter shaped Jewish vocabulary about evil, and I think some influence on names and imagery is likely (Asmodeus is the clearest example). I do not claim that every Christian reading of Isaiah 14 or Ezekiel 28 was the prophets' conscious intent; I read them as typology — a human king's pride as the earthly image of a heavenly rebellion — on the authority of Jesus' and the apostles' teaching. And I grant that the dating of Job, Numbers, and Leviticus is disputed. None of these uncertainties touches the central claim, which rests on the testimony of Jesus.

## Sources and their limits

This essay leans on the standard scholarly record: the biblical text (quoted from the King James Version), the reel itself, and the usual reference works on the Dead Sea Scrolls, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and Zoroastrian literature. That record is curated by an academic field whose method, by design, sets aside the question of whether spiritual beings exist. Scholarship within that field can describe the history of an idea well, but it is not built to weigh evidence for the reality behind the idea, and a reader should not expect it to. The dating of the biblical books and of the Zoroastrian sources is disputed, and I have tried to mark where it matters. The Barna figure is the one the reel cites; I have not independently re-examined that survey's wording. Firsthand Christian testimony about spiritual oppression and deliverance, which I have not drawn on here, is a further body of evidence the academic record largely excludes.
